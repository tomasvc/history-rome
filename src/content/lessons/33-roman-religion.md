## Introduction {#context}

To a Roman, religion (*[[term:religio]]*) meant the scrupulous observance of obligations towards the gods. The gods were powerful beings with
whom the community and its members had relationships of exchange: humans offered sacrifice, prayer and vows; gods granted favour. The goal was
the "peace of the gods" (*[[term:pax-deorum]]*), on which the welfare of the city depended. Correct practice, rather than belief or doctrine,
was what counted. This lesson explores that system and how it changed.

:::check rr-1
:::

## Gods and temples {#gods}

Rome's gods included the great deities of the Capitol, Jupiter Best and Greatest, Juno and Minerva, whose temple was the centre of state
religion; Mars, father of Romulus; Vesta, guardian of the hearth; and a multitude of others, many identified with Greek gods. There were gods
of particular places, activities and moments, and personified qualities such as Concord, Victory, Faith and Fortune, who received temples.
Romans added gods through vows in battle, through the "evocation" of an enemy city's gods, and on the advice of the Sibylline books.

A temple was the house of a god, not a congregational building. Worship happened at the altar in front of it, in public view.

## Priests and divination {#priests}

Rome had no separate priestly caste. The main priesthoods were held by members of the political elite, often for life: the pontiffs, headed by
the *pontifex maximus*, supervised sacred law and the calendar; the augurs interpreted signs; the board of fifteen kept the Sibylline books;
the *flamines* served individual gods. The six Vestal Virgins, chosen as girls, served for thirty years, keeping the sacred fire; a Vestal who
broke her vow of chastity was buried alive.

:::check rr-2
:::

Romans constantly sought to know the gods' will. Magistrates "took the auspices" before public actions, watching the flight of birds or the
feeding of sacred chickens; *haruspices*, experts in the Etruscan discipline, read the entrails of sacrificial animals and interpreted
prodigies such as lightning strikes or monstrous births, which were reported to the Senate and expiated by ritual. Cicero, himself an augur,
wrote a dialogue *On Divination* in which his brother defends divination and Cicero criticises it, showing how educated Romans could debate
religious practice while continuing to perform it.

## Sacrifice and festivals {#ritual}

Animal sacrifice was the central act of Roman cult. The animal was led to the altar, sprinkled with wine and salted grain, killed and
butchered; its entrails were inspected and burned for the gods, and the meat was often eaten by the participants. Bloodless offerings of
cakes, wine, incense and first fruits were also common. The calendar was filled with festivals: the Lupercalia, the Saturnalia, the Parilia
(the city's birthday), games in honour of the gods, and annual sacrifices for the safety of the state.

## A sacrifice, step by step {#sacrifice-steps}

Relief sculptures and inscribed records allow a reconstruction of a public animal sacrifice. The most detailed written records come from the Arval Brothers, a priestly college whose minutes were inscribed on stone at their grove outside Rome over more than two centuries.

1. **Procession.** The victim, chosen for its species, sex and colour to suit the god (white animals for the gods above, dark ones for the gods below), was led to the altar decorated with ribbons. A struggling animal was a bad omen.
2. **Preliminary offering.** The presiding magistrate or priest, his toga drawn over his head, offered incense and wine at a portable altar, while a flute-player played to drown out ill-omened sounds.
3. **Consecration.** The victim was sprinkled with salted grain (*mola salsa*) and wine, and a knife passed along its back.
4. **Killing.** Attendants stunned and killed the animal; its blood was caught.
5. **Inspection.** The internal organs (*exta*), especially the liver, were examined. If they were abnormal, the sacrifice had to be repeated.
6. **Offering and feast.** The exta were cooked and burned on the altar for the gods; the meat was distributed, eaten at a banquet or sold.

Exactness mattered: a mistake in the words or actions could require the whole ritual to be repeated. Prayers were addressed precisely, sometimes with a formula covering the possibility that the god preferred another name.

## Religion and politics {#politics}

Religion and politics were inseparable. The Senate decided religious questions; magistrates conducted sacrifices and took auspices; priests
were senators. Religion could be used politically: a magistrate could block an assembly by announcing unfavourable omens, as Bibulus did against
Caesar in 59 BC. This does not mean that Romans were cynical. Mary Beard, John North and Simon Price argue that we should not assume that
"genuine" religion must be personal and separate from politics, an assumption derived from Christianity.{{ref:beard-north-price-1998}}

## Household and personal religion {#household}

Every household honoured its own gods: the *lares* (guardians of the household and the land), the *penates* (guardians of the store cupboard),
and the *[[term:genius]]* of the paterfamilias. Shrines in Pompeian houses show painted lares and serpents. Romans also made personal vows to
gods for health, safe travel or success, recorded on inscriptions and in votive offerings such as clay body parts left at healing sanctuaries.
Curse tablets of lead, such as those found at Bath in Britain, asked gods to punish thieves and rivals.

## Magic and curses {#curses}

Alongside public cult, many Romans sought supernatural help privately, by means that the authorities sometimes condemned as magic. Curse tablets (*defixiones*), thin sheets of lead inscribed with requests to gods or spirits and deposited in springs, graves or shrines, survive in large numbers. At the sacred spring of Sulis Minerva at Bath, about 130 have been recovered. Most ask the goddess to punish a thief: a bather whose cloak or ring has been stolen lists the suspects and asks that the culprit not sleep, or lose blood and eyes, until the property is returned to the temple. They give a rare view of the religious beliefs and the everyday worries of ordinary provincials, written in their own Latin.

:::claim established
Curse tablets show religion used for personal justice: the writers treat the goddess as a powerful patron who can be asked to recover stolen goods, often promising her a share. This is the same logic of exchange (*do ut des*, "I give so that you may give") that underlay public sacrifice.
:::

## New gods and foreign cults {#foreign}

During the war with Hannibal, in 204 BC, the Senate on the advice of the Sibylline books brought the Great Mother (Magna Mater, Cybele) from
Asia Minor; her ecstatic eunuch priests were confined and Roman citizens were at first forbidden to serve her. In 186 BC, by contrast, the
Senate suppressed the Bacchic associations.

:::source sc-bacchanalibus
:::

:::check rr-3
:::

In the empire, cults of Isis from Egypt, Mithras (a Roman cult with Persian imagery, popular among soldiers), Jupiter Dolichenus and others
spread widely. Some offered initiation into mysteries and the promise of a better afterlife. At Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, a Mithraeum, a
synagogue with painted scenes from the Hebrew Bible, a Christian house-church and temples of many gods stood within a few streets of each other
in the third century.

## The emperor and the gods {#imperial-cult}

Augustus restored temples and priesthoods and became pontifex maximus; emperors were deified after death, and in the provinces worshipped in
their lifetimes. The imperial cult gave subjects a religious language for the emperor's extraordinary power.{{ref:price-1984}}

## Christians, Jews and the limits of tolerance {#tolerance}

Roman religion was open to new gods but not to those who refused the gods of the community. Judaism was tolerated as an ancestral religion;
Christianity, which spread among gentiles and demanded exclusive worship, seemed atheistic and dangerous.

:::source pliny-christians
:::

:::source libelli-250
:::

From the fourth century, emperors moved from favouring Christianity to banning traditional sacrifice (391–392). The traditional religion
survived in the countryside and among some elites for generations, but the public cults that had defined Roman religion for a thousand years
ended. The lesson on Christianisation traces this story.

:::

## Historiography {#historiography}

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars, influenced by Protestant assumptions, often saw Roman religion as cold, formal and legalistic,
in decline by the late Republic and ripe for replacement by more "spiritual" eastern cults and Christianity. Recent scholarship treats it on its
own terms as a dynamic system, embedded in social and political life, capable of change and debate. Studies of "lived religion" (Jörg Rüpke and
others) focus on individuals' religious experience rather than state cult.{{ref:rives-2007}}

:::aside Do not ask "did they believe?"
Asking whether Romans "really believed" in their gods imposes a Christian model in which faith is the core of religion. A Roman might doubt
stories about the gods while scrupulously performing rituals, because the rituals were what the relationship with the gods required.
:::
