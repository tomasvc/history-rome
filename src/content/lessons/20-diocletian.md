## Historical context {#context}

Diocletian, born Diocles in Dalmatia to a family of humble origin, was proclaimed emperor by the army in November 284. He was one of a line
of Illyrian soldier-emperors, and nothing about his accession suggested that he would last longer than his predecessors. He ruled for
twenty-one years, abdicated voluntarily, and died in his bed in his palace at Split. He transformed the structure of the Roman state more
radically than any emperor since Augustus.

## Four emperors {#tetrarchy}

:::diagram tetrarchy
:::

Diocletian quickly recognised that one emperor could not be present on every threatened frontier, and that any absent emperor invited
usurpation by a successful general. In 285 he made his comrade Maximian Caesar and in 286 Augustus, responsible for the West. In 293 each
Augustus took a Caesar: Constantius (father of Constantine) in the West and Galerius in the East. The Caesars were adopted and married into
the families of their Augusti. The emperors took divine patrons: Diocletian and Galerius were *Iovii*, under the protection of Jupiter;
Maximian and Constantius *Herculii*, under Hercules.

:::check di-1
:::

The four rulers resided in cities closer to the frontiers: Nicomedia, Sirmium, Milan, Trier, Antioch. Rome was visited rarely; Diocletian
went there for the first time in 303. Images emphasised unity: the famous porphyry group now at St Mark's in Venice shows the rulers as
identical figures embracing.

:::source tetrarchs-porphyry
:::

:::image tetrarchs-venice
:::

The system worked militarily. Constantius recovered Britain from the usurper Carausius' successor Allectus in 296; Maximian campaigned in
Africa; Diocletian suppressed a revolt in Egypt; and Galerius, after a defeat, crushed the Persian king Narseh in 298, capturing his harem.
The peace of 298/299 gave Rome territories beyond the Tigris and a frontier that lasted until 363.

## Rebuilding the state {#reforms}

:::diagram late-administration
:::

**Provinces.** Diocletian roughly doubled the number of provinces, to about a hundred, by dividing them into smaller units. Governors could
supervise taxation and justice more closely and had fewer resources with which to rebel. The provinces were grouped into twelve dioceses
under *vicarii*, who answered to the praetorian prefects. Italy lost its privileged status and was divided into provinces paying taxes.

**Army.** The army grew substantially, though how much is disputed; estimates for the late empire range from about 400,000 to over
600,000. Lactantius, hostile, complained that each tetrarch wanted more soldiers than earlier emperors had had alone, so that those who
received from the treasury outnumbered those who paid into it.{{anc:Lactant. De mort. pers. 7.2–3}} Military command in many frontier
provinces was separated from civil government and given to *duces*.

**Taxation.** Diocletian created a system in which land and people were assessed in standard fiscal units (*iugum* and *caput*), so that
each year the government could calculate its needs and set a rate. Taxes were collected largely in kind (grain, meat, clothing) to supply
the army and administration. A new census was held. A regular cycle of reassessment was later fixed at fifteen years (the *indiction*).
For the first time the empire had something like an annual budget.

**Coinage.** In about 294 Diocletian introduced new gold, silver and bronze coins. The reforms failed to stop inflation, perhaps because the
state continued to issue large quantities of bronze coin and tariffed it at unrealistic values.

:::

## The Price Edict {#price-edict}

In 301 the emperors issued an edict fixing maximum prices for over a thousand goods and services, from wheat and wine to a lion, a
barber's haircut and a teacher's fee, with the death penalty for violation. It survives in fragments of inscriptions, mostly from the
eastern provinces.

:::source price-edict
:::

:::check di-2
:::

Lactantius claimed that the edict caused goods to vanish from the markets and bloodshed over trifles, until it was abandoned.{{anc:Lactant.
De mort. pers. 7.6–7}} The edict is now an invaluable source for relative prices and wages, and for transport costs, which have been used
to model the Roman economy.

## Preparing for an emperor: the Panopolis papyri {#panopolis}

How did Diocletian's demanding state work at local level? Two papyrus rolls from Panopolis (modern Akhmim) in Upper Egypt, now in the Chester Beatty Library in Dublin, preserve copies of letters received and sent by the chief official of the district (*strategos*) in 298 and 300. In 298 the emperor was expected to travel up the Nile, and the strategos had to make sure that bread, meat, wine, fodder and other supplies were ready at stations along his route. The letters show him chasing local men appointed to compulsory public services (liturgies), many of whom tried to avoid them; superiors threatening him with punishment for delays; and the constant movement of officials, soldiers and requisitions. The roll from 300 records payments of salaries and donatives to troops, in coin and in kind, under the new system.

:::claim interpretation
The Panopolis papyri suggest that Diocletian's reforms did not replace the old structures of local government but laid new demands on them. The empire's ability to feed its army and court still depended on unpaid local officeholders, reluctant villagers and the threat of punishment. They also remind us that Egypt is unusually well documented, so its experience cannot simply be assumed for other provinces.
:::

## The emperor as a sacred figure {#dominate}

Diocletian's court adopted elaborate ceremonial. Those admitted to the emperor's presence performed *adoratio*, prostrating themselves and
kissing the hem of his purple robe; the emperor wore gold, jewels and a diadem; his palace and council became the "sacred" palace and the
*sacrum consistorium*. Older scholarship called the new system the "Dominate", from *dominus* (lord), as opposed to Augustus' Principate.

:::claim interpretation
The contrast between "Principate" and "Dominate" is a modern simplification. Many elements of late Roman ceremony had developed gradually
in the third century, and the emperor's practical power did not suddenly increase. But the self-presentation of the emperor as remote and
sacred, rather than a first citizen among senators, was a real and deliberate change.
:::

## The Great Persecution {#persecution}

For almost two decades Diocletian left Christians alone; there were Christians in his household and possibly his family. In 297 or 302 he
ordered the persecution of the Manichaeans, a new religion of Persian origin. In February 303 the church at Nicomedia, opposite the palace,
was destroyed, and a series of edicts followed: scriptures were to be handed over and burned, churches demolished, Christians of rank
deprived of their status, clergy arrested, and finally all inhabitants ordered to sacrifice. Lactantius, who was in Nicomedia, blamed
Galerius and his mother. Enforcement varied: severe in the East and in Africa, minimal in Constantius' Gaul and Britain. In the East
persecution continued intermittently until Galerius issued an edict of toleration on his deathbed in 311.

## Lactantius: witness and prosecutor {#lactantius}

Much of what we know about Diocletian's later years comes from a single hostile witness. Lactantius, a North African teacher of rhetoric, had been appointed to teach Latin at Nicomedia, where he lived through the beginning of the persecution in 303. Later he became tutor to Constantine's son Crispus. Around 314–315 he wrote *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*, a pamphlet arguing that every emperor who had persecuted Christians, from Nero to Galerius, had met a terrible end, sent by God. Valerian is flayed by the Persians; Galerius dies of a disgusting disease that Lactantius describes in loving detail.

The work is invaluable. Lactantius was in Nicomedia, knew people at court, and preserves the Latin texts of Galerius' edict of toleration (311) and of the letter issued by Licinius after the agreement at Milan (313). But it is also polemic. Diocletian appears as greedy and timid, his reforms as oppression; Galerius as a monster; Constantine and his father as virtuous. His account of Constantine's vision before the Milvian Bridge must be read in the same way (see [[lesson:constantine#vision|the vision]]). Historians therefore test Lactantius against legal texts, papyri and inscriptions, and treat his explanations of motive with particular caution.

:::claim interpretation
Lactantius' account of the persecution blames Galerius for pushing a reluctant Diocletian. This may be true, but Lactantius had reasons to make Galerius the chief villain: Galerius had been the senior emperor in the East and the patron of Constantine's rivals, while Constantine's own father had been Diocletian's colleague, so a story that spared Diocletian some blame also spared the tetrarchic order from which Constantine's claims derived.
:::

## Abdication and collapse {#abdication}

On 1 May 305 Diocletian, after a serious illness, abdicated at Nicomedia and made Maximian do the same at Milan. Galerius and Constantius
became Augusti; the new Caesars were Severus and Maximinus Daia, both Galerius' men. The sons of Constantius (Constantine) and Maximian
(Maxentius) were passed over.

The succession system collapsed within a year. When Constantius died at York in 306, his soldiers proclaimed Constantine. Maxentius seized
Rome with the support of the Guard and the people. Maximian came out of retirement. At a conference at Carnuntum in 308, Diocletian refused
to return, reputedly saying that if they could see the cabbages he had grown at Split, they would not ask him.{{anc:Epit. de Caes. 39.6}} By
310 six men claimed the title of Augustus.

## Consequences and historiography {#historiography}

Diocletian's administrative, fiscal and military reforms endured, revised by Constantine, and shaped the late Roman and Byzantine state. His
succession system and his persecution failed. Older accounts, following Lactantius, portrayed him as an oppressive bureaucrat who
crushed the empire under taxes and officials. Modern historians, using legal texts and papyri (Corcoran, Barnes), emphasise the coherence
and practicality of his government, while questioning how far its measures were planned as a system rather than improvised in response to
problems.{{ref:corcoran-2000}}

:::aside The palace at Split
Diocletian's retirement palace, a fortified compound of about 3 hectares on the Dalmatian coast, became the nucleus of the medieval and
modern city of Split, whose inhabitants still live within its walls. His mausoleum became the city's cathedral, dedicated to a saint
believed to have been martyred in his persecution.
:::
