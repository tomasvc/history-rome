## Introduction {#context}

Roman buildings and infrastructure are among the most visible legacies of the empire: aqueducts striding across valleys, roads running straight
for miles, amphitheatres, bridges and domes still standing after two thousand years. They were made possible by new materials and techniques,
by the organisation of labour and capital, and by the political and social motives that drove emperors, cities and individuals to build.

## Materials and techniques {#materials}

The key Roman innovation was **concrete** (*opus caementicium*): a mortar of lime and volcanic ash (pozzolana) mixed with rubble aggregate. It
was strong, cheap, could be poured into timber moulds to make any shape, and would set even under water. Romans faced it with stone, brick or
stucco. Combined with the **arch** and its extensions, the barrel vault, cross vault and dome, concrete allowed the Romans to roof vast
interior spaces without columns.

:::check en-1
:::

The Pantheon, rebuilt under Hadrian, has an unreinforced concrete dome some 43 metres in diameter, the largest in the world for well over a
thousand years. Its builders lightened the dome by using progressively lighter aggregate towards the top and by coffering, and left an open
oculus at the centre. Modern studies of Roman marine concrete have identified chemical processes that made it more durable in seawater than
many modern concretes.

Vitruvius, writing under Augustus, set out the principles of architecture as firmness, utility and beauty (*firmitas, utilitas, venustas*).
His treatise, rediscovered in the Renaissance, shaped European architecture for centuries.{{ref:vitruvius-rowland-1999}}

## Water {#water}

:::diagram aqueduct
:::

Rome's first aqueduct, the Aqua Appia, was built in 312 BC by the censor Appius Claudius; by the third century AD eleven aqueducts supplied the
city. Aqueducts were mostly underground channels, rising onto arches only where they crossed valleys. They depended on gravity, with gradients
carefully surveyed using instruments such as the *chorobates* (a long levelling table) and the *groma*. The aqueduct supplying Nemausus (Nîmes)
in Gaul descended only about 12 metres over some 50 kilometres; its bridge over the river Gardon, the Pont du Gard, stands nearly 50 metres
high.

:::image pont-du-gard
:::

Water fed public fountains, baths, latrines and the houses of those with rights to private connections. In 97, Sex. Julius Frontinus became
curator of the waters and wrote a treatise on Rome's supply.

:::

## Roads {#roads}

The first great road, the Via Appia from Rome to Capua, was also begun by Appius Claudius in 312 BC. By the second century AD the empire had a
network of tens of thousands of kilometres of main roads, built with layered foundations, cambered surfaces and drainage, and marked with
milestones recording distances and the emperor responsible. Roads served above all military and administrative purposes, the movement of
armies and of the imperial post, but also carried trade and travellers. Modern network models such as ORBIS, built at Stanford, use Roman
roads, rivers and sea routes to calculate travel time and cost across the empire.

## Cities and monuments {#cities}

Roman colonies and military camps were often laid out on a grid with two main streets crossing at the forum. Cities across the empire
acquired a recognisable set of buildings: forum, basilica, temples, baths, theatre, amphitheatre, aqueduct and walls. These were paid for by
emperors, by local elites competing for honour, and by city funds, and built by contractors using free labour, slaves and, for public works,
sometimes soldiers.

:::check en-3
:::

The Colosseum (opened 80) seated perhaps 50,000 spectators, with an elaborate system of entrances, awnings and underground lifts for animals and
scenery. Harbours such as Claudius' and Trajan's at Portus near Ostia, and Herod's harbour at Caesarea built with hydraulic concrete, show the
scale of investment in the grain supply and trade.

## Military engineering {#military}

Roman armies built constantly: camps every night on campaign, roads, bridges (Caesar's Rhine bridge, Trajan's bridge over the Danube), siege
works, forts and frontier walls such as Hadrian's Wall. The Theodosian walls of Constantinople, completed in 413, defended the city for a
thousand years.

## Case study: the Colosseum {#colosseum}

The Flavian Amphitheatre was begun by Vespasian in the early 70s on the site of the artificial lake of Nero's Golden House, inaugurated by Titus in 80, and completed by Domitian, who added the underground passages and lifts (*hypogeum*). Its outer wall is of travertine stone; inside, radial walls of tufa and brick-faced concrete support vaulted corridors and the sloping seating. About eighty entrances, most of them numbered, allowed tens of thousands of spectators to reach their places quickly; seating was arranged by social rank, with senators at the front and women and slaves at the top. Sailors from the fleet at Misenum operated the great awnings (*velarium*) that shaded the spectators.

How was it paid for? The epigrapher Géza Alföldy reconstructed a dedicatory inscription from the holes that once held its bronze letters, reading that the emperor had the new amphitheatre built "from the spoils of war", probably the treasure taken from Jerusalem in 70. If his reading is right, the building that symbolised the Flavians' gift to the Roman people was paid for by the destruction of the Temple.

:::claim interpretation
Alföldy's reconstruction of the Colosseum inscription from peg-holes is widely accepted but not certain; reconstructions of lost bronze letters always involve inference. It is a good example of how epigraphers can recover texts that no longer exist.
:::

## Case study: Trajan's bridge {#bridge}

For his second Dacian war, Trajan's architect Apollodorus of Damascus built a bridge across the Danube near the Iron Gates, completed around 105. Cassius Dio, who marvelled at it, says it stood on twenty stone piers carrying a timber superstructure, and the total length was over a kilometre.{{anc:Cass. Dio 68.13}} The bridge appears on Trajan's Column, and the stumps of some piers still survive. It was a military and political statement as much as a work of engineering: Rome could cross the greatest river of Europe at will. Hadrian is said to have removed its superstructure to stop raiders using it.

## Who did the work? {#labour}

Great building projects required enormous labour, materials and organisation. Public works in Rome were generally let to contractors (*redemptores*) who employed skilled free craftsmen, hired labourers and slaves; soldiers built roads, bridges, forts and aqueducts in the provinces; and condemned criminals worked in quarries and mines. Bricks were stamped with the names of the estates and workshops that produced them, which allows archaeologists to date buildings and to see that members of the elite, including emperors, profited from brick production. Construction also employed large numbers of the free urban poor, which may explain why emperors valued building as a source of work as well as of glory. Suetonius tells of an engineer who offered Vespasian a cheap way of hauling great columns up to the Capitol; the emperor rewarded him but declined to use the device, saying that he must be allowed to feed the common people.{{anc:Suet. Vesp. 18}}

## The late antique and Byzantine achievement {#late}

Roman engineering did not end with the western empire. Hagia Sophia (532–537), designed by Anthemius and Isidore, combined a central dome with
semi-domes in a design unprecedented in scale.

:::

## Technology and the economy {#technology}

Historians once argued, following Finley, that ancient technology was stagnant because slave labour removed the incentive to innovate and elites
despised manual work. Recent research has found much more evidence of innovation and diffusion: water-mills (the complex at Barbegal in southern
Gaul had sixteen wheels), large-scale hydraulic mining (Pliny describes the techniques used in north-western Spain, visible at Las Médulas),
mechanical cranes, glass-blowing and mass production of pottery. There was no industrial revolution, but the Roman world used technology
extensively within the limits of an agrarian economy.

:::

## Historiography {#historiography}

Architectural history traditionally studied Roman buildings stylistically and as a sequence of great monuments. Engineering and archaeological
science now examine materials, construction processes, labour and costs. Studies of building economics ask how many workers and how much money
a project required, and what it tells us about the state's resources.{{ref:adam-1994}}

:::aside Building as communication
Roman buildings carried inscriptions naming those who paid for them: "Marcus Agrippa, son of Lucius, consul for the third time, made this" on
the Pantheon, even though Hadrian rebuilt it. Reading building inscriptions is one of the best ways to see who claimed credit for public works,
and why.
:::
