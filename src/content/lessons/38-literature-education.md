## Education {#education}

Roman education was private and unequal. Wealthy families educated children at home with tutors, often Greek slaves, or sent them to schools.
A *ludi magister* taught reading, writing and arithmetic to children from about seven; from about eleven, boys and some girls of the elite studied
with a *[[term:grammaticus]]*, who taught Greek and Latin literature, above all the poets, with close attention to language. From about fifteen,
young men studied with a *rhetor*, learning to compose and deliver speeches through exercises culminating in *[[term:declamation]]* on
imaginary legal cases and historical dilemmas.

:::check le-2
:::

Rhetoric was the core because public speaking was the essential skill of the Roman elite, in the courts, the Senate and the assemblies, and
later in imperial administration. Quintilian, a professor of rhetoric under Vespasian and Domitian, wrote a detailed treatise on the education
of the orator. Some, like Cicero and many later Romans, completed their education in Athens or Rhodes, studying philosophy and rhetoric with
Greek teachers. Education was bilingual for the elite: Greek was the language of culture throughout the empire.

How many Romans could read? Estimates vary widely; William Harris argued for low literacy rates, perhaps 10 per cent or less overall, while
the evidence of graffiti, tablets and papyri suggests that many people had at least basic or functional literacy.

## Latin literature {#literature}

Latin literature began around 240 BC with Livius Andronicus' adaptations of Greek plays and Homer. Denis Feeney argues that the creation of a
literature in Latin, translating and adapting another culture's canon, was an extraordinary and unusual event in world history.{{ref:feeney-2016}}

- **The Republic**: comedies of Plautus and Terence; Ennius' epic; Cato's prose; the philosophical poem of Lucretius; the lyric poetry of
  Catullus; the speeches, letters and treatises of Cicero; Caesar's commentaries; Sallust's histories.
- **The Augustan age**: Virgil, Horace, Livy, Propertius, Tibullus and Ovid.
- **The early empire**: Seneca's tragedies and philosophical letters; Lucan's epic on the civil war; Petronius' novel; the satire of Persius
  and Juvenal; Martial's epigrams; Pliny the Elder's encyclopaedia and Pliny the Younger's letters; Tacitus and Suetonius.
- **Later Latin**: Apuleius' *Golden Ass*; Christian writers such as Tertullian, Jerome, Ambrose and Augustine; Ammianus; the poetry of Claudian.

## Inside a school {#school}

Most teaching took place in rented rooms, porticoes or the corners of public squares, with the teacher on a chair and pupils on benches, writing on wax tablets with a stylus. Children learned letters, then syllables, then words, by copying and reciting. Discipline was harsh: Horace remembered his teacher Orbilius as "fond of the cane".{{anc:Hor. Epist. 2.1.70}} Teachers had low status and were often freedmen or immigrants from the Greek East, paid by parents. Diocletian's Price Edict of 301 set the maximum monthly fee per pupil at 50 denarii for a reading teacher, 200 for a teacher of grammar and 250 for a teacher of rhetoric, which shows how the price rose with the level of study.

Some girls of wealthy families received an education with tutors at home, and a few women became known as writers or scholars, but formal rhetorical training, which prepared men for public life, was for boys.

From the reign of Vespasian, a few teachers of rhetoric held chairs paid by the state; Quintilian was the first in Rome. In late antiquity emperors and cities paid professors in major centres such as Rome, Constantinople, Carthage and Athens, and the grammarian became, as Robert Kaster has shown, a guardian of correct language and of the social distinctions that it marked.{{ref:kaster-1988}}

## Writing, performing and publishing {#publishing}

Roman literature was performed before it was read. Authors gave recitations of new work to invited audiences, a practice that Pliny the Younger both enjoyed and complained about. A "published" book was one released for copying: authors gave copies to friends, and booksellers employed slaves to produce copies for sale. There was no copyright, authors earned little from sales, and patronage, hospitality and gifts from wealthy friends mattered more. Martial complains that his poems were read throughout the empire while he stayed poor.

Literature was also political. Writers under the emperors negotiated what could be said: Ovid's exile, the forced suicide of Lucan after the Pisonian conspiracy, and Tacitus' description of the burning of books under Domitian show the risks.{{anc:Tac. Agr. 2}} At the same time, emperors and their circles were the most important patrons of letters.

## Christian literature and classical learning {#christian}

Christian writers were educated in the same schools as pagans and used the same rhetorical skills. Many were uneasy about it. Jerome reported a dream in which he was accused before God's judgement seat of being "a Ciceronian, not a Christian".{{anc:Jer. Ep. 22.30}} Augustine, a professor of rhetoric before his conversion, argued in *On Christian Teaching* that Christians should take what was useful from pagan learning, as the Israelites took the gold of the Egyptians. In practice, classical education continued to be the education of the Christian elite, and Christian monasteries and Byzantine scholars preserved much of the classical literature that survives.

## Greek literature in the Roman empire {#greek}

Much of the literature of the Roman empire was written in Greek: Polybius, Strabo, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch, Josephus, Appian,
Arrian, Lucian, Galen, Cassius Dio. In the second century, the "Second Sophistic" saw celebrity Greek orators performing across the empire.
Later Greek historians, from Procopius to Anna Komnene and Choniates, continued the classical tradition in Byzantium.

## Philosophy {#philosophy}

Roman aristocrats studied Greek philosophy, mainly as a guide to life. **Stoicism** taught that virtue is the only good, that one should live in
accordance with nature and reason, and accept what one cannot control; it was popular among senators, from Cato the Younger to Seneca, and
among emperors (Marcus Aurelius). **Epicureanism**, presented in Lucretius' poem, taught that the gods do not intervene in the world and that
the goal of life is tranquillity. Platonism, revived as Neoplatonism by Plotinus in the third century, shaped both late pagan and Christian
thought.

## Books and libraries {#books}

Books were papyrus rolls, copied by hand, often by slaves. Wealthy Romans built private libraries; public libraries appeared in Rome from the
reign of Augustus. The Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum preserved a library of carbonised rolls, mostly Epicurean philosophy; researchers have
begun to read unopened rolls using X-ray tomography and machine learning. The rubbish dumps of Oxyrhynchus in Egypt have yielded hundreds of
thousands of papyrus fragments, including lost literary works.

Between the second and fourth centuries the codex, a book with pages, replaced the roll, a change associated especially with Christians.

## Reading the unreadable: the Herculaneum scrolls {#herculaneum}

In 1752 workers tunnelling through the volcanic deposits of Herculaneum found a villa containing a library: about 1,800 papyrus rolls, carbonised by the eruption of 79. Many were destroyed by early attempts to open them; others were cut into pieces or slowly unrolled with a machine designed by the priest Antonio Piaggio. The texts that could be read were mostly works of Epicurean philosophy, many by Philodemus of Gadara, a Greek philosopher who lived in Italy in the first century BC and enjoyed the patronage of the Roman aristocrat L. Calpurnius Piso, Caesar's father-in-law; the villa may have belonged to Piso's family.

Hundreds of rolls could not be opened at all. In the 2010s researchers began scanning them with X-ray tomography, and in 2023 an international competition, the Vesuvius Challenge, offered prizes to anyone who could use the scans to read the hidden ink. In October 2023 a student identified the first word from inside an unopened scroll, the Greek for "purple"; early in 2024 a team won the grand prize for reading several columns of a philosophical text about pleasure, probably by Philodemus.

:::claim established
The Herculaneum library shows what a private Roman library could contain, how much ancient literature has been lost, and how new technologies can add to the corpus of ancient texts. It also shows how the accidents of survival shape our picture: we have a great deal of one minor Epicurean author because one villa was buried by a volcano.
:::

## Survival and loss {#survival}

Only a fraction of ancient literature survives: about a quarter of Livy's 142 books, perhaps half of Tacitus' historical works, and nothing of many
authors known by name. Survival depended on repeated copying through late antiquity, Byzantium, the Islamic world and medieval Western monasteries,
and on choices made by teachers, scholars and copyists. Byzantine scholars, such as the ninth-century patriarch Photios, preserved and
summarised many works; Carolingian scribes copied much of the Latin literature we have.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Literary scholars now read Roman literature not only as art but as evidence for social values, politics and identity, and historians read it with
literary sophistication, attending to genre, intertextuality and rhetoric. The study of education, literacy and book culture has become a field
in its own right.{{ref:morgan-1998}}
