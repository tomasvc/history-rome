## Historical context {#context}

Roman religion was a matter of cult practice rather than belief: communities and households honoured the gods with sacrifices, festivals
and vows to maintain the peace of the gods (*[[term:pax-deorum]]*). It was polytheistic and open: new gods could be added, and the
empire's peoples worshipped their own deities, which Romans often identified with their own. Judaism, though exclusive, was tolerated as
an ancestral tradition. Into this world came a new movement that began in Judaea around AD 30 with followers of Jesus of Nazareth, executed
by the prefect Pontius Pilate.

## Growth {#growth}

The first Christians were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Messiah, risen from the dead. Within a generation, missionaries such as Paul
had founded communities of Jews and gentiles in the cities of the eastern Mediterranean and in Rome. Christianity spread through networks of
family, friendship and trade, particularly in cities, and among people of varied social status, including slaves and women, but also some
of the wealthy.

:::diagram christian-growth
:::

How many Christians were there? No figures survive. Historians have extrapolated from scattered clues, such as a letter of around 251
suggesting that the Roman church supported 1,500 widows and poor and had 154 clergy.{{anc:Euseb. Hist. eccl. 6.43.11}} The sociologist Rodney
Stark proposed that a constant growth rate of about 40 per cent per decade would carry Christians from perhaps a thousand in AD 40 to some 6
million (about 10 per cent of the population) by 300 and a majority by 350.{{ref:stark-1996}}

:::check ch-3
:::

:::debate christian-growth
:::

Why did people join? Ancient Christians stressed the truth of their message and miracles. Modern historians point to the strength of
community: mutual support for widows, orphans and the sick; care during epidemics, which may have raised survival rates; a universal message
that crossed ethnic boundaries; a clear moral code; and the promise of salvation. Robin Lane Fox emphasised how demanding and distinctive
Christian life was, compared with the rich religious world of pagans.{{ref:lane-fox-1986}}

## A frontier town's gods: Dura-Europos {#dura}

The most vivid picture of religious diversity in the third-century empire comes from Dura-Europos, a garrison town on the Euphrates. Around 256, expecting a Persian siege, the defenders filled the buildings along the western wall with earth to strengthen it. The town fell anyway and was abandoned, but the buried buildings survived to be excavated by Yale University and the French Academy in the 1920s and 1930s.

Along that single street stood:

- **A Christian meeting house**, an ordinary private house converted for worship in the 240s, with an assembly room for perhaps sixty or seventy people and a baptistery painted with scenes including a shepherd with his flock, the healing of a paralysed man, Christ walking on the water, and women approaching a tomb. It is one of the oldest identified church buildings anywhere.
- **A synagogue**, its walls covered in the 240s with paintings of biblical stories, from the infancy of Moses to the Exodus and Ezekiel's vision, which overturned assumptions that Jewish art of this period avoided figurative images.
- **A Mithraeum** and temples of Palmyrene, Greek and Mesopotamian gods, used by soldiers and townspeople alike (compare the [[lesson:army-frontiers#feriale|army's religious calendar from the same town]]).

:::claim interpretation
Dura shows Christianity as one small community among many in a frontier town, meeting in a domestic setting, using a visual language shared with its Jewish and pagan neighbours. It cautions against imagining third-century Christians as either hidden underground or already dominant.
:::

## Persecution {#persecution}

For two and a half centuries persecution was local and sporadic, not a constant policy. It was usually triggered by popular hostility,
especially in times of disaster, when Christians, who refused to honour the gods, were blamed for divine anger. Tertullian, around 197,
complained that if the Tiber flooded or the Nile failed to rise, the cry went up, "The Christians to the lion!"{{anc:Tert. Apol. 40.2}}

:::check ch-1
:::

Trajan's reply to Pliny set the pattern: Christians were not to be hunted, but if accused and convicted they should be punished unless they
sacrificed.

:::source pliny-christians
:::

Martyr narratives, such as the account of the martyrs of Lyon (177) and the prison diary of Perpetua, a young mother executed at Carthage in
203, became central to Christian identity. The Decian edict of 249–250 created the first empire-wide crisis, followed by Valerian's targeted
persecution of clergy and Christian property, and then the Great Persecution of 303–311.

:::source libelli-250
:::

Persecution divided the churches. What should be done with those who had sacrificed or handed over scriptures and later wanted to return? In
Rome and North Africa disputes over these "lapsed" Christians produced schisms, including the Donatist church in Africa, which lasted for
more than a century.

## Constantine and his successors {#emperors}

Constantine's support for Christianity after 312 transformed the Church's position: from a persecuted association it became a
privileged institution receiving imperial money, buildings and legal powers.

:::source milan-letter
:::

His sons, especially Constantius II (337–361), went further. Constantius issued laws against sacrifice and closed some temples, though
enforcement was patchy, and he favoured a non-Nicene, "homoian" theology, opposing bishops such as Athanasius of Alexandria.

Julian (361–363), Constantine's nephew, raised as a Christian, secretly converted to the worship of the gods under the influence of
Neoplatonist philosophers. As emperor he restored temples and sacrifices, tried to create a pagan priesthood with moral and charitable duties
modelled on the Church, forbade Christians to teach classical literature, and planned to rebuild the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem. At Antioch
his lavish sacrifices and austere personal style provoked ridicule from Christians and pagans alike. He died in Persia in 363; his successors
were Christians.

:::

## Theodosius and the making of orthodoxy {#theodosius}

The decisive steps came under Theodosius I (379–395) and his western colleagues. In 380 an edict issued at Thessalonica defined the religion
of the empire as the Nicene faith.

:::source cunctos-populos
:::

:::check ch-2
:::

The Council of Constantinople (381) confirmed the Nicene creed in an expanded form. Gratian gave up the title of pontifex maximus and removed
the Altar of Victory from the Senate house; in 384, the prefect of the city, Symmachus, petitioned for its return in a famous plea for
religious tolerance, and Ambrose, bishop of Milan, successfully opposed him. In 390 Ambrose forced Theodosius to do public penance after his
soldiers massacred civilians at Thessalonica, a sign of the new moral authority of bishops.

Laws of 391–392 banned sacrifice and visits to temples. Christian crowds and monks destroyed shrines; in Alexandria the great temple of
Serapis was destroyed around 391. In 394 Theodosius defeated the western usurper Eugenius, whose supporters included pagan senators, at the
Frigidus. In 415 the Alexandrian philosopher Hypatia, a pagan and a teacher of Christians and pagans, was murdered by a Christian mob.

:::claim contested
Older accounts described a "pagan revival" among the Roman aristocracy in the 380s–390s and a final pagan resistance at the Frigidus. Alan
Cameron has argued that this is largely a modern construction: most aristocrats converted quietly, the Frigidus was not a religious war, and
the culture of classical learning was shared by pagans and Christians alike.
:::

## A conversion in his own words: Augustine {#augustine}

The most famous conversion narrative of antiquity was written by Augustine of Hippo. In the *Confessions*, composed around 397–400 when he was already a bishop, he describes how as a young man from a modest family in North Africa he studied rhetoric, joined the Manichaeans, rose to become professor of rhetoric at Milan, the imperial residence, and was drawn by Neoplatonist philosophy and the sermons of Bishop Ambrose towards Christianity. In a garden in Milan in 386, in turmoil, he heard a child's voice repeating "take up and read", opened Paul's letters at random, and read a passage that resolved his doubts. He was baptised by Ambrose at Easter 387.{{anc:Aug. Conf. 8.12.29}}

:::claim interpretation
The *Confessions* are not a diary but a theological work written more than a decade after the events, addressed to God and shaped by models such as the life of the monk Antony, whose story Augustine says helped bring about his own conversion. They show what conversion could mean to an educated man of the late fourth century: not a sudden change of belief alone but the abandonment of a secular career, marriage plans and ambitions. They cannot show what it meant to the many ordinary people who became Christians with less reflection or by social pressure.
:::

## Christian empire, Christian society {#society}

By the mid-fifth century the empire was officially Christian. Bishops had become civic leaders, managing church wealth, charity and
building, mediating with governors and emperors. Monasticism, beginning with figures like Antony and Pachomius in Egypt, spread across the
empire. Pilgrimage to the holy places and the cult of martyrs' relics reshaped the sacred landscape. The Theodosian Code (438) gathered laws
restricting pagans, heretics and Jews; Jews retained legal protection for their synagogues and practices but faced growing restrictions.

The Church was itself divided. After Nicaea came disputes about how divinity and humanity were united in Christ. The Council of Chalcedon
(451) defined Christ as one person in two natures; many Christians in Egypt and Syria rejected this definition, creating lasting divisions
among the churches of the East.

## Consequences and historiography {#historiography}

Edward Gibbon included Christianity among the causes of Rome's fall, accusing it of diverting energies and wealth to the Church and
undermining civic virtue. Modern historians see the story differently. Peter Brown's work recast late antiquity as a period of creative
religious and cultural transformation rather than decline.{{ref:brown-2003}} Ramsay MacMullen emphasised the role of miracles, coercion and
imperial power in conversion.{{ref:macmullen-1984}} Recent work stresses that "pagan" and "Christian" were not always sharp categories: many
people participated in both worlds, and Christianity itself was transformed by becoming the religion of the empire.

:::

:::aside The word "pagan"
"Pagan" (*paganus*) was a Christian term, perhaps from the Latin for a rural villager or a civilian as opposed to a "soldier of Christ". It
lumped together very diverse religious practices that their adherents did not see as a single religion. Historians use it for convenience,
but some prefer "polytheist" or "traditional religion".
:::
