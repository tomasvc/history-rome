## Historical context {#context}

In 410 the western empire still claimed authority from Britain to Africa. By 476 its emperor ruled only Italy, and in that year he was
deposed. The intervening decades saw the settlement of barbarian groups throughout the West, the loss of the most valuable provinces, the
dominance of generals over emperors, and a series of failed attempts at recovery. The eastern empire, under a separate emperor since 395,
supported the West intermittently but survived intact.

## Aetius and the Huns {#aetius}

After Honorius' death in 423, Galla Placidia secured the throne for her six-year-old son Valentinian III. Real power passed to the general
Flavius Aetius, who had spent years as a hostage among the Huns and used Hunnic troops to fight Goths, Burgundians and Bagaudae (rebels in
Gaul). For two decades he held the western provinces together through a mix of campaigns and treaties.

The Huns had built a large empire north of the Danube that, under Attila (from 434 jointly with his brother Bleda, from about 445 alone),
extracted huge payments in gold from the eastern empire and ravaged the Balkans. The historian Priscus, who accompanied an eastern embassy
to Attila's court in 449, left the most detailed account of it.

:::source priscus-attila
:::

In 451 Attila invaded Gaul and was checked by Aetius, with the Goths of Toulouse, at the Catalaunian Plains; in 452 he invaded Italy, sacked
Aquileia and withdrew after meeting an embassy including Pope Leo I. He died in 453, and his empire collapsed among his sons and subject
peoples.

:::claim interpretation
Later tradition credited Pope Leo with persuading Attila to spare Italy. Contemporary hints suggest more mundane reasons for his withdrawal:
famine and disease in his army, and an eastern army attacking his territories. The story of Leo's success says more about the growing prestige
of the bishop of Rome.
:::

## The loss of Africa {#africa}

In 429 the Vandals and Alans under Gaiseric crossed from Spain to Africa. Augustine died in 430 while Hippo was besieged. In 439 Gaiseric took
Carthage, the second city of the West. The loss of Africa removed the western government's richest source of taxes and the grain supply of
Rome, and gave the Vandals a fleet that dominated the western Mediterranean.

## Kingmakers {#kingmakers}

In 454 Valentinian III murdered Aetius with his own hand; in 455 two of Aetius' followers murdered Valentinian. Gaiseric, claiming that a
marriage treaty had been broken, sailed to Rome and sacked it for two weeks, systematically removing its treasures, including the spoils of the
Jerusalem Temple brought by Titus. The modern word "vandalism" was coined in the eighteenth century from this sack.

For the next two decades the western emperors were made and unmade by generals, above all Ricimer, a general of Suevic and Gothic royal
descent. Emperors such as Majorian (457–461), an able soldier, and Anthemius (467–472), sent from Constantinople, tried to restore imperial
authority. In 468 a vast joint expedition of the eastern and western empires against the Vandals, commanded by Basiliscus, was destroyed by
Vandal fireships off Cape Bon. It was the last realistic attempt to recover Africa, and it bankrupted the eastern treasury for years.

## A Roman aristocrat among kings: Sidonius Apollinaris {#sidonius}

Gallo-Roman aristocrats lived through the collapse of imperial power in Gaul and left the best evidence for it. Sidonius Apollinaris, born at Lyon around 430 into a family of prefects, married the daughter of Avitus, who was briefly emperor (455–456). He delivered verse panegyrics for three emperors, served as prefect of the city of Rome in 468, and around 470 became bishop of Clermont in the Auvergne. There he led the city's resistance to the Visigothic king Euric, until in 475 the imperial government in Italy, seeking peace, ceded the Auvergne to the Goths. Sidonius was briefly exiled, then restored to his see.

His letters, written in elaborate Latin and published in collections for an educated readership, show aristocrats keeping up their literary culture, estates and friendships across new political borders. They include a famous admiring portrait of the Visigothic king Theoderic II at his court in Toulouse, attending to business, prayers and dinner like a civilised Roman gentleman, and a comic poem complaining of seven-foot Burgundian guests whose hair was greased with rancid butter.

:::claim interpretation
Sidonius shows how the western aristocracy adapted. Many served barbarian kings as officials and advisers, or, like him, became bishops, an office that gave them local leadership as imperial structures faded. His letters also show the limits of accommodation: he regarded the abandonment of Clermont as a betrayal.
:::

## 476 {#476}

In 475 the general Orestes, once secretary to Attila, drove out the emperor Julius Nepos and proclaimed his own young son Romulus, nicknamed
Augustulus, "the little Augustus". In 476 Orestes' soldiers, barbarian federates, demanded land in Italy; when he refused, they proclaimed
their officer Odoacer king. Odoacer killed Orestes and deposed Romulus, who was pensioned off to a villa in Campania. He sent the imperial
regalia to Constantinople, declaring that the West did not need its own emperor, and asked to govern Italy as a patrician under the eastern
emperor Zeno.

:::

Zeno told the envoys that they should restore Nepos, who still controlled Dalmatia; but he accepted the situation. Odoacer ruled Italy with
the Senate and the Roman administration largely intact. In 489 Zeno sent the Ostrogothic king Theoderic to replace him; after a long war
Theoderic killed Odoacer at a banquet in Ravenna in 493 and ruled Italy until 526, presenting himself as a Roman ruler governing Romans through
Roman law while his Goths formed the army.

## The end seen from the frontier: Severinus of Noricum {#severinus}

What did the end of the western empire look like away from Ravenna and Rome? The *Life of Severinus*, written in 511 by Eugippius, a monk who had known the saint, describes the province of Noricum on the upper Danube (in modern Austria) in the 460s–480s. Severinus, a holy man of mysterious origins, organised the defence and food supply of the remaining Roman towns, negotiated with the kings of the Rugi across the river, and foretold disasters.

One passage captures the end of the Roman army on the frontier. While the empire still stood, Eugippius says, soldiers were maintained at public expense in many towns to guard the border; when this ceased, the units were disbanded along with the frontier. Only the garrison of Batavis (Passau) held on. Some of its men set off for Italy to fetch the last pay for their comrades. They never returned: their bodies were found washed up on the river bank, killed by barbarians on the way.{{anc:Eugippius, V. Sev. 20}} After Severinus' death in 482, Odoacer ordered the Roman population of Noricum to withdraw to Italy, and the monks carried the saint's body with them.

:::claim interpretation
The *Life of Severinus* is a saint's life, written to show God's power working through a holy man, and its chronology is vague. But it gives an unmatched picture of a provincial society in which the state had simply stopped functioning: no pay, no reinforcements, local defence led by a holy man, and negotiation with neighbouring kings as the only policy available.
:::

## Why did the West fall? {#causation}

:::debate fall-of-west
:::

There are hundreds of proposed explanations. A German scholar, Alexander Demandt, collected 210 of them in 1984. They can be grouped:

- **External pressure**: the Huns' impact on the peoples beyond the frontier, and the arrival of large barbarian groups inside the empire
  (Heather).{{ref:heather-2005}}
- **Internal political failure**: civil wars, usurpations, child emperors and rivalries among generals that diverted resources from
  defence, and a court at Ravenna that could not or would not negotiate effectively.
- **Fiscal collapse**: the loss of revenue-producing provinces, especially Africa, which made it impossible to pay armies, so that the
  government increasingly relied on barbarian federates whom it rewarded with land or tax revenues.
- **East–West difference**: the East had shorter, more defensible frontiers; Constantinople was almost impregnable; its richest provinces
  (Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor) were not threatened in the fifth century; and it could pay enemies to go west.
- **Longer-term transformations**: religious, social and economic changes, including the growth of great estates and the withdrawal of
  aristocratic loyalty, which some historians see as weakening the state's capacity.

Historians increasingly emphasise how these factors interacted, and how the crisis unfolded differently in each region.

## Decline, fall or transformation? {#historiography}

Gibbon's *Decline and Fall* framed the end of the western empire as the "triumph of barbarism and religion".{{ref:gibbon-1776}} From the 1970s,
Peter Brown and others redefined the period as "late antiquity", emphasising creativity and continuity in religion, culture and society.
Walter Goffart argued that the barbarian settlements were achieved largely by accommodation, with barbarians receiving shares of tax revenue
rather than land seized from Romans.{{ref:goffart-1980}} The European Science Foundation's project "The Transformation of the Roman World"
(1993–1998) popularised the language of transformation.

In 2005 two books pushed back. Peter Heather argued for a violent fall caused by external pressure. Bryan Ward-Perkins, using archaeology,
argued that the end of Roman rule produced a dramatic collapse in economic complexity and living standards, which the language of
"transformation" obscures.{{ref:ward-perkins-2005}}

:::check fw-3
:::

:::claim interpretation
Most historians now accept that the experience varied greatly. In Britain, Roman towns, villas, coinage and pottery production disappeared
within a generation or two after 410. In southern Gaul, Spain and Italy, Roman aristocrats, bishops, cities and law persisted for
generations under barbarian kings, even as trade and the economy contracted. The political end of the western empire and the end of the
Roman world in the West were not the same event.
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

By 500 the former western provinces were ruled by kings: Visigoths in Spain and southern Gaul, Franks in northern Gaul, Burgundians in the
Rhône valley, Vandals in Africa, Ostrogoths in Italy, Angles and Saxons and Britons in Britain. Most ruled through Roman institutions where
they survived, issued laws in Latin, and acknowledged in varying degrees the emperor in Constantinople. The eastern Roman empire, under
Justinian, would try to reverse the result.

:::aside A contemporary's eye view
Salvian of Marseille, a priest writing in the 440s, claimed that the poor in Gaul fled to the barbarians to escape Roman tax collectors and
that Romans deserved their fate for their sins. His rhetoric is moralising and exaggerated, but it shows that some contemporaries saw the
empire's collapse as self-inflicted.
:::
