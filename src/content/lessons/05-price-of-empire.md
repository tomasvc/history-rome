## Historical context {#context}

In 200 BC Rome had beaten Carthage and controlled Italy, Sicily, Sardinia and parts of Spain. The eastern Mediterranean was divided
among the great Hellenistic kingdoms that succeeded Alexander the Great: Macedon under Philip V, the Seleucid empire under Antiochus
III, Ptolemaic Egypt, and smaller powers such as Pergamum, Rhodes and the leagues of Greek cities. By 133 BC Rome had defeated
Macedon and the Seleucids, destroyed Carthage and Corinth, and been left the kingdom of Pergamum in a will. Polybius wrote his history
to explain this astonishing rise.

This lesson follows the expansion in the East and then turns to its effects at home: the government of provinces, the inflow of
wealth and slaves, and the transformation of Italian society that made the Gracchi possible.

## Expansion in the East {#expansion}

Rome's first war against Philip V (214–205) was a sideshow to the war with Hannibal. In 200, prompted by appeals from Pergamum and
Rhodes about Philip's aggression, the Senate proposed a new war. Polybius' and Livy's accounts show that the centuriate assembly at
first voted against it, war-weary after Hannibal, and had to be persuaded.{{anc:Livy 31.6–8}} In 197 T. Quinctius Flamininus defeated
Philip at Cynoscephalae; the Macedonian phalanx, formidable on level ground, was broken on uneven hills by the more flexible legions.
At the Isthmian Games of 196 Flamininus proclaimed that the Greek cities formerly under Philip would be free, ungarrisoned and
governed by their own laws. Plutarch describes a crowd so loud that birds fell from the sky.{{anc:Plut. Flam. 10}} Then Roman armies
left Greece.

What did "freedom" mean? Greeks understood it in their own diplomatic language, as independence. Romans seem to have understood it as
a relationship like that of patron and client: the freed owed gratitude and deference. Many later conflicts in Greece followed from
this misunderstanding, and Erich Gruen has argued that Roman policy was shaped by Greek diplomatic conventions far more than by any
plan of annexation.{{ref:gruen-1984}}

When Antiochus III crossed into Greece in 192, Rome fought him at Thermopylae (191) and then in Asia Minor, where L. Cornelius Scipio,
with his brother Africanus as adviser, won the battle of Magnesia (190). By the Peace of Apamea (188) Antiochus withdrew beyond the
Taurus mountains and paid a vast indemnity. Again Rome took no territory, rewarding Pergamum and Rhodes instead.

The next war showed a harder Rome. Philip's son Perseus was accused of plotting, and in the Third Macedonian War (171–168) L. Aemilius
Paullus defeated him at Pydna. The monarchy was abolished; Macedonia was divided into four republics forbidden to trade or intermarry
with each other. Paullus' army sacked seventy towns in Epirus, which had sided with Perseus, and enslaved 150,000 people, according to
Polybius as reported by Livy and Strabo.{{anc:Livy 45.34}} A thousand leading Achaeans, Polybius among them, were deported to Italy as
hostages. In 168 a Roman envoy, C. Popillius Laenas, ordered Antiochus IV to withdraw from Egypt; when the king asked for time, Popillius
drew a circle around him in the sand and demanded an answer before he stepped out of it. Antiochus withdrew.{{anc:Polyb. 29.27}}

Finally, after a rising in Macedonia (149–148) and a war with the Achaean League, Macedonia became a province and in 146 L. Mummius
sacked Corinth, killing the men and enslaving the women and children; its art treasures were shipped to Rome. In Spain, wars against
the Celtiberians and Lusitanians, with their leader Viriathus, lasted with intervals from the 190s to 133, when Scipio Aemilianus
starved Numantia into surrender. These Spanish wars were brutal, unprofitable and unpopular; men resisted the levy, and in 151 tribunes
imprisoned the consuls over conscription.

## Governing the provinces {#institutions}

Roman provincial government was minimal. A province (*provincia*) was originally a magistrate's sphere of command; by the second
century it also meant a territory regularly assigned to a governor. Each year the Senate decided which provinces would be given to
consuls and praetors, and their commands were often prorogued. A governor had a quaestor, a few legates and his personal staff
(friends, freedmen, slaves); there was no permanent bureaucracy. He commanded troops, judged important cases and oversaw the collection
of revenue.

Revenues took different forms. Sicily paid a tithe on grain under rules inherited from Hiero II; Spain paid a fixed tribute
(*stipendium*); mines, harbour duties and public land were leased. Increasingly, the right to collect taxes and run state contracts
was auctioned to companies of [[term:publicani|publicani]], men of equestrian wealth who formed partnerships to bid for contracts. They
paid the state a fixed sum and kept what they collected beyond it.

The system invited abuse. Governors served a year or two, had no salary, had often spent heavily to win election, and faced few
checks while in post. In 171 envoys from Spain complained in the Senate of extortion. In 149 the lex Calpurnia created a permanent
court (*[[term:quaestio]]*) for the recovery of money illegally taken by officials ([[term:repetundae]]), staffed by senators as jurors.
That senators judged senators would become a point of conflict in the next generation.

## Wealth, slaves and the Italian countryside {#social}

### Wealth

Conquest brought money into Italy on an unprecedented scale: booty displayed in triumphs, war indemnities from Carthage and Antiochus,
the silver mines of Spain, and provincial revenues. After Pydna the state treasury was so full that from 167 BC Roman citizens ceased to
pay the direct property tax ([[term:tributum]]).{{anc:Plin. HN 33.56; Plut. Aem. 38}} Commanders, officers and soldiers shared in booty.
Public building in Rome expanded: basilicas, porticoes, temples vowed in battle, and new aqueducts (the Aqua Marcia, 144–140 BC).

### Slaves

War also brought people. Mass enslavements, such as at Epirus in 167 and Carthage in 146, together with piracy, kidnapping, trade and
the children of slaves, created one of the few true slave societies in history, in which slaves formed a large part of the population
and played a central role in production. The Aegean island of Delos, made a free port by Rome in 166, became a great slave market;
Strabo says it could receive and dispatch tens of thousands of slaves in a day, though this is surely exaggeration.{{anc:Strabo
14.5.2}}

How many slaves were there? No ancient count survives. Modern estimates for Italy at the end of the Republic usually range between
one and two million, out of a total population of perhaps five to seven million, but they rest on assumptions about the Augustan census
figures and about slave demography.{{ref:scheidel-1997}}

:::claim contested
The numbers of slaves in Republican Italy are modern estimates, not ancient data. They depend on how the census figures of 28 BC
onwards are read (the "low count" and "high count" interpretations) and on models of how slave populations were sustained. Treat any
single figure as a scenario rather than a fact.
:::

The first great slave rising broke out in Sicily around 135 BC under a Syrian slave, Eunus, who styled himself king; it took Roman
consular armies until 132 to suppress. Ancient writers blamed the cruelty of Sicilian masters and the concentration of slaves on big
ranches and estates.

### The countryside

:::diagram hopkins-model
:::

Ancient writers believed that the wealth of empire had transformed Italian farming. Appian and Plutarch, telling the story of Tiberius
Gracchus, describe rich men buying up or seizing the land of poor neighbours and of the public domain ([[term:ager-publicus]]), and
working great estates ([[term:latifundia]]) with slaves, while the free poor, worn out by long military service, were driven
off.{{anc:App. B Civ. 1.7; Plut. Ti. Gracch. 8}} Cato's handbook *On Agriculture*, written around 160 BC, describes a profitable olive
and vine estate of moderate size worked by slaves under a slave overseer, and recommends selling worn-out slaves along with old oxen.

Keith Hopkins turned this picture into a model: conquest, wealth, slaves and the displacement of the peasantry were linked parts of a
single process.{{ref:hopkins-1978}} P. A. Brunt's *Italian Manpower* documented the strain of military service, estimating that in many
years of the second century a very high proportion of adult male citizens was under arms.{{ref:brunt-1971}}

:::check pe-2
:::

Since the 1980s the model has been challenged. Archaeological field surveys in several regions find small farmsteads continuing through
the second century, with villas appearing alongside rather than replacing them; the picture varies greatly by region. Nathan
Rosenstein argued that military service need not have ruined farms, since families could spare young unmarried sons, and that the
real problem in the 130s may have been population growth without enough land, rather than decline.{{ref:rosenstein-2004}} The census
figures of the period are ambiguous: they dip in the 130s and rise sharply after the Gracchan land distributions.

:::check pe-3
:::

:::claim interpretation
Most historians now accept a more varied picture than Appian's: large slave-run estates grew in some regions, especially in central
and southern Italy, while elsewhere small farmers persisted. What contemporaries perceived as a crisis, the shortage of men qualified
for legionary service, was real as a political problem even if its causes were more complex than the ancient narratives allow.
:::

## Greek culture and Roman identity {#culture}

Conquest brought Greek art, books, teachers and slaves to Rome. The first Latin literature was written by men from the Greek or
Oscan south: Livius Andronicus, a freed Tarentine, adapted Greek plays and the *Odyssey*; Ennius wrote an epic history of Rome; Plautus
and Terence adapted Greek comedies. Roman history itself began in Greek, with Fabius Pictor around 200 BC. Aemilius Paullus brought
the Macedonian royal library to Rome after Pydna.

Elite Romans responded ambivalently. Cato the Elder, who learned Greek and studied Greek literature, attacked Greek luxury and doctors
and wrote the first Latin history, the *Origines*, in deliberate contrast to his Greek-writing predecessors. In 161 philosophers and
rhetoricians were expelled from Rome; in 155 the Athenian philosopher Carneades caused scandal by arguing for justice one day and
against it the next. Hellenisation was not a one-way process of Romans becoming Greek: Romans adopted what they wanted, defined
themselves against Greek "softness", and created a new hybrid culture.{{ref:wallace-hadrill-2008}}

The Senate's handling of the Bacchic cult in 186 BC shows this anxiety turned into policy. Livy tells a lurid story of secret nocturnal
rites, crime and conspiracy; thousands were said to have been executed. An inscribed copy of the Senate's decree, found at Tiriolo in
southern Italy, shows that the Senate did not abolish the cult but brought it under strict control.

## Consequences {#consequences}

By 133 BC the Roman elite had become far richer, and the gap between the greatest families and the rest had widened. Competition for
office, commands and triumphs intensified, and with it electoral bribery (a law against it was passed in 181) and the cost of a
career. The equestrian order grew richer through contracts and trade. Italy's allies fought Rome's wars but had no share in its
decisions and could be treated arrogantly by Roman magistrates. And in the countryside, whether as a result of slavery, of service, or
of population growth, many citizens lacked the land that would qualify them to serve and support a family.

These were the conditions that a young noble, Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, brother-in-law of Scipio Aemilianus, would try to address
in 133 BC, with consequences that no one foresaw.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Was Roman imperialism planned, defensive or driven by aggressive habits? Theodor Mommsen and Maurice Holleaux saw much of it as
reactive, "defensive imperialism". W. V. Harris (1979) argued that Roman society was structurally bellicose: aristocrats needed glory,
voters expected booty, and Rome went to war almost every year.{{ref:harris-1979}} Arthur Eckstein (2006) replied that the Hellenistic
Mediterranean was an anarchic system of militarised states in which all powers behaved aggressively; Rome was distinctive in its
capacity to mobilise and integrate allies rather than in its appetite for war.{{ref:eckstein-2006}} The debate overlaps with Gruen's
emphasis on Greek initiative and Roman indifference to the East.{{ref:gruen-1984}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Reading Polybius on Rome's rise
Polybius claimed that from 220 BC the history of the Mediterranean became an organic whole, all of it tending to one end: Roman rule.
That is a powerful explanatory frame, but it is also teleological. When reading him, ask whether events really pointed in one
direction at the time, or only seem to in retrospect.
:::
