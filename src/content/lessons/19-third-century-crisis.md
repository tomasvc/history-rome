## Historical context {#context}

The murder of Severus Alexander in 235 began half a century in which at least twenty men were recognised as emperor by the Senate and
many more were proclaimed by armies; most died violently. The empire suffered its worst defeats in centuries, a Roman emperor was
captured, the currency collapsed, a pandemic recurred, and for over a decade the empire split into three. Yet by 284 it had been
reunited and was about to be reorganised more thoroughly than at any time since Augustus. Historians have called this "the
third-century crisis", and have debated the label ever since.

## Emperors made by soldiers, 235–253 {#emperors}

Maximinus, an officer from Thrace who had risen from the ranks, was proclaimed by the soldiers on the Rhine who had killed Severus
Alexander. He never visited Rome. His demands for money provoked a revolt in Africa in 238, where landowners proclaimed the elderly
proconsul Gordian and his son; the Senate recognised them, then, after they were killed by the governor of Numidia, elected two of its
own, Pupienus and Balbinus. Maximinus was killed by his own troops besieging Aquileia; the Guard murdered Pupienus and Balbinus; the
thirteen-year-old Gordian III, grandson of the African Gordian, survived as the sixth emperor of the year.

Gordian III died on campaign against Persia in 244; his successor Philip, an Arab from the province of Arabia, made peace with Shapur I,
paying a large sum, and celebrated Rome's thousandth birthday in 248. In 249 Decius, a senator from Pannonia sent to restore order on the
Danube, was proclaimed by his troops and defeated Philip.

The pattern was the same each time: an army proclaimed its commander, who had to defeat rivals and pay soldiers, and who would be killed if
he failed. The frontiers were stripped of troops for civil war, inviting attack, and attacks prompted new usurpations by commanders who
had to deal with them locally.

## Enemies on two fronts {#enemies}

The Sasanian dynasty, founded by Ardashir in the 220s, was more centralised and aggressive than the Parthians. Ardashir's son Shapur I
(c. 240–270) invaded Roman territory three times, captured Antioch, and destroyed Dura-Europos on the Euphrates around 256. Excavations at
Dura have revealed the siege tunnels in which Roman and Persian soldiers died, as well as a synagogue and a house-church with remarkable
paintings.

On the Rhine and Danube, Rome faced new confederations: the Franks and Alamanni on the Rhine, and on the lower Danube the Goths, who had
migrated from the Baltic region over the previous century. In 251 Decius was killed at Abritus in a swamp, the first emperor to die
fighting a foreign enemy. Goths and other peoples raided by sea across the Black Sea and Aegean; in 267 or 268 Athens was sacked by the
Heruli, and the historian Dexippus led a local resistance.

## Valerian, Gallienus and the fragmentation of the empire {#fragmentation}

Valerian (253–260) made his son Gallienus co-emperor and divided responsibilities, Valerian in the East, Gallienus in the West. In 260
Valerian was captured by Shapur near Edessa, along with his senior staff, and died in captivity; later Christian writers, notably
Lactantius, gloated that the persecutor's skin was flayed and displayed in a Persian temple, a story that cannot be confirmed. Rock
reliefs at Naqsh-e Rustam and Bishapur show Roman emperors submitting to Shapur on horseback.

The disaster split the empire. In the West, the commander M. Cassianius Latinius Postumus was proclaimed emperor by the Rhine army after a
dispute with Gallienus' son, and created a separate "Gallic empire" (260–274) covering Gaul, Germany, Britain and for a time Spain, with
its own consuls and Senate, based at Cologne and then Trier. In the East, Odaenathus, the ruler of the caravan city of Palmyra, defeated
the Persians on Rome's behalf and was given a commanding role; after his murder in 267, his widow Zenobia, ruling for their son Vaballathus,
extended Palmyrene control over Egypt and much of Asia Minor.

:::claim established
An altar found at Augsburg in 1992 records a victory in April 260 by the governor of Raetia over Semnones or Juthungi returning from a raid
on Italy, and the liberation of "many thousands" of Italian captives. It names Postumus as emperor, showing that Raetia briefly recognised
the Gallic empire, and gives vivid documentary evidence of the scale of raiding into Italy itself.
:::

:::check tc-1
:::

Gallienus, sole emperor from 260 to 268, held the centre: Italy, Africa, the Danube and Greece. He created a mobile force with a strong
cavalry element, based at Milan, which some historians see as the ancestor of the later field armies. Later sources claim that he excluded
senators from army commands, which were henceforth held by equestrian officers, many of them from the Danubian provinces. He ended the
persecution of Christians that Valerian had begun. In 268 he was murdered by his own officers.

## Money, plague and the cities {#money}

:::diagram debasement
:::

To pay soldiers, emperors minted more coins with less silver. The standard silver coin by mid-century, the *antoninianus* introduced by
Caracalla, fell from around 50 per cent silver to under 5 per cent by the late 260s. Prices in Egypt, which had been relatively stable for
two centuries, rose sharply in the 270s. The state increasingly collected taxes and paid soldiers in kind.

A pandemic, called after the bishop of Carthage, Cyprian, who described it, spread from about 249 and recurred for perhaps two decades.
Cyprian's description of fever, vomiting, bleeding eyes and lost limbs has led some to suggest a viral haemorrhagic fever; the agent is
unknown. A letter of Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria, suggests that the city's population fell dramatically, though his figures are
difficult to interpret.

:::claim contested
How much the plague contributed to the military and fiscal crisis is debated. Kyle Harper argues that the pandemic, together with a
deteriorating climate, was a decisive driver; others see its role as secondary to warfare and political instability, or argue that the
evidence for its scale is too thin to judge.
:::

:::debate climate-disease
:::

Decius' edict of 249–250, requiring all inhabitants to sacrifice to the gods for the safety of the empire, can be read as a response to
crisis: a demand for collective piety to restore the gods' favour. It led to the first empire-wide persecution of Christians.

## Recovery: the Illyrian emperors {#recovery}

The empire was reunited by emperors from the Danubian provinces, career soldiers of modest origins. Claudius II (268–270) defeated the
Goths at Naissus. Aurelian (270–275) drove invaders out of Italy and, as a precaution, began a new circuit of walls around Rome, about 19
km long, the first since the fourth century BC. He abandoned the province of Dacia north of the Danube, resettling its population south of
the river. In 272 he defeated Zenobia and took Palmyra, which was sacked after a second revolt; in 274 he defeated the last Gallic emperor,
Tetricus, who surrendered. Aurelian celebrated a triumph in which Zenobia was displayed, and built a temple to the Unconquered Sun (Sol
Invictus) in Rome. He attempted to reform the coinage, and was murdered in 275 by officers who believed false rumours of their own
execution.

His successors, Tacitus, Probus and Carus, were also soldier-emperors. Carus invaded Persia and died there in 283, reportedly struck by
lightning; his son Numerian died mysteriously on the march home, and in November 284 the army proclaimed the commander of the household
guard, Diocles, who took the name Diocletian.

## Was there a crisis? {#debate}

:::debate third-century-crisis
:::

Older historiography, from Gibbon to Rostovtzeff, saw the third century as a general collapse: political, military, economic and moral.
Géza Alföldy argued that contemporaries perceived their age as one of crisis, citing writers such as Cyprian.{{ref:alfoldy-1974}}
Christian Witschel's regional study found great variation: some regions, especially the northern frontier provinces, suffered badly, while
others, such as Africa, show continued building and prosperity.{{ref:witschel-1999}} Many historians now speak of a crisis of the imperial
state, with severe but uneven effects, or prefer terms like "transformation".

:::check tc-4
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The third century changed the empire. Emperors now came from the army, not the Senate. Senators lost their military roles. Emperors were
almost constantly on campaign, ruling from cities near the frontiers. Rome and other cities built walls. Taxation shifted towards payment in
kind. And the experience of near-collapse prepared the ground for Diocletian's attempt to rebuild the state on new foundations.

## Historiography and evidence {#historiography}

The period is poorly served by narratives: Dio ends in 229 and Herodian in 238; we rely on the late, fictionalised Historia Augusta, on
fourth-century epitomes, and on Byzantine chroniclers who drew on lost works such as Dexippus. Coins, inscriptions, papyri, archaeological
evidence and non-Roman sources such as Shapur's inscription are therefore unusually important. The scarcity of evidence is itself partly a
symptom: fewer inscriptions were set up in the middle of the century, though historians debate whether this reflects crisis or a change of
habit.

:::aside The epigraphic habit
The number of Latin inscriptions set up across the empire peaks in the late second and early third centuries and falls steeply after.
Ramsay MacMullen called this "the epigraphic habit" and warned against reading the decline simply as a decline in prosperity: it may also
reflect changes in the ways people chose to commemorate themselves.
:::
