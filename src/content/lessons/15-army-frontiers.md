## Historical context {#context}

The professional standing army created by Augustus was the largest single institution of the Roman empire, its most expensive
commitment and the ultimate foundation of every emperor's power. It defended (and extended) the frontiers, policed the provinces,
built roads and towns, spread Latin and Roman citizenship, and from time to time made and unmade emperors. This lesson describes how it
was organised, how its soldiers lived, and what frontiers meant.

## Organisation {#organisation}

:::diagram legion
:::

Under Augustus there were 28 legions, reduced to 25 after the Varian disaster, later increasing to about 30 under the Antonines and 33
under Severus. Each legion had about 5,000–5,500 men: ten cohorts, of six centuries of 80 men (the first cohort was of double strength
from the late first century), plus a small cavalry detachment. The commander (*legatus legionis*) was a senator of praetorian rank;
under him served six tribunes (one senatorial, five equestrian), a camp prefect who had risen from the ranks, and 59 or 60
centurions, the professional backbone.

Legionaries had to be Roman citizens. Alongside them served the **auxilia**, recruited from non-citizen provincials and peoples on the
frontiers, in infantry cohorts and cavalry *alae* of about 500 or 1,000 men, with specialists such as archers and slingers. By the second
century the auxilia equalled or exceeded the legions in number. After twenty-five years an auxiliary received citizenship for himself
and his children, recorded on bronze **diplomas**, hundreds of which survive and give a precise picture of units and their locations.

There were also fleets, the main ones at Misenum and Ravenna; the Praetorian Guard and urban cohorts in Rome; and the night watch
(*vigiles*). Altogether the empire probably maintained 300,000–400,000 soldiers, from a population of perhaps 50–70 million.

## Pay, service and recruitment {#service}

Under Augustus a legionary received 225 denarii a year, raised by Domitian to 300, and by Septimius Severus and Caracalla further.
Deductions were made for food, clothing and equipment. Service was 20 years (with five as a veteran), then 25. On discharge, veterans
received a cash bonus or land. Military pay was probably the largest item in the imperial budget.

Most recruits seem to have been volunteers, though conscription was used in emergencies. Over time recruitment became more local:
legions on the Rhine or Danube drew men from their provinces and from the families of veterans, so that "Roman" armies were increasingly
composed of provincials whose Roman identity came from service itself. Until Severus, soldiers below the rank of centurion could not
legally marry, but many lived with partners and children in the settlements outside the forts; their unions were recognised at
discharge.{{ref:phang-2001}}

:::claim interpretation
The army was one of the main routes into Roman citizenship and culture for provincials. Latin, Roman law, Roman religion and Roman
ways of building spread with the forts and veterans' colonies, though the culture of the frontier army was its own hybrid, not a copy of
Italy.
:::

## Hadrian inspects the army: Lambaesis, AD 128 {#lambaesis}

In the summer of 128 Hadrian spent several days with the army of Africa at Lambaesis in Numidia, the base of the Third Legion Augusta. His comments on their exercises were inscribed on a monument on the parade ground, and enough survives to hear an emperor talking to soldiers. He addressed the legion, its cavalry and several auxiliary units in turn. He praised horsemen for throwing their javelins well and mounting quickly; he complimented one unit for finishing in a single day a defensive wall that others would have taken several days to build; he gave technical criticism, for example of one unit that was slow to close ranks. Throughout he credited the legion's commander, his legate Catullinus, for the troops' quality.

The text shows the emperor in his role as commander (*imperator*): personally present, knowledgeable about drill, generous with praise but careful to show that he noticed weaknesses. It also shows how much of a peacetime army's life consisted of training, building and moving detachments between posts, rather than fighting.

## Soldiers' lives: the Vindolanda tablets {#vindolanda}

At Vindolanda, a fort just south of the later line of Hadrian's Wall, waterlogged deposits have preserved hundreds of thin wooden writing
tablets from around AD 85–130, the largest collection of Latin documents from the northern frontier. They include strength reports,
accounts, requests for leave, lists of supplies, and private letters: about socks and underpants sent from home, about beer running out,
about money owed.

The tablets show an army that was an administrative machine as much as a fighting force, constantly counting, requisitioning and
writing; a garrison composed largely of Batavians and Tungrians from the lower Rhine; and a social world in which commanders' families,
slaves, traders and soldiers lived together.{{ref:bowman-1994}}

## The army's religious year: Dura-Europos {#feriale}

Among the papyri found at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates are the records of an auxiliary unit, the Twentieth Cohort of Palmyrenes. They include daily reports and rosters and a calendar of festivals, the *Feriale Duranum*, written in the reign of Severus Alexander (222–235). It lists the sacrifices the unit was to perform through the year: vows for the emperor's safety at the start of January, the birthdays of deified emperors and members of the imperial family, the anniversaries of the reigning emperor's accession, festivals of Roman gods, and the *Rosaliae signorum*, when the standards were decorated with roses. Similar calendars were probably issued to units across the empire, binding soldiers to the emperor and to the official gods of Rome.

Yet the same unit appears in a wall painting from a temple at Dura, in which its tribune, Julius Terentius, offers incense before the gods of Palmyra, with the protecting goddesses of Dura and Palmyra beside them. Soldiers kept the official calendar and their own ancestral cults at the same time. Roman army religion was a framework of loyalty, not an exclusive faith.

## Frontiers {#frontiers}

The Romans had no single word for "frontier". *Limes* originally meant a road or path for military movement and came to mean the
fortified zone along a border; *ripa* meant a river bank. Roman frontiers took different forms in different regions:

- **Rivers**: the Rhine and Danube, lined with forts and patrolled by fleets.
- **Walls and barriers**: Hadrian's Wall and the Antonine Wall in Britain; a timber palisade and later a wall in Upper Germany and Raetia;
  the *fossatum Africae*, a series of ditches and walls in North Africa.
- **Roads and forts** without barriers, as on much of the eastern frontier, where Rome faced Parthia and desert peoples and where
  garrisons were often in cities.

Beyond the frontiers, Rome influenced peoples through diplomacy, subsidies, trade and client kings. Frontiers were zones where Roman
and non-Roman societies interacted, traded and intermarried, rather than lines that separated distinct worlds.{{ref:whittaker-1994}}

## The grand strategy debate {#debate}

In 1976 Edward Luttwak, a strategist, argued that the empire had a coherent "grand strategy" that passed through three phases: a
Julio-Claudian system of client states and mobile legions; an Antonine system of "preclusive" linear defence along fortified borders; and
a late Roman system of "defence in depth" with mobile field armies behind the frontiers.{{ref:luttwak-1976}}

Historians responded critically. Benjamin Isaac argued that Romans lacked the information and institutions for such planning, and that
the army in the East served largely internal security and aggressive purposes.{{ref:isaac-1990}} C. R. Whittaker showed frontiers as
social and economic zones.{{ref:whittaker-1994}} Susan Mattern argued that decisions were shaped by values such as honour, revenge and
the need to inspire fear, rather than by strategic calculation.{{ref:mattern-1999}} Others, like Kimberly Kagan, have defended the idea
that Romans could think strategically without modern bureaucracy.{{ref:kagan-2006}}

## The army and politics {#politics}

The army's political role was usually latent but always decisive. Every emperor cultivated the soldiers through pay, donatives and his
image as commander (*imperator*). The Praetorian Guard made Claudius and destroyed Galba; the frontier armies made Vespasian and, in 193,
Septimius Severus. Severus' reputed advice to his sons was to "enrich the soldiers and scorn everyone else".{{anc:Cass. Dio 77.15.2}}
The third century would show what happened when armies made and unmade emperors repeatedly.

## Consequences and historiography {#historiography}

The army shaped the empire's geography: legionary fortresses grew into cities, such as York, Mainz, Vienna, Budapest and Belgrade.
It drove the imperial economy through taxation and supply. Military history has shifted from battles and tactics to the social history
of soldiers, using documents like the Vindolanda tablets, papyri from Egypt and Dura-Europos, tombstones and diplomas. Adrian
Goldsworthy's work bridges traditional and social military history;{{ref:goldsworthy-2003}} J. B. Campbell's sourcebook makes the
documents accessible.{{ref:campbell-army-1994}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Tombstones
Soldiers' tombstones record name, unit, origin, age and length of service, often with a portrait. They allow historians to trace
recruitment patterns statistically, but they record only those who could afford a monument and whose stones survive, so they are a
biased sample.
:::
