## Historical context {#context}

After the sack of 1204, the crusaders established a Latin empire at Constantinople and divided much of the Aegean between themselves and
Venice. Byzantine successor states arose at Nicaea in western Asia Minor, at Trebizond on the Black Sea and in Epirus. The empire of Nicaea,
under the Laskarid emperors, proved the most successful: well administered and prosperous, it expanded into the Balkans and in 1261 its
general recovered Constantinople almost without a fight.

## The restored empire {#palaiologoi}

Michael VIII Palaiologos (1259–1282), who had usurped power from the young Laskarid heir (later blinded), ruled a restored empire much smaller
than before 1204. He faced the threat of a western invasion led by Charles of Anjou, king of Sicily, and tried to forestall it by accepting
union with the Roman Church at Lyon (1274), which most of his clergy and people rejected. The danger receded when the Sicilian Vespers (1282)
destroyed Charles's power in Sicily, a revolt Michael had encouraged with money.

:::

To recover Constantinople and Greece, Michael neglected Anatolia, where Turkish principalities grew. His successors faced financial
weakness, dependence on Genoese and Venetian merchants who dominated the empire's trade, and repeated civil wars. A Catalan mercenary company
hired against the Turks in 1303 turned against the empire. The civil war of 1341–1347 between the regency for John V and John Kantakouzenos
tore the empire apart, involved a revolt by the "Zealots" in Thessalonica, and drew in Serbian and Turkish allies. The Black Death arrived in
1347.

## The Ottomans {#ottomans}

The Ottoman principality, founded by Osman in north-western Anatolia around 1300, took Bursa (1326), Nicaea (1331) and Nicomedia (1337). In
1354, after an earthquake destroyed the walls of Gallipoli, Ottoman forces, earlier invited as allies in the civil war, occupied it and began
the conquest of the Balkans. Adrianople became their capital; Serbia was defeated at Kosovo (1389); Bulgaria was annexed. By 1400 the "empire"
of the Romans consisted of Constantinople, a few towns and islands, and the Despotate of the Morea in the Peloponnese, and paid tribute to
the sultan.

Sultan Bayezid I blockaded Constantinople from 1394. The emperor Manuel II travelled to Italy, Paris and London (1399–1403) in search of help.
The city was saved not by the West but by Timur, the Central Asian conqueror, who destroyed Bayezid's army at Ankara in 1402. The Ottoman state
recovered within two decades.

## An emperor in London {#manuel}

The weakness of the last centuries forced emperors to travel in search of help. In 1399 Manuel II left Constantinople, then under Ottoman blockade, and spent more than three years in Italy, France and England. In Paris he was lodged at the Louvre; in December 1400 he was received in England by Henry IV and spent Christmas at the royal palace of Eltham. The Welsh chronicler Adam of Usk, who saw him, reflected sadly on the sight of a Christian prince driven from the furthest East by unbelievers to beg for aid in the distant islands of the West. Manuel and his entourage impressed observers with their dignity and their white robes.

The visit produced sympathy, money and promises, but no army. Manuel was in Paris when news arrived that Timur had defeated Bayezid at Ankara in 1402, which saved the city for another half-century.

:::claim interpretation
Manuel's journey shows both the prestige that the Roman emperor still carried in western Europe and the limits of what it could achieve. It also shows the reversal of roles since the twelfth century: the emperor of Constantinople now came west as a petitioner.
:::

## Union and its failure {#florence}

In 1438–1439 John VIII travelled with the Patriarch and a large delegation to the council of Ferrara and Florence, where the union of the
Churches was proclaimed. Many Byzantine delegates repudiated their signatures on returning home, and the union deepened divisions in
Constantinople. A western crusade was destroyed by the Ottomans at Varna in 1444.

## Plethon and Bessarion: Byzantium and the Renaissance {#plethon}

The last century of Byzantium produced intellectuals whose influence was felt more in Italy than at home. George Gemistos Plethon, a philosopher at Mistra, the capital of the Despotate of the Morea, sent memoranda to the emperor and the despot proposing a reform of the Peloponnese on Platonic lines, with a citizen army and a reorganised economy. He attended the Council of Ferrara–Florence in 1438–1439 and there lectured on the differences between Plato and Aristotle, stimulating Italian interest in Plato. After his death his *Book of Laws*, which outlined a revived polytheism, was burned by Gennadios Scholarios, the future patriarch.

His pupil Bessarion, metropolitan of Nicaea, supported the union of the Churches at Florence, became a cardinal of the Roman Church, and settled in Italy. He collected Greek manuscripts systematically, saving texts from the loss of Byzantine libraries, and in 1468 gave his collection to Venice, where it formed the core of the library of St Mark.

:::claim interpretation
The transmission of Greek learning to Italy did not begin in 1453: Greek scholars had taught in Italy since the 1390s, and Italians had travelled to Constantinople to buy books. The fall of the city accelerated a movement already under way. Byzantium's contribution to the [[lesson:legacy|Roman legacy]] in the Renaissance was the work of people who chose, or were forced, to make their lives in the West.
:::

## The siege of 1453 {#siege}

:::diagram theodosian-walls
:::

Mehmed II, who became sultan in 1451 at nineteen, made the conquest of Constantinople his first goal. In 1452 he built the fortress of
Rumeli Hisarı on the Bosporus to cut the city off from the Black Sea. He assembled an army that the eyewitness sources put at between 80,000
and 200,000 or more; modern estimates are lower but still many times the defenders. Constantine XI had perhaps 7,000–8,000 men, including
several hundred Genoese under Giovanni Giustiniani Longo and Venetians, to defend 22 km of walls.

The siege began on 6 April 1453. Mehmed's artillery, including a giant bombard built by a Hungarian founder named Urban, battered the
fifth-century Theodosian land walls; the defenders repaired breaches each night. A boom across the Golden Horn kept the Ottoman fleet out
until Mehmed had some seventy ships dragged overland behind Galata on 22 April and launched into the harbour. Assaults on 18 April, 7 May and
12 May failed. Before dawn on 29 May the final assault began. After hours of fighting, Giustiniani was wounded and withdrew, Janissaries broke
through, and the city fell. Constantine XI died in the fighting; his body was never certainly identified.

:::

:::claim contested
Several famous details of the fall, such as the small gate (Kerkoporta) left open, or Constantine XI throwing off his imperial insignia to die
as a soldier, come from accounts written after the event and are uncertain. Estimates of the numbers of attackers and of those killed and
enslaved vary widely between sources.
:::

The city was sacked for a day (by some accounts three); thousands were enslaved. Mehmed entered Hagia Sophia and had it converted into a
mosque. He then set about repopulating and rebuilding the city as his capital, appointed the scholar Gennadios Scholarios as Orthodox
Patriarch, and took the title *Kayser-i Rum*, Caesar of Rome. The Despotate of the Morea, with its capital Mistra, fell in 1460 and the empire
of Trebizond in 1461.

## Consequences {#consequences}

The fall of Constantinople ended a political tradition that went back, through Constantine, to Augustus and the Roman Republic. Its impact was
felt widely: Greek scholars and manuscripts in Italy, already arriving before 1453, strengthened the study of Greek in the Renaissance; the
Orthodox Church survived as a self-governing community within the Ottoman empire; Moscow's rulers increasingly claimed to be the successors of
Byzantium ("the Third Rome"); and the Ottomans became a great Mediterranean power with their capital on the Bosporus.

## Historiography {#historiography}

For western historians, 1453 was long seen as a dividing line between the Middle Ages and the modern era. Byzantine historians now emphasise
how long the empire's decline lasted, and the creativity of its last centuries: the art of the Chora church, the theology of Gregory Palamas,
the Platonic philosophy of Gemistos Plethon at Mistra. Jonathan Harris has stressed the internal politics and the diplomatic skill that kept
the empire alive so long.{{ref:harris-2010}} The siege is unusually well documented, with Greek, Latin, Italian, Slavonic and Ottoman Turkish
accounts.

:::aside The walls
The land walls of Theodosius II, built in 413 and strengthened after an earthquake in 447, defended the city for over a thousand years. A moat,
an outer wall and a higher inner wall, with 96 towers, formed a triple barrier. They were breached by an enemy army only twice, in 1204 (from
the sea walls along the Golden Horn) and in 1453, the first time gunpowder artillery decided their fate.
:::
