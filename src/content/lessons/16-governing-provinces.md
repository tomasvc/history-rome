## Historical context {#context}

By Hadrian's reign the empire was divided into more than forty provinces with perhaps fifty to seventy million inhabitants. The central
government that ruled them was tiny: at any time a few hundred senators and equestrians held posts as governors, legates and
procurators, with small staffs of slaves, freedmen and soldiers. Keith Hopkins estimated that the empire had only one or two hundred
elite administrators at any one time, a far thinner layer of officials than imperial China maintained.{{ref:hopkins-1980}} How was this
possible?

## Governors and officials {#governors}

Under Augustus' settlement of 27 BC, provinces with significant armies were governed by **legates of the emperor** (*legati Augusti pro
praetore*), senators chosen by the emperor and usually serving three years or more. The others were "public" provinces governed by
**proconsuls**, senators chosen by lot for a year. Egypt and some smaller provinces (Judaea for much of the first century, the Alpine
districts, later Mesopotamia) were governed by **equestrian prefects or procurators**. Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea in 26–36, is
attested on an inscription found at Caesarea in 1961.

A governor commanded troops, kept order and judged cases, travelling on a circuit of assize towns (*conventus*). Alongside him,
equestrian **procurators** managed the emperor's estates and revenues and reported directly to Rome, a deliberate check on the governor.

:::check gp-1
:::

Governors remained capable of extortion. Senators were prosecuted under the extortion law throughout the first two centuries; Pliny
and Tacitus prosecuted Marius Priscus, proconsul of Africa, in 100. But emperors had an interest in good government: a plundered
province paid less tax and might rebel. Tiberius is supposed to have told a governor who sent more tax than required that a good
shepherd should shear his flock, not flay it.{{anc:Suet. Tib. 32}}

## Cities and local elites {#cities}

The answer to the question of how so few governed so many is that they did not do the governing themselves. The empire was a mosaic of
self-governing communities: Greek cities (*poleis*) in the East, Roman colonies and municipalities in the West, tribal communities
(*civitates*) in Gaul and Britain reorganised around new urban centres. Each had a council of wealthy local men (decurions), annual
magistrates and a body of citizens.

These cities collected taxes, maintained roads and water supplies, organised markets, festivals and games, policed their territory,
judged minor cases, and represented themselves to the governor and emperor through embassies. Their councillors were personally
liable for shortfalls in tax collection. In return they received status, honours, and often Roman citizenship.

:::claim established
Municipal charters inscribed on bronze in Flavian Spain, above all the lex Irnitana discovered in 1981, set out in detail how a provincial
town elected magistrates, ran its council, managed its finances and conducted legal cases according to Roman procedure. Holders of local
magistracies in towns with Latin rights became Roman citizens with their families.
:::

:::check gp-3
:::

Local elites spent heavily on their cities, a practice historians call [[term:euergetism]] ("doing good deeds"): they built temples,
baths, theatres and aqueducts, funded games and distributions, and were honoured with statues and inscriptions. The great urban
monuments of the Roman provinces, from Ephesus to Lepcis Magna, were mostly paid for by local benefactors rather than by Rome.

## Taxes {#taxes}

The main direct taxes were a tax on land (*tributum soli*) and a poll tax (*tributum capitis*), assessed by periodic censuses. The census
of Judaea under the governor of Syria, P. Sulpicius Quirinius, in AD 6, provoked resistance; the Gospel of Luke connects it with the birth
of Jesus, which creates a famous chronological problem. Italy paid no direct tax until the late third century. Indirect taxes included
customs dues (*portoria*), a 5 per cent tax on inheritances and on the manumission of slaves (paid by citizens), and taxes on sales.

Under the Republic, publicani had collected many direct taxes; under the emperors, direct taxes were increasingly collected by the cities
under the supervision of imperial officials, and publicani were confined largely to indirect taxes. Egypt, with its extensive papyrus
records, shows a highly developed system of registration and collection.

How heavy was the burden? Estimates suggest that the total tax take was probably modest in proportion to the empire's output, perhaps
around 5–10 per cent, but it fell unevenly, and rents paid to landowners were often far heavier than taxes paid to the state.

## Law, petition and the emperor {#law}

Provincials were subject to their own laws in most matters, to Roman law where Roman citizens were involved, and to the governor's
jurisdiction in serious cases. Anyone could petition the governor or the emperor. The emperor answered petitions from individuals with
short legal rulings (*[[term:rescript|rescripts]]*), which were posted publicly and became precedents. Fergus Millar showed how much of the
emperor's time was spent receiving embassies and answering such requests.{{ref:millar-1977}} The correspondence between Pliny and Trajan
shows the same system at the level of the governor.

## Babatha: a provincial in the Roman courts {#babatha}

In the early 1960s Israeli archaeologists exploring the Cave of Letters, in a ravine above the Dead Sea, found a leather pouch containing about thirty-five legal documents. They belonged to a Jewish woman named Babatha, from the village of Maoza at the southern end of the Dead Sea, in the territory of the former Nabataean kingdom that Trajan annexed as the province of Arabia in 106. She had apparently fled to the cave with her papers during the Bar Kokhba revolt and never came back for them.

The documents, written mostly in Greek with others in Aramaic and Nabataean, run from the 90s to 132 and show what Roman government meant to a property-owning provincial:

- **Census.** In the provincial census of 127 Babatha travelled to Rabbath-Moab to declare her date groves, their size and the tax they owed, before a Roman officer.
- **Guardianship.** After her first husband died, the city council of Petra appointed two guardians, one Jewish and one Nabataean, for her young son. Babatha took them to the governor's court, arguing that the allowance they paid was too small; among her papers is a Greek translation of the Roman legal formula for an action against guardians.
- **Family property.** After her second husband's death she was in dispute with his other wife and with his relatives over his estate.
- **Summonses.** Parties summoned one another to appear before the governor when he held his assizes at Petra or Rabbath-Moab.

:::claim interpretation
The archive shows Roman, Jewish and Nabataean legal traditions in use side by side, and a woman using Roman courts actively to defend her property and her son's interests. It also shows the limits of that system: a governor's assize came round only periodically, and litigation required travel, money, scribes and literate advisers.
:::

## When petitioning failed: Skaptopara, AD 238 {#skaptopara}

How well did the system of petition work for people far below the elite? In 238 the villagers of Skaptopara in Thrace petitioned the young emperor Gordian III. Their village lay near famous hot springs and a fair that lasted fifteen days, and soldiers, officials and visitors who came for the springs and the fair were demanding lodging and supplies from them without payment. Governors' orders against the abuse had worked only for a time. The villagers said that their numbers had already fallen, and warned that unless they were protected they would abandon their ancestral homes, and the treasury would lose their taxes. A fellow villager serving as a soldier helped them present the petition in Rome.

The emperor's reply, dated December 238, was brief: they should take this kind of complaint to the governor, who would settle it. The villagers nonetheless had the petition and the reply inscribed on stone, which was found in the nineteenth century in what is now Bulgaria.{{ref:hauken-1998}}

:::claim interpretation
The Skaptopara inscription shows both sides of Roman government by petition. Ordinary villagers could reach the emperor and expected a reply. But the reply sent them back to the local authorities who had failed them, and the abuses they describe, unpaid requisitioning by soldiers and officials, are among the most persistent complaints in the documents of [[lesson:third-century-crisis|the third century]].
:::

## Citizenship and integration {#citizenship}

Roman citizenship spread through several channels: colonies of veterans, grants to individuals and families, Latin rights for whole
communities (whose magistrates became citizens), auxiliary service, and manumission by citizen owners. By the early third century
perhaps a third of the free population held it. Provincial elites entered the equestrian order and the Senate; by the second century
about half of the Senate came from the provinces, and emperors themselves were born in Spain and Africa.

:::source claudius-gauls
:::

In 212 Caracalla extended citizenship to almost all free inhabitants of the empire (the subject of a later lesson). By then the
distinction between citizen and non-citizen had already been overtaken by a new one between *honestiores* (the privileged) and
*humiliores* (the humble), who faced harsher punishments.

## Why did provincials accept Roman rule? {#loyalty}

Rome's rule rested ultimately on force, as revolts in Britain, Judaea and elsewhere show. But most provinces did not rebel most of the
time. Historians have suggested several reasons: the integration of local elites, who had most to lose from disorder; the absence of any
alternative after the destruction of independent states; the benefits of peace for trade and agriculture; the imperial cult, which gave
provincial communities a way to express loyalty and compete for honours; and the emperor's accessibility through petition. Clifford Ando
argues that provincials came to believe in the legitimacy of Roman rule through repeated participation in its rituals of communication,
such as oaths, census and the display of documents.{{ref:ando-2000}} J. E. Lendon emphasised the role of honour: officials and subjects
traded respect and favours in a shared aristocratic culture.{{ref:lendon-1997}}

David Mattingly, by contrast, stresses the "discrepant experiences" of empire: elites gained, but many provincials experienced
dispossession, taxation, slavery and violence.{{ref:mattingly-2011}}

## Consequences {#consequences}

The system of government through cities was flexible and cheap, but it depended on the wealth and willingness of local elites. When
taxes rose and the cities' finances came under strain in the third century, decurions became reluctant, and late Roman emperors tied
them hereditarily to their duties. The model of an empire of self-governing cities under a thin layer of imperial officials was one of
the main things that changed between the second century and the fourth.

## Historiography {#historiography}

The study of Roman provincial government shifted in the late twentieth century from the history of administration (the lists of
governors and offices) to the study of how rule worked in practice and how it was experienced. Garnsey and Saller's synthesis made
"government without bureaucracy" a standard description.{{ref:garnsey-saller-2014}} Papyri from Egypt and new inscriptions, such as the
Spanish municipal laws, continue to change the picture.

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Is Egypt typical?
Most of our documents about everyday government come from Egypt, where papyrus survives in the dry sand. But Egypt had its own
traditions of administration going back to the Ptolemies and the pharaohs, and was governed differently from other provinces. Historians
debate how far the Egyptian evidence can be used to understand other regions.
:::
