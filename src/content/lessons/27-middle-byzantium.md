## Historical context {#context}

The empire that survived the Arab sieges of the seventh and early eighth centuries was smaller, poorer and more militarised than Justinian's.
Over the next five centuries it would recover much lost ground in the Balkans and the East, become the cultural model for the Slavic world,
suffer a new catastrophe in Anatolia, and in 1204 lose its capital to a crusading army. Its rulers and people called it the empire of the
Romans; western Europeans increasingly called them "Greeks".

## Iconoclasm {#iconoclasm}

Images of Christ, the Virgin and the saints had become increasingly important in Christian devotion during the sixth and seventh centuries. In
the 720s–730s the emperor Leo III, whose reign saw continued Arab attacks and a volcanic eruption in the Aegean, is said to have ordered the
removal of an image of Christ from the palace gate and to have opposed the veneration of icons. His son Constantine V (741–775), a successful
general, convened a council in 754 that condemned icons as idolatrous. The empress Irene, regent for her son, restored them at the Second
Council of Nicaea in 787. Iconoclasm was revived in 815 and finally ended in 843, an event celebrated ever since in the Orthodox Church as
the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

:::claim contested
Why did iconoclasm happen, and how violent was it? Explanations have included imperial reaction to military defeats as signs of divine
anger, influence from Islam or Judaism, a struggle against monastic power, and theological concerns about representing the divine. Leslie
Brubaker and John Haldon have argued that much of what we "know" comes from iconophile sources written after 843, which exaggerated
persecution, and that the first iconoclast emperors did less than later tradition claimed.
:::

## The Macedonian revival {#macedonians}

From the mid-ninth century the empire went on the offensive. The Abbasid caliphate was fragmenting; the themes provided local armies; the
economy and population were growing; and the currency, the gold *nomisma* (the descendant of Constantine's solidus), remained stable. The
missions of Cyril and Methodius to the Slavs created a Slavonic liturgy and alphabet; Bulgaria converted in the 860s and the Rus' of Kyiv
under Vladimir around 988.

The Macedonian emperors (867–1056) presided over a cultural revival in Constantinople, sometimes called the "Macedonian Renaissance": the
recovery and copying of classical texts, encyclopaedic compilations under Constantine VII (913–959), including manuals on court ceremony and
on foreign peoples, and the development of a sophisticated court culture.

Generals of great landowning families such as Nikephoros II Phokas (963–969) and John I Tzimiskes (969–976) recovered Crete, Cyprus, Cilicia
and Antioch. Basil II (976–1025), after defeating aristocratic rebellions, waged a long war against Bulgaria, which he annexed after the
victory at Kleidion (1014). Later sources claim he blinded thousands of prisoners, sparing one eye in every hundred to lead them home, which
earned him the name "Bulgar-slayer"; the story and the epithet first appear in sources written long after his reign. By his death the empire
stretched from southern Italy to the Caucasus and from the Danube to Syria.

## A view of the court: Liudprand of Cremona {#liudprand}

Much of the western image of Byzantium comes from one diplomat. Liudprand, a cleric from northern Italy, visited Constantinople twice. In 949, as envoy of the Italian ruler Berengar, he was received by Constantine VII in a hall where golden lions roared and beat their tails, mechanical birds sang in a gilded tree, and the emperor's throne was raised high into the air while he prostrated himself; he records that he was not surprised, having asked beforehand.{{anc:Liutpr. Antapodosis 6.5}}

In 968 he returned as envoy of the German emperor Otto I, to negotiate a marriage alliance. His *Embassy* is a hostile pamphlet: he complains of his lodgings, of resinated wine and fish sauce, and of the emperor Nikephoros II Phokas, whom he describes as an ugly, deceitful tyrant. At its core was a quarrel about titles. The Byzantines would call Otto only "king"; there was one emperor of the Romans, and he ruled in Constantinople. Liudprand answered with contempt for the Byzantines' claim, and a papal letter that addressed Nikephoros as "emperor of the Greeks" caused fury at the court.

:::claim interpretation
Liudprand's two accounts show how ceremony was used to overwhelm and impress foreign envoys, and how the claim to be the Roman empire mattered to both sides. His second report, written to please Otto after a failed mission, is a source for western prejudice as much as for Byzantine realities.
:::

## The eleventh-century crisis {#crisis}

After Basil, who had no children, the empire was ruled by his niece Zoe and her husbands and successors, while court officials, the
aristocracy and the army competed for power. The currency began to be debased in the 1040s. New enemies appeared: the Normans in southern
Italy, Pechenegs on the Danube, and the Seljuk Turks in the East.

In 1071 Romanos IV Diogenes was defeated and captured by the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan at Manzikert, near Lake Van. In the same year the Normans
took Bari, the last imperial city in Italy.

## The people and the throne: the revolt of 1042 {#1042}

The eleventh-century courtier and philosopher Michael Psellos wrote in his *Chronographia* an eyewitness account of one of the most remarkable events in Byzantine history. In April 1042 the emperor Michael V, the adopted son of the empress Zoe, Basil II's niece and the last representative of the Macedonian dynasty, exiled her to a monastery. When the decree was read in the city, the people of Constantinople rose. Crowds, including women, attacked the palace; Zoe was brought back, and her sister Theodora was brought out of her convent and proclaimed. Michael fled to a monastery, was dragged out, and blinded. The two sisters ruled jointly until Zoe married Constantine IX.

Psellos stresses the attachment of the people to the Macedonian dynasty, which had ruled for almost two centuries. Historians such as Anthony Kaldellis have used this episode, together with the self-description of the medieval [[lesson:roman-or-byzantine#identity|Romans of Byzantium]], as evidence that Byzantine emperors ruled with the consent of the people of the capital, who could and did withdraw it.{{ref:kaldellis-2015}}

:::claim contested
Was the Byzantine monarchy limited by popular sovereignty, or was the crowd simply a force that ambitious factions could mobilise? Psellos, our main witness, was a court insider writing years later, and he presents the revolt with literary art. The debate turns on whether such events were exceptions or expressions of an accepted principle.
:::

## The Komnenoi and the Crusades {#komnenoi}

Alexios I Komnenos (1081–1118) seized power in a military coup and restored the empire's position through constant warfare, diplomacy and
financial reform, including a new gold coinage. He fought off a Norman invasion of the Balkans with Venetian naval help, rewarding Venice with
trading privileges in 1082 that later fuelled resentment. Seeking mercenaries against the Turks, he appealed to Pope Urban II; the response was
the First Crusade (1096–1099), larger and more independent than Alexios had wanted. The crusaders restored Nicaea to him but kept Antioch and
created their own states in Syria and Palestine.

His daughter Anna Komnene wrote the *Alexiad*, a history of his reign in classicising Greek, one of the few medieval histories by a woman, and
a vivid source for Byzantine attitudes to the crusaders.

John II (1118–1143) and Manuel I (1143–1180) maintained the empire's position as a great power, with Manuel deeply involved in western
politics. But his defeat by the Seljuks at Myriokephalon (1176) ended hopes of recovering central Anatolia, and in 1182 a massacre of Latins in
Constantinople showed how hostile relations had become.

## 1204 {#1204}

The Fourth Crusade was launched to attack Egypt. Unable to pay Venice for its fleet, the crusaders agreed to divert to Constantinople to restore
a deposed emperor's son, Alexios IV, who promised money and troops. When he failed to pay and was murdered, the crusaders and Venetians stormed
the city in April 1204 and sacked it for three days. They elected a Latin emperor, divided the empire's territories, and carried relics,
treasures and the bronze horses now in Venice to the West.

## Consequences {#consequences}

Byzantine successor states emerged at Nicaea, Trebizond and in Epirus. In 1261 the Nicaean emperor Michael VIII recovered Constantinople, but the
restored empire was a regional power. The sack of 1204 poisoned relations between Orthodox and Latin Christians for centuries.

## Historiography {#historiography}

For much of modern history, Byzantium was seen as a decadent, priest-ridden autocracy, a view shaped by Enlightenment writers such as Gibbon
and Voltaire. Twentieth-century scholarship (Ostrogorsky, Obolensky) rehabilitated it as a centre of civilisation; more recent work (Herrin,
Kaldellis) emphasises its Roman identity, its political culture, and its interaction with the Islamic world and the West. Anthony Kaldellis has
argued that Byzantium was a "Roman republic" in which popular consent, and sometimes popular violence, legitimised emperors.{{ref:kaldellis-2015}}

:::aside Byzantine women in power
Irene (797–802), and the sisters Zoe and Theodora in the eleventh century, ruled in their own names; regents such as Theodora (842–856) and
empresses such as Anna Dalassene, Alexios I's mother, to whom he delegated government, exercised great power. The Roman empire of the Middle
Ages offers more examples of female rule than its ancient predecessor.
