## Introduction {#context}

Rome was one of the few societies in history in which slaves formed a large part of the population and were central to production, a "slave
society" in Moses Finley's sense.{{ref:finley-1980}} Slaves worked fields, mines, workshops and houses; they managed businesses, kept accounts,
taught children and ran the emperor's household administration. Slavery was sustained by violence and the law; at the same time Rome freed
slaves on a scale unparalleled in other slave societies, and freed slaves became citizens.

## Becoming a slave {#sources}

People became slaves through war (whole populations were enslaved after the sack of cities such as Carthage and Corinth), piracy and kidnapping,
trade from beyond the frontiers, birth to an enslaved mother, the exposure and collection of unwanted infants, and, in some places, self-sale or
sale of children. In the late Republic, war captives were probably the largest source; under the empire, birth and trade became more important.
The question of how slave populations were reproduced is central to estimating their numbers.{{ref:scheidel-1997}}

:::claim contested
How many slaves were there? Estimates for Italy at the end of the Republic range from about one to three million, perhaps 15–35 per cent of the
population; for the empire as a whole, commonly 10 per cent or somewhat more. All such figures are modern estimates built on assumptions, not
ancient counts.
:::

## The law and the whip {#law}

In law a slave was property (*res*), with no legal personality, no legal family and no rights. The master could sell, punish or (until the
emperors restricted it) kill a slave. Slaves' evidence in court was taken under torture. Slaves could, however, hold a *[[term:peculium]]*,
a fund that legally belonged to the master but which they managed and might use to buy their freedom.

Mastery depended on violence and fear: whipping, chains, branding, sale away from family, and crucifixion for runaways and rebels. When the
city prefect Pedanius Secundus was murdered by one of his slaves in AD 61, the law required the execution of all the slaves in the house, about
four hundred.

:::

The great slave revolts happened in Sicily (c. 135–132 and 104–100 BC) and in Italy under Spartacus (73–71 BC), a Thracian gladiator who escaped
from a training school at Capua and defeated several Roman armies before Crassus crushed his forces; six thousand captives were crucified along
the Appian Way. After that, there were no large slave revolts in the Roman world; slaves' resistance took the form of flight, sabotage, theft and
occasional violence against masters.

## Slaves' lives {#lives}

Experiences varied enormously. A slave in the mines of Spain or a chained agricultural gang lived in brutal conditions with short life
expectancy; a slave secretary in a senator's household or the emperor's administration might be educated, influential and wealthy, though still
subject to the master's will. Slaves formed families without legal recognition (*contubernium*), which could be broken by sale. Tombstones set up
by and for slaves record partners, children and fellow slaves, and offer glimpses of lives that literary sources ignore.{{ref:joshel-2010}}

## Manumission and freedpeople {#freedpeople}

Romans freed slaves frequently, especially urban household slaves, through formal procedures (before a magistrate, in the census, or by will) or
informally. Formal manumission by a citizen made the slave a citizen. Augustus restricted manumission: the lex Fufia Caninia (2 BC) limited the
number that could be freed by will, and the lex Aelia Sentia (AD 4) set minimum ages, creating a lesser status (Junian Latins) for those freed
informally or too young.

:::check sf-1
:::

Why free slaves? Manumission rewarded loyalty and gave others an incentive to work and obey; freed slaves often continued to work for their former
owners, now patrons, to whom they owed respect (*obsequium*) and sometimes services. Owners could profit from the purchase price. And many slaves
were freed to marry their owners or because of personal attachment.

Freedpeople were prominent in commerce and the crafts of Roman cities. In many towns wealthy freedmen served as *Augustales*, priests of the
imperial cult, gaining honour though barred from local office. Their tombs proudly record their trades.

:::source eurysaces-tomb
:::

Elite writers despised them. Petronius' fictional Trimalchio, a fabulously rich freedman who boasts of his fortune at a vulgar dinner party, is
a satirical caricature. Freedmen of the emperor, such as Claudius' secretaries Narcissus and Pallas, wielded great power and were especially
resented by senators.{{ref:mouritsen-2011}}

:::

## Hierarchy among the enslaved: Musicus Scurranus {#musicus}

Slavery in Rome included people of very different conditions. A funerary inscription from Rome commemorates Musicus Scurranus, a slave of the emperor Tiberius who served as cashier (*dispensator*) of the imperial treasury in the province of Gallia Lugdunensis. He died while on a visit to Rome, and his memorial was set up by sixteen of his own slaves (*vicarii*), who were with him when he died. They list their roles: a business agent, a manager of expenses, three secretaries, a doctor, two cooks, chamber servants, keepers of his silver plate and his clothes, footmen and a woman named Secunda.

Legally, these sixteen people belonged to Musicus' *peculium*, the fund that his master allowed him to manage, and therefore ultimately to the emperor. Musicus himself could be sold, punished or freed at Tiberius' will. Yet in practice he lived better than most free inhabitants of the empire, with a household of his own.

:::claim interpretation
The inscription shows how imperial administration relied on slaves and freedmen, and how slavery could combine total legal subjection with real wealth and authority. It also warns against generalising: Musicus' life had little in common with that of a chained labourer in a mine or on a great estate.
:::

## Traces of enslaved people {#traces}

Enslaved people left few texts, but their presence can be recovered from many kinds of evidence.

- **Collars.** Several bronze collars survive from late antique Rome with inscriptions identifying the wearer as a runaway and asking the finder to return him. One reads, in translation: "I have run away; hold me. When you have returned me to my master Zoninus, you receive a gold coin (*solidus*)." Such collars, some with Christian symbols, show how masters controlled people who had already tried to escape.
- **Epitaphs.** Slaves and freedpeople set up thousands of epitaphs, especially in the great communal tombs (*columbaria*) of Roman households, recording partners, children and fellow slaves, and the jobs they did.
- **Sale documents.** Papyri from Egypt and wax tablets from the gold-mining district of Roman Dacia record sales of slaves, including children, with guarantees that the person sold was healthy and not a runaway.
- **Architecture.** Small, dark rooms in Pompeian houses and villas, are interpreted as slave quarters; in 2021 excavators at the villa of Civita Giuliana, just outside Pompeii, found a small room with three simple beds, a chamber pot and harness gear, apparently home to a family of enslaved workers.
- **Literature by former slaves.** The fabulist Phaedrus and the Stoic philosopher Epictetus were former slaves; Epictetus taught that true slavery and freedom lay in the mind, a philosophy that both consoled and did nothing to challenge the institution.

:::claim interpretation
Sandra Joshel and others argue that these traces, read carefully, show enslaved people as agents who formed families, sought advancement, resisted and escaped, not only as the objects that the law and elite literature describe.{{ref:joshel-2010}} The evidence is fragmentary, and those who left traces are disproportionately urban household slaves, not the agricultural and mining slaves whose lives were hardest.
:::

## How slaves were acquired and sold {#trade}

The slave trade linked the empire's frontiers and wars to its markets. Captives from wars were sold by commanders to dealers who followed the armies. Traders bought people from beyond the frontiers, in the Black Sea region, Germany, Africa and Arabia. Within the empire, exposed infants were collected and raised as slaves, children were sometimes sold by desperate parents, and kidnapping was a constant fear. Markets at Delos, and later at Ephesus and in Rome itself, sold people with placards round their necks stating their origin, skills and defects, which the law required the seller to declare.

## Slavery in a Christian empire {#late}

Christianity did not abolish slavery. Christian writers urged masters to treat slaves humanely and slaves to obey; Constantine allowed
manumission in church; bishops and monasteries owned slaves. Augustine regarded slavery as a consequence of sin. Kyle Harper has shown that
slavery remained extensive and economically significant in the fourth century, correcting an older view that it had been replaced by
tied tenancy (the *colonate*).{{ref:harper-2011}}

## Historiography {#historiography}

The study of Roman slavery has been shaped by modern debates. Nineteenth-century abolitionists and their opponents both used ancient slavery as
an example. Marxist historians saw slavery as the basis of the ancient mode of production. Finley's *Ancient Slavery and Modern Ideology* (1980)
examined how modern ideologies shaped scholarship.{{ref:finley-1980}} Since then, historians such as Keith Bradley and Sandra Joshel have
foregrounded the violence of slavery and the perspectives of enslaved people, reading slaveholders' sources against the grain.

:::debate roman-economy
:::

:::aside Reading against the grain
Almost every text about Roman slaves was written by slaveholders. Historians "read against the grain" by asking what a text assumes, what
anxieties it reveals (fear of poisoning, of runaways), and what slaves' own monuments, curses, graffiti and legal cases show. Even a law
forbidding something shows that people did it.
:::
