## Historical context {#context}

After Sulla, the Republic carried on with an enlarged Senate, restored tribunes and a citizen body that now included Italy. Its
institutions continued to function: elections were held, laws passed, courts sat. But the scale of the empire's problems, pirates
across the Mediterranean, Mithridates in the East, slave revolt in Italy, rebellions in Spain, invited the people to vote large,
long commands to individual generals. The men who held them returned with wealth, clients and veterans far beyond the reach of any
ordinary senator.

## The age of Pompey, 78–60 BC {#pompey}

Pompey's career broke every rule of the cursus honorum. He raised a private army for Sulla at twenty-three, celebrated a triumph as a
young equestrian, commanded in Spain against Sertorius as a private citizen with proconsular imperium, and was elected consul for 70
without having held any previous office. In that year he and Crassus, who had destroyed the army of Spartacus in 71, restored the
tribunate's powers.

In 67 the tribune A. Gabinius passed a law giving Pompey a command against the pirates who were disrupting the grain supply: three
years, fifteen legates, 500 ships, and imperium over the whole sea and fifty miles inland. Pompey cleared the sea in three months. In
66 the lex Manilia, supported by Cicero, transferred to him the command against Mithridates. Pompey defeated the king, annexed Syria,
ended the Seleucid dynasty, captured Jerusalem (63) and reorganised the eastern provinces and client kingdoms, enormously increasing
Rome's revenues.

:::check cs-1
:::

Meanwhile, in Rome, the consul of 63, the new man M. Tullius Cicero, exposed a conspiracy led by L. Sergius Catilina, a bankrupt
patrician who had appealed to indebted veterans and ruined nobles. With the Senate's ultimate decree as authority and after a debate
in which Caesar urged life imprisonment and Cato the Younger execution, Cicero had five conspirators strangled without trial. He
regarded this as his greatest achievement; it would cost him exile.

:::people
catiline cato-younger clodius
:::

When Pompey returned in 62 and disbanded his army, the Senate, led by Cato and Lucullus, refused to ratify his eastern settlement or
grant land to his veterans. It was a demonstration that senatorial resistance could humble even the greatest general, and it pushed
Pompey into the arms of Caesar.

## Caesar's rise and the pact of 60 BC {#triumvirate}

C. Julius Caesar was a patrician from an old but undistinguished family, the nephew by marriage of Marius and son-in-law of Cinna. He
built popularity through lavish games as aedile, won election as pontifex maximus in 63 against senior rivals, borrowing heavily, and
governed Further Spain in 61. In 60 he made a private agreement with Pompey and Crassus: they would support his consulship, and he
would pass the measures each wanted. Pompey married Caesar's daughter Julia.

As consul in 59 Caesar passed land laws for Pompey's veterans and the ratification of his settlement, using the people's assembly and
ignoring the obstruction of his colleague M. Bibulus, who withdrew to his house and announced that he was watching the sky for omens,
a device that technically invalidated public business. The lex Vatinia gave Caesar Cisalpine Gaul and Illyricum for five years; the
Senate added Transalpine Gaul. His enemies maintained that all his laws were invalid and that he should be prosecuted the moment he
returned to private life.

## The conquest of Gaul, 58–50 BC {#gaul}

Caesar used a migration of the Helvetii in 58 as the opening for a war that, over eight years, brought all of Gaul between the
Pyrenees, the Alps and the Rhine under Roman control. He bridged the Rhine and twice crossed to Britain (55, 54). In 52 the Arvernian
noble Vercingetorix united much of Gaul in revolt; Caesar besieged him at Alesia, built double siege lines against the town and a
relieving army, and forced his surrender.

:::claim contested
Ancient writers record that Caesar's wars in Gaul killed a million people and enslaved another million. These are round numbers
from hostile or admiring sources (Pliny the Elder, Plutarch, Velleius) and cannot be verified. Some historians use the figures and
Caesar's own accounts of massacres, such as that of the Usipetes and Tencteri in 55 BC, to describe the conquest as genocidal; even in
Rome, Cato proposed that Caesar be handed over to the Germans for breach of faith.
:::

Caesar's *Commentaries* on the Gallic War, written in plain third-person Latin, reported each year's campaign to the Roman public and
built his reputation. They are our main source and also a work of political self-presentation.{{ref:caesar-hammond-1996}}

## Breakdown in Rome, 58–50 BC {#breakdown}

Politics in Rome grew more violent. P. Clodius Pulcher, a patrician who had transferred to the plebs, was tribune in 58. He made the
grain ration free, legalised neighbourhood associations (*collegia*) that he used to mobilise his supporters, and exiled Cicero for
executing citizens without trial. His rival T. Annius Milo organised gangs of his own. The three partners renewed their pact at Luca
in 56: Caesar's command was extended for five years; Pompey and Crassus held a second joint consulship in 55 and received great
provinces, Spain and Syria.

Then the arrangement unravelled. Julia died in childbirth in 54. Crassus invaded Parthia and was killed at Carrhae in 53, with most
of his army. In January 52 Milo's men killed Clodius on the Appian Way; Clodius' supporters burned the Senate house as his pyre. The
Senate, with Cato's agreement, made Pompey sole consul to restore order. Pompey now moved closer to Caesar's enemies.

## Rubicon {#rubicon}

The crisis of 50–49 turned on a narrow issue with existential stakes. Caesar wanted to pass straight from his Gallic command to a
second consulship (permitted from 48), standing for election in absentia, so that he would never be a private citizen whom his
enemies could prosecute. His enemies wanted him to give up his army first. Proposals for both Caesar and Pompey to lay down their
commands won overwhelming votes in the Senate in December 50 but were blocked. On 7 January 49 the Senate passed the ultimate decree;
the tribunes who had vetoed measures against Caesar, M. Antonius among them, fled to his camp.

On about 10 January Caesar crossed the Rubicon, the boundary between his province and Italy, with a single legion. Suetonius has him
say "the die is cast".{{anc:Suet. Iul. 32–33}} Pompey and most of the magistrates abandoned Rome and then Italy. In his own account
Caesar explains what he was fighting for.

## Civil war and dictatorship, 49–44 BC {#dictatorship}

Caesar won with astonishing speed. He took Italy in two months, pardoning opponents who surrendered at Corfinium, a policy of
clemency (*[[term:clementia]]*) designed to contrast with Sulla's proscriptions. He defeated Pompey's legates in Spain, crossed to
Greece, and in August 48 defeated Pompey at Pharsalus. Pompey fled to Egypt, where the advisers of the young Ptolemy XIII had him
murdered on the shore. Caesar, arriving in Alexandria, was besieged there for months, took the side of Cleopatra VII in her dynastic
war with her brother, and in 47 defeated Pharnaces of Pontus at Zela so quickly that he reported it in three words, "I came, I saw, I
conquered".{{anc:Suet. Iul. 37}} The republican resistance was crushed at Thapsus in Africa (46), after which Cato killed himself at
Utica rather than accept Caesar's pardon, and at Munda in Spain (45).

As dictator, repeatedly and finally (early in 44) for life, Caesar carried out a wide programme. He reformed the calendar, creating
the 365-day Julian year; settled veterans and urban poor in colonies, including new foundations at Carthage and Corinth; enlarged the
Senate to about 900 with men from Italy and the provinces; increased the number of magistrates; gave citizenship to the Transpadane
Gauls; and planned a great campaign against Parthia.

:::claim contested
Did Caesar want to be king? In February 44, at the Lupercalia, Antony offered him a diadem, which he refused, and he was said to have
replied to a crowd hailing him as king that he was "Caesar, not Rex". Some historians read these as tests of opinion, others as
attempts to scotch rumours. His honours (a gilded chair, statues among the kings, a priest of his own cult, his portrait on coins in
his lifetime) went far beyond anything a Republican magistrate had received, whatever title he used.
:::

## The Ides of March {#ides}

On 15 March 44, three days before he was due to leave for Parthia, Caesar was stabbed to death at a meeting of the Senate in the
Curia of Pompey's theatre complex by some sixty conspirators led by M. Junius Brutus and C. Cassius Longinus. Many had fought for
Pompey and been pardoned; some were Caesar's own officers. They called themselves liberators and expected the Republic to resume.

The Republic did not resume. The conspirators had no plan for the day after; the consul Antony and Caesar's eighteen-year-old heir
Octavian, with the dead dictator's veterans and the urban crowd, turned Caesar's funeral and will into a political force against them.

## Causation: why did the Republic fall? {#causation}

:::debate fall-of-republic
:::

Explanations fall into a few families.

- **Personal ambition.** Ancient writers, especially Sallust, Lucan and Plutarch, explained the collapse by ambition and greed:
  Pompey could tolerate no equal, Caesar no superior. Ronald Syme recast this as the competition of an oligarchy whose members
  could no longer contain their greatest men.{{ref:syme-1939}}
- **Structural crisis.** Christian Meier described a "crisis without alternative": everyone could see something was wrong but no
  group had an interest in or a vision of a different system. Brunt emphasised the grievances of the poor, veterans and Italians.
- **Contingency.** Erich Gruen argued that institutions worked well into the 50s: elections, courts and legislation continued, and
  the civil war resulted from a specific miscalculation in 50–49 rather than long decay.{{ref:gruen-1974}}
- **Legitimacy and the people.** Robert Morstein-Marx has argued that Caesar's actions in 49 were seen by many Romans, including
  most of his soldiers and the urban plebs, as a defence of the people's rights against a faction, which explains his support.{{ref:morstein-marx-2021}}

:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The Ides of March produced fourteen more years of civil war, ended only by Actium. Caesar's career provided a model, and a warning,
for his heir: power had to rest on the army and the people, but its holder had to avoid the appearance of kingship and leave room for
the nobility's pride. Augustus would solve that problem where Caesar had not.

## Historiography {#historiography}

This is the best-documented period of ancient history before the Principate: we have Cicero's speeches, treatises and some 900
letters; Caesar's Commentaries; Sallust's monographs; and later narratives by Plutarch, Suetonius, Appian and Cassius Dio. The
quantity of evidence brings its own danger: we see the period largely through Cicero's eyes, with his friendships, enmities and
self-justification. Reading Cicero's letters against his public speeches is one of the best exercises in source criticism available
to an ancient historian.

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside The view from the provinces
Most of our sources look from Rome. To see the late Republic from elsewhere, consider Cicero's prosecution of Verres (70 BC) for his
plundering of Sicily, Caesar's own descriptions of Gallic peoples, and the cities of Asia that paid Sulla's indemnity and then
Pompey's taxes. The politics of the capital were financed by the provinces.
:::
