## Categories of difference {#context}

Romans described human difference in many ways: by people or nation (*gens*, *natio*), by language, customs, religion, dress and appearance,
by legal status (citizen, foreigner, slave), and by the broad cultural opposition between the civilised and the "barbarian". They did not have
a concept of "race" in the modern biological sense, which developed in Europe from the seventeenth century, and the question of whether their
ideas resembled modern racism is a matter of debate.

## Environment and character {#environment}

Greek and Roman writers often explained the characters of peoples by climate and geography. Northern peoples, in cold, wet lands, were said to be
brave but unintelligent; southern peoples, in hot lands, clever but cowardly; Italy, in the temperate middle, produced a people ideally suited to
rule. Vitruvius sets out such a theory to explain why Rome was destined to govern the world.{{anc:Vitr. De arch. 6.1.10–11}} Such ideas
attributed collective traits to whole peoples, and some writers suggested that these traits could be inherited.

Benjamin Isaac argued that these ideas amounted to "proto-racism", the precursors of modern racism.{{ref:isaac-2004}} Erich Gruen replied that
Greek and Roman texts more often imagined connections and kinship with other peoples than hostility, and that stereotypes coexisted with
admiration and integration.{{ref:gruen-2011}} Frank Snowden's earlier work had argued that colour prejudice of the modern type was absent in
antiquity;{{ref:snowden-1970}} others, such as Denise McCoskey, ask how "race" can be used as an analytical category for antiquity without
importing modern assumptions.{{ref:mccoskey-2012}}

## A civic identity {#civic}

Roman identity was, in principle, defined by citizenship and participation rather than descent. Rome's foundation legends told of a city formed
from refugees, Trojans and Sabines; Romans prided themselves on having incorporated outsiders. Citizenship expanded to Latins, Italians, and
eventually, in 212, to almost all free inhabitants of the empire. Emperors came from Spain, Gaul, Africa, Syria, Arabia, Pannonia and Illyricum.

Yet prejudice was everywhere: against Greeks as frivolous and untrustworthy, Syrians and Egyptians as servile, Gauls and Germans as wild, Jews for
their separateness. Roman writers stereotyped provincials and barbarians, and Roman soldiers at Vindolanda called local Britons by a dismissive
nickname.

## Outsiders at the top {#outsiders}

Roman citizenship was a legal and political status, not an ethnic one, and men of provincial origin could rise to the highest offices. Some careers show how far, and where the limits lay.

- **Lucius Cornelius Balbus**, from Gades (Cádiz) in Spain, received citizenship from Pompey, became Caesar's agent and in 40 BC the first consul born outside Italy. Cicero defended his citizenship in court when it was challenged.
- **Quintus Lollius Urbicus**, from the small town of Tiddis in Numidia, probably of local African descent, rose through the army and the Senate to govern Britain for Antoninus Pius, building the Antonine Wall, and later became prefect of the city of Rome.
- **Philip "the Arab"**, born at Shahba in southern Syria, became emperor in 244 and in 248 presided over the celebrations of Rome's thousandth birthday. He renamed his home town Philippopolis and built it as a Roman city.
- **Maximinus "the Thracian"**, emperor 235–238, a soldier from the Balkan frontier, was portrayed by senatorial writers as a half-barbarian giant of low birth, a portrait that tells us more about senatorial snobbery than about his origins (see [[lesson:third-century-crisis#emperors|emperors made by soldiers]]).

:::claim interpretation
These careers show the openness of the Roman elite to wealthy, educated and loyal provincials, an openness that impressed later observers and that Claudius invoked in his speech on the Gauls. They also show that prejudice did not disappear: it attached to class, culture and manners, as the hostile portraits of Maximinus show, more than to descent as such.
:::

## Slavery and colour {#slavery}

Roman slaves came from every part of the known world and beyond, and were not identified by skin colour; Italians, Greeks, Gauls and Syrians were
all enslaved. Slavery was not racialised as Atlantic slavery would be, and freed slaves became citizens.

:::check ra-2
:::

People from sub-Saharan Africa appear in Roman art and literature, sometimes in stereotyped ways (as "Ethiopians", a Greek term meaning "burnt
faces"), sometimes not. Interpreting these images is difficult: a dark-skinned figure on a mosaic may represent an African, a stock type, or a
symbol.

## Images of difference {#images}

Look more closely at the visual evidence. Mosaics, paintings and small bronzes depict Black Africans as servants, bath attendants, performers, soldiers and mythological figures; some are sympathetic and individual, others stereotyped or caricatured. Roman writers mention dark skin, sometimes neutrally, sometimes as a joke or an omen. Frank Snowden argued that these images reveal curiosity and stereotype but not the systematic colour prejudice of modern racism; critics reply that his reading was too optimistic and underplayed hostility.{{ref:snowden-1970}}

:::claim contested
Whether Roman attitudes to skin colour amounted to racism is debated. The evidence includes both positive and negative representations, and much depends on how "racism" is defined: as any prejudice based on appearance or origin, or as a system of hierarchy based on supposedly fixed biological difference. Most scholars agree that the latter, in the modern sense, did not exist in antiquity.
:::

## The diversity of the Roman world {#diversity}

New scientific methods have shown the mobility of the Roman population. Isotope analysis of skeletons, which can indicate where a person grew up,
has identified people of African and Mediterranean origin buried at York and other sites in Roman Britain, such as a well-to-do woman of the
fourth century known as the "Ivory Bangle Lady". A genetic study of ancient individuals from Rome and central Italy published in 2019 found that
the population of the imperial city drew heavily on ancestry from the eastern Mediterranean and Near East, consistent with the movement of
slaves, merchants and migrants to the capital. Inscriptions record soldiers, traders and slaves moving across the empire, from Syria to
Britain and from Africa to the Danube.

:::check ra-4
:::

:::claim interpretation
Ancient DNA and isotope studies offer powerful evidence for mobility and ancestry, but they are based on small samples of individuals whose
burials happen to survive and have been studied. They tell us about ancestry and childhood residence, not about identity: how a person saw
themselves, or how others saw them, depended on culture, status and language.
:::

## Modern uses and misuses {#modern}

Rome has been appropriated by modern ideologies of race and empire. Nineteenth-century European imperialists claimed the Roman legacy to justify
their own empires. Tacitus' *Germania*, which claimed that the Germans were an unmixed people, was used by German nationalists and later by the
Nazis. Fascist Italy promoted "romanità" as a racial and imperial ideal. Today, some white supremacist groups use imagery of classical antiquity
to claim a "Western" racial heritage, a claim that historians have shown to be a distortion of the diverse Mediterranean world of the Roman
empire.{{ref:mccoskey-2012}}

## Case study: Tacitus' Germania {#germania}

In AD 98 Tacitus wrote a short ethnography of the peoples beyond the Rhine. Among much else, he claimed that the Germans were indigenous and unmixed by intermarriage with other peoples, and so had a uniform physical appearance: fierce blue eyes, reddish hair and large bodies.{{anc:Tac. Germ. 4}} The work also praised German simplicity and chastity, partly as a moral reproach to Roman luxury.

The text survived in a single manuscript, rediscovered in a German monastery in the fifteenth century. German humanists used it to give their nation a noble ancient ancestry; nineteenth-century nationalists celebrated it; and in the twentieth century Nazi ideologues treated Tacitus' remark about unmixed blood as ancient testimony to racial purity. Christopher Krebs has traced this history; his title, *A Most Dangerous Book*, takes up Arnaldo Momigliano's judgement that the Germania belongs among the most dangerous books ever written.{{ref:krebs-2011}} The case shows how an ancient author's literary and moral purposes could be turned, by later readers, into racial doctrine he never held.

## Historiography {#historiography}

The study of race and ethnicity in antiquity has grown rapidly since the 1990s, prompted by debates over the place of classics in modern culture,
post-colonial theory, and new scientific evidence. It raises questions about the discipline itself: who studies antiquity, whose heritage it is
claimed to be, and how scholarly categories can reinforce or challenge modern hierarchies.{{ref:kennedy-roy-goldman-2013}}

:::aside A note on Severus
Septimius Severus is sometimes called "the first Black emperor" in popular media. He was born in Lepcis Magna to an elite family of Punic and
Italian descent; ancient sources do not describe his skin colour; the painted panel of his family from Egypt shows him with a darker complexion
than his wife, but painting conventions make this hard to interpret. The claim reveals the modern desire to find representation in the past,
which is understandable, but the evidence does not support certainty either way.
:::
