## The household {#context}

The Roman *familia* in law meant everyone under the power of the *[[term:paterfamilias]]*: his children, his sons' children, and his slaves. In
everyday usage, the *domus* meant the household or the extended family of kin by blood and marriage. The paterfamilias held *patria potestas*
over his descendants for as long as he lived: in law they could own nothing, and in early law he had the power of life and death. Yet Roman
families were not simply despotisms. Custom, affection, the family council and, later, imperial law limited fathers' powers, and demography
limited their duration.

## Demography {#demography}

Roman life expectancy at birth was probably between twenty and thirty years, mainly because perhaps a third or more of infants died in their
first year, and many more before adulthood. Those who reached adulthood could expect to live into their fifties or sixties. Richard Saller's
computer simulations of Roman families suggest that many Romans lost their fathers before reaching adulthood, and that relatively few men in
their late twenties still had a living father, so the lifelong power of fathers was, for most, a short-lived reality.{{ref:saller-1994}}

Inscriptions suggest that elite women typically married for the first time in their mid- to late teens and men in their mid- to late twenties;
among non-elites the ages were probably similar or somewhat later. Infant exposure was legal and practised, though how widely is debated.

## Marriage and divorce {#marriage}

Roman marriage required the consent of the partners (and of those in whose power they were) and the intention to be married; no state or
religious ceremony was legally required. In early Rome, wives often passed into their husband's power (*manus*); by the late Republic marriage
without *manus* was the norm, so a wife's property remained hers or her father's, and she brought a dowry (*[[term:dos]]*) that had to be
returned on divorce or death. Divorce was easy for either partner and common among the elite, where marriages formed political alliances.

:::

The ideal Roman wife was a *univira*, married to one man, chaste, modest and devoted to her household; she spun wool and was praised for it on
tombstones. Real marriages could be partnerships of great affection, as one remarkable eulogy shows.

Augustus' marriage laws made marriage and childbearing among the propertied classes a matter of state policy, and adultery a public crime.

## Women in law and economy {#law}

Women could not vote or hold office. Under the guardianship of women (*tutela mulierum*), adult women not in a father's power needed a guardian's
authorisation for important legal acts, but by the late Republic this was often a formality, and Augustus freed women with three children (freedwomen
with four) from it. Women could own, inherit and manage property, lend money and run businesses. Elite women controlled large fortunes; the
inscriptions of Pompeii record the public benefactions of Eumachia, a priestess who built a large building on the forum for the fullers, and
documents record women as landowners and creditors.

Non-elite women worked: as shopkeepers, innkeepers, weavers, midwives, wet-nurses, hairdressers and in many other trades, as epitaphs record.
Enslaved women were vulnerable to sexual exploitation by their owners, which the law did not regard as a crime against them.

## Women's work {#work}

Elite literature presents the ideal woman as a wife who spun wool at home. Inscriptions, reliefs and papyri show women of the lower orders working in many trades. Epitaphs from Rome name women as midwives, wet-nurses, hairdressers, seamstresses, sellers of fish, vegetables and perfume, innkeepers and doctors. Many were slaves or freedwomen working in great households; others ran small businesses with their husbands. A tomb at the cemetery of Isola Sacra, near Ostia, belonging to the midwife Scribonia Attice, was decorated with a terracotta relief showing a woman giving birth on a birthing chair, assisted by a midwife and a helper. Reliefs from Ostia show women selling vegetables and poultry from stalls.

In Egypt, papyri record women owning and leasing land, lending money, and running textile workshops. In the countryside, most women's work, in fields, with animals and in the household, left little trace beyond the bones and teeth studied by bioarchaeologists, which often show heavy physical labour.

:::claim interpretation
Historians debate how far these sources represent women's work generally. Women who commemorated their trade on a tomb were proud of it and could afford a monument; the many women whose work was unpaid household labour, or who were enslaved, are less visible. Still, the evidence refutes the idea, encouraged by moralising literature, that Roman women did not work outside the home.
:::

## Women and public life {#public}

Although excluded from formal politics, elite women could exercise influence through family networks, patronage and wealth. Cornelia, mother of
the Gracchi, became a model of the educated Roman matron. Fulvia, wife of Clodius and then Antony, raised troops during the Perusine war.
Imperial women such as Livia, Agrippina the Younger and Julia Domna held public honours and real power, which hostile historians portrayed as
illegitimate scheming. In religion, women held priesthoods, most famously the Vestals, and participated in festivals and cults.

At the edge of the empire, officers' wives kept up friendships across the forts.

In the provinces, women of wealthy families served as priestesses and benefactors of their cities and were honoured with statues and
inscriptions; Emily Hemelrijk's work shows how common such public roles were in the Latin West.{{ref:hemelrijk-2015}}

## Women in their own words and actions {#voices}

Most texts about Roman women were written by men, but some sources bring us closer to women's own actions and voices.

**Hortensia.** In 42 BC the triumvirs imposed a special tax on 1,400 of the richest women in Rome to pay for the civil war. The women went to the Forum, and Hortensia, daughter of the great orator Hortensius, addressed the triumvirs. According to Appian, she asked why women should pay for wars in which they had no share of honours, offices or command; the tax was reduced to 400 women.{{anc:App. B Civ. 4.32–34}} Quintilian, writing more than a century later, says that her speech was still read, and not merely as a compliment to her sex.{{anc:Quint. Inst. 1.1.6}}

**Julia Felix.** At Pompeii a painted notice advertised that in the property of Julia Felix, daughter of Spurius, an elegant bath, shops with rooms above, and apartments were available to rent for five years. It shows a woman managing urban property as a business.

**Plancia Magna.** In Perge, in southern Asia Minor, in the reign of Hadrian, Plancia Magna, priestess and civic magistrate, paid for the monumental city gate with statues of her family and of the city's founders. She was honoured in inscriptions as a benefactor of the city, one of many provincial women who held public honours through wealth and priesthoods.

**Perpetua.** At Carthage in 203 a young mother of good family, imprisoned as a Christian, wrote an account of her imprisonment, her conflicts with her father and her visions, which was incorporated into the *Passion of Perpetua and Felicity*. It is one of the very few surviving Latin texts written by a woman, and the earliest known by a Christian woman.{{ref:perpetua-heffernan-2012}}

## Childhood {#childhood}

Childhood was dangerous. Many infants died in their first year, and parents, especially elite parents, might hand babies to wet-nurses, often slaves. Fathers decided whether to raise a newborn; unwanted infants could be exposed, and some were collected and raised as slaves. Children of poor families worked from an early age, and apprenticeship contracts from Egypt show boys placed with weavers and other craftsmen for several years. Elite children had tutors, played with toys such as dolls, nuts and hoops, and were commemorated by grieving parents in touching epitaphs. The legal minimum age of marriage was twelve for girls and fourteen for boys, though most girls married in their later teens.{{ref:dixon-1992}}

## Christianity and Byzantium {#late}

Christianity offered women new roles and ideals: martyrs such as Perpetua, ascetics and patrons of monasteries, and benefactors of churches, but
also excluded them from the priesthood and promoted ideals of virginity that challenged traditional expectations of marriage. In late antiquity
and Byzantium, empresses such as Galla Placidia, Theodora, Irene, and later Zoe and Anna Dalassene, wielded political power, and Anna Komnene
wrote one of the major histories of the Middle Ages. The philosopher Hypatia taught in Alexandria until her murder in 415.

## Historiography {#historiography}

The history of Roman women developed from the 1970s with sourcebooks such as Lefkowitz and Fant and studies of law and family (Treggiari,
Dixon, Saller). Gender history now asks how masculinity and femininity were constructed, how ideals of the matron and the virile man shaped
politics, and how sources written by men represent women. Demographic and legal approaches have shown how the family worked in practice.
Inscriptions, papyri and archaeology allow access to non-elite women.{{ref:dixon-1992}}

:::aside The "bad woman" trope
Roman historians often explain political crises by the ambition, lust or cruelty of women: Tullia in the regal period, Fulvia, Messalina,
Agrippina. Such portraits served to criticise the men associated with them and to mark the boundaries of acceptable female behaviour. When
reading them, ask what the story allows the author to say about the emperor or the regime.
:::
