## A name imposed {#context}

Nobody in the medieval Roman empire called themselves "Byzantine". Its inhabitants called themselves *Rhomaioi*, Romans, and their state the
*politeia* or *basileia* of the Romans; its emperors were "emperor of the Romans" (*basileus ton Rhomaion*). Its neighbours agreed: to Arabs,
Persians and Turks, it was *Rum*, Rome, and the Seljuk sultanate that took over much of its Anatolian territory was the "Sultanate of Rum".
"Byzantion" was the old Greek name of the city refounded by Constantine, and "Byzantines" in Byzantine sources were the inhabitants of
Constantinople.

The use of "Byzantine" for the empire as a whole was popularised by western scholars in the sixteenth century; the German humanist
Hieronymus Wolf published a collection of its historians as a "Byzantine" corpus in 1557. Western Europeans of the Middle Ages, meanwhile, had
long called its people "Greeks" and their state the "empire of the Greeks", a usage that served to deny their Roman claims, especially after
the coronation of Charlemagne as Roman emperor in 800.

## Continuity {#continuity}

The case for calling the medieval empire Roman rests on continuities that were obvious to its inhabitants.

- **The state.** The empire was ruled continuously from Constantinople from 330 to 1204 and again from 1261 to 1453. There was no conquest,
  no new founding. Its emperors claimed descent, in office if not in blood, from Augustus and Constantine.
- **Law.** Justinian's codification remained the basis of law; it was adapted into Greek in the *Basilika* around 900 and continued to be
  used and studied.
- **Institutions.** A Senate, the titles of patrician and consul (eventually as court dignities), the circus factions, imperial acclamations,
  the gold coinage and an administration of salaried officials descended from late Roman models.
- **Identity.** The inhabitants called themselves Romans, wrote Roman history and saw their armies as Roman armies.

## Change {#change}

The case for a separate label rests on transformations that were equally real.

- **Language.** By the seventh century Greek had replaced Latin as the language of government, law and the army, although Latin titles and
  terms survived for centuries.
- **Religion.** The medieval empire was a Christian, Orthodox state whose identity was bound up with its Church, icons, monasteries and
  liturgy.
- **Geography.** After the seventh century the empire was centred on Anatolia, the Aegean and the Balkans; Rome itself was lost to it from
  the eighth century.
- **Society.** Cities, economy, army and aristocracy were medieval rather than ancient in form.

## Who were the Romans of Byzantium? {#identity}

Anthony Kaldellis has argued that by the middle Byzantine period "Roman" had become not just a political or religious identity but an ethnic
one: the Romans were a people defined by language (Greek), religion (Orthodoxy), customs and a sense of shared descent, distinct from Armenians,
Bulgarians, Slavs or Latins living in or near the empire.{{ref:kaldellis-2019}} Others argue that "Roman" identity was above all an ideology of
the state and the elite, and that we know little about how peasants in the provinces saw themselves. Either way, the evidence shows that being
"Roman" mattered to the people who claimed it.

## Romans in their own words {#voices}

How did medieval Romans describe themselves and others? A few examples show the range of the evidence.

- **The emperor's handbook.** In the mid-tenth century Constantine VII compiled for his son a confidential guide to foreign peoples, known as *On the Administration of the Empire*. Among its advice: never give foreign rulers imperial robes, crowns or the secret of Greek fire, and never marry into foreign royal families, with the exception of the Franks, because Constantine the Great himself had come from their lands.{{anc:Const. Porph. DAI 13}} The emperor writes as the ruler of Romans, set apart from all other nations.
- **Anna Komnene.** Writing about the crusaders, Alexios I's daughter calls her own people Romans and the westerners "Latins", "Franks" or "Kelts", barbarians who were brave but greedy and unreliable.
- **Their neighbours.** Arabic and Persian writers called the empire and its people *Rum*; the Seljuk state in Anatolia became the "Sultanate of Rum"; and for centuries after 1453 Greek-speaking Orthodox Christians under Ottoman rule were known as *Rum* and called themselves *Romioi*.

:::claim interpretation
The persistence of the Roman name across these very different sources, imperial, literary and foreign, is strong evidence that "Roman" was a lived identity and not just an official title. It does not tell us how a farmer in Anatolia understood it, since the sources speak for the educated and the powerful.
:::

## Why the name stuck {#historiography}

The term "Byzantine" acquired negative connotations in the Enlightenment. Montesquieu and Voltaire saw Byzantium as a decadent, superstitious
autocracy; Gibbon described its history as a "tedious and uniform tale of weakness and misery". "Byzantine" became an adjective for
deviousness and bureaucratic complexity. Separating "Byzantium" from "Rome" also allowed western Europeans to claim the classical Roman
heritage as their own while relegating the eastern empire to the margins of history.

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the field was rehabilitated: historians such as George Ostrogorsky, Dimitri Obolensky, Averil
Cameron and Judith Herrin emphasised Byzantium's achievements and influence.{{ref:herrin-2007}} Greek national historiography, from Konstantinos
Paparrigopoulos in the nineteenth century, incorporated Byzantium into a continuous history of the Greek nation. Some scholars now advocate
replacing "Byzantine" with "eastern Roman" or "medieval Roman"; others keep it as a conventional term while acknowledging its history.{{ref:cameron-2014}}

## When did the Roman empire end? {#end}

The question has many defensible answers, each implying a different idea of what Rome was:

- **476**, if Rome means the western empire and the city of Rome's political world.
- **1204**, if Rome means the continuous state centred on Constantinople.
- **1453**, if it means the last emperor ruling from Constantinople, or **1461**, if it includes Trebizond.
- **1806**, if the Holy Roman Empire's claim counts.
- **1922**, if one follows the Ottoman sultans' claim to be Caesars of Rome.

## Who inherited Rome? {#heirs}

After 1453 several powers claimed the Roman inheritance. Mehmed II took the title *Kayser-i Rum*, Caesar of Rome, ruled from Constantine's city, and patronised Greek scholars; his court historian Kritoboulos, a Greek, wrote his history as the deeds of a new emperor. In the Holy Roman Empire, a line of emperors going back to Charlemagne's coronation in 800 continued to call themselves Roman emperors until 1806. In Moscow, Ivan III married Sophia (Zoe) Palaiologina, the niece of the last emperor, in 1472, and the Muscovite court adopted Byzantine ceremonial and the double-headed eagle. In the early sixteenth century the monk Philotheus of Pskov wrote to the grand prince that two Romes had fallen, that Moscow was the third, and that there would be no fourth.

:::claim interpretation
Each claim answered a different question about what Rome was: a city and its empire (the Ottomans), the imperial title in the Latin West (the Holy Roman Empire), or the Orthodox Christian empire (Moscow). That so many rulers wanted the name shows the enduring prestige of Rome (compare the [[lesson:legacy#appropriations|modern appropriations]]); that the claims were incompatible shows that "Rome" had become an idea that could be detached from any single state.
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

How we name the medieval Roman empire shapes how it is taught: as part of "Roman history", it extends the story of Rome by a thousand years
and shifts its centre to the eastern Mediterranean; as "Byzantine history", it becomes part of medieval or Greek history. This course has
followed the first approach while using "Byzantine" as a convenient label, because both the continuities and the transformations are needed
to understand a state that called itself Roman for more than two thousand years.

:::aside Rome in the medieval imagination
Medieval Constantinople was full of ancient Roman objects and memories: the column of Constantine, the obelisk and serpent column in the
Hippodrome, statues of emperors, and the walls of Theodosius. Its people told stories about them, sometimes misidentifying them, often
treating them as prophetic. The past was not a separate world but part of the city's living landscape.
:::
