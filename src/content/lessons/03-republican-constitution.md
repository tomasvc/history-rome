## Historical context {#context}

By the early third century BC the institutions of the Republic had reached the form that Polybius described around 150 BC and that
Roman writers later idealised. The Conflict of the Orders was over; the patricio-plebeian nobility competed for office within agreed
rules; and Rome was about to embark on overseas war against Carthage. The "constitution" was not a written document. It was a set
of offices, councils and assemblies, plus the customs and precedents that governed their use: the [[term:mos-maiorum|mos maiorum]],
the "custom of the ancestors".

This lesson describes how the system worked in practice between the lex Hortensia (287 BC) and the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus
(133 BC), the period that later Romans looked back on as the Republic at its best.

## The magistrates {#institutions}

:::diagram cursus-honorum
:::

Roman magistrates were elected annually, served without pay, and held office in colleges of two or more, each able to block his
colleagues. These two principles, annuality and collegiality, were the main safeguards against a return to kingship.

**Consuls.** The two consuls were the chief magistrates. They held [[term:imperium]], the power to command armies and to coerce, and
in this period they usually spent their year commanding in the field. They presided over the Senate and the centuriate assembly.
Twelve attendants (lictors) carried the fasces before them, rods bound around an axe that symbolised the power to beat and execute.

**Praetors.** From 367 BC a praetor took charge of justice in the city; a second was added around 242 BC to deal with foreigners, and
from 227 BC further praetors were elected to govern Sicily and Sardinia, then the Spanish provinces. Praetors also held imperium.

**Promagistrates.** As Rome's commitments grew, the Senate extended (prorogued) the imperium of consuls and praetors after their year
of office, so that they could continue to command or govern as proconsuls and propraetors. Prorogation allowed a small number of
annual officials to run an empire, but it also gave individuals long commands far from Rome.

**Quaestors and aediles.** Quaestors managed finance in Rome and for commanders in the field. Aediles supervised streets, markets,
the grain supply and public games, which gave ambitious men a chance to win popularity before standing for the praetorship.

**Tribunes of the plebs.** The ten tribunes, elected by plebeians only, could veto any magistrate, propose laws to the plebeian
assembly, and protect citizens. In the middle Republic most tribunes were young nobles at the start of their careers and cooperated
with the Senate. The office's potential as a vehicle for opposition remained latent until 133 BC.

**Censors.** Every five years or so two senior ex-consuls were elected as censors for eighteen months. They held the [[term:census]],
assigning citizens to property classes and tribes, and revised the membership of the Senate, expelling men for misconduct. Their
moral authority was considerable; Cato's harsh censorship of 184 BC became legendary.

**Dictator.** In emergencies a consul could appoint a dictator with overriding imperium for up to six months. The office was used
often in the early Republic and during the war with Hannibal, then fell into disuse after 202 BC until Sulla revived it in a new form.

:::check rc-1
:::

The sequence of offices, the [[term:cursus-honorum]], was regulated by the lex Villia annalis of 180 BC, which set minimum ages and
required a gap between offices. The system funnelled ambition: twenty or so young men each year might become quaestors, but only
two could become consul. The intense competition that resulted drove Roman aristocrats to seek military glory and popular favour.

## The Senate {#institutions-senate}

The [[term:senate|Senate]] of about 300 members consisted largely of ex-magistrates, enrolled for life by the censors. It had no
power to pass laws. It met when summoned by a magistrate, debated in order of rank, and issued advice in the form of decrees
([[term:senatus-consultum|senatus consulta]]). Yet in practice its advice was almost always followed, and it controlled the
treasury, the assignment of provinces and armies, the reception of embassies and foreign policy, and religious matters.

Why did magistrates obey? Because nearly every magistrate had been, or hoped to be, a senator for life; because the Senate collectively
embodied the experience of everyone who had held office; and because its [[term:auctoritas]] (an authority beyond legal power) was
part of the mos maiorum. A consul who defied the Senate would face colleagues, tribunes and his own future peers.

## The assemblies {#institutions-assemblies}

The Roman people exercised sovereignty in assemblies that could only vote on proposals put to them by a magistrate, without debate
from the floor. Speeches were delivered beforehand in informal meetings (*contiones*), where magistrates and their allies addressed
the crowd.

:::diagram centuriate-assembly
:::

- The **centuriate assembly** ([[term:comitia-centuriata|comitia centuriata]]) elected consuls, praetors and censors, declared war,
  and heard capital charges. Citizens voted in "centuries" grouped by wealth and age. The wealthiest voted first, and voting stopped
  when a majority of centuries was reached.
- The **tribal assembly** ([[term:comitia-tributa|comitia tributa]]) of all citizens, organised in 35 territorial tribes, elected
  quaestors and curule aediles and could pass laws.
- The **plebeian assembly** ([[term:concilium-plebis|concilium plebis]]), also organised by tribes, elected tribunes and plebeian
  aediles and passed plebiscites, which after 287 BC bound everyone. Most legislation of the later Republic took this form.

:::check rc-2
:::

Voting by tribe also had a bias, though a different one. The four urban tribes held the mass of the city population, while the 31
rural tribes covered the countryside; a citizen living far from Rome rarely made the journey, so the votes of rural tribes were often
cast by the wealthier landowners who could.

## Polybius' mixed constitution {#causation}

:::diagram mixed-constitution
:::

Polybius, writing as a Greek who knew the Roman elite intimately, explained Rome's success by its constitution, which mixed the three
classical forms: the monarchic element (consuls), the aristocratic (Senate) and the democratic (the people). Each could check and
assist the others, so the whole was stable.

:::source polybius-constitution
Polybius' analysis is the starting point for every modern account, and for every critique.
:::

:::check rc-4
:::

What does Polybius leave out? Modern historians point first to informal power. Roman society was laced with relationships of
patronage ([[term:clientela|clientela]]): patrons protected and helped their clients, who in return supported them, including at
elections. Great families accumulated networks of clients, friends and dependent communities. Second, wealth: offices were unpaid,
campaigns costly, and the assemblies weighted to the rich. Third, the nobility's near-monopoly of the consulship: between 200 and
133 BC a large majority of consuls came from families that had held the office before, and a "new man" ([[term:novus-homo|novus
homo]]) like Cato was a rarity.

## Aristocratic competition and its rewards {#people}

:::people
polybius cato-elder scipio-africanus
:::

Roman politics was a competition for glory, played according to rules. The rewards were office, military command, a triumph, and
memory: the right to have one's wax mask displayed in the family atrium and carried at funerals, where a son or relative delivered a
eulogy recounting the dead man's offices and those of his ancestors. Polybius describes these funerals as a powerful education in
civic virtue.{{anc:Polyb. 6.53–54}} The epitaph of Scipio Barbatus shows the values in compressed form.

:::source scipio-barbatus
An aristocrat's achievements, as his family wanted them remembered: offices "among you", the Roman people, and conquests.
:::

The competitive ethos had a darker side. When a single individual became too successful, his peers closed ranks against him, as
they did against Scipio Africanus in the 180s. And competition for military glory pushed Rome toward war. Some historians see in
aristocratic competition a central driver of Roman imperialism.{{ref:harris-1979}}

## How democratic was the Republic? {#historiography}

:::debate roman-democracy
:::

Through much of the twentieth century historians saw the Republic as an oligarchy. Matthias Gelzer (1912) and Ronald Syme (1939)
analysed politics through the family connections of the nobility; Lily Ross Taylor described "party politics" as competition between
noble groups using clients and religion. In 1984 Fergus Millar challenged this view, arguing that the people were sovereign in
law and in practice: they elected all magistrates, passed all laws and judged capital cases, and politicians had to persuade
them in the Forum.{{ref:millar-1984}}

Responses have been varied. John North accepted that the people chose, but argued that they had a real choice only when the elite
was divided.{{ref:north-1990}} Henrik Mouritsen stressed how few citizens could physically vote in Rome and argued that the voting
crowd was unrepresentative.{{ref:mouritsen-2001}} Karl-Joachim Hölkeskamp reframed the debate around "political culture": a
consensus about values, displayed in rituals, monuments and oratory, that bound people and elite together.{{ref:holkeskamp-2010}}

:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The system worked well for a city-state fighting in Italy, and remarkably well during the war with Hannibal, when the Senate's
collective leadership and the people's endurance held Rome together after Cannae. Its weaknesses appeared as Rome acquired an
overseas empire. Annual magistrates could not run distant provinces, so prorogation and long commands multiplied. Fortunes made in
war and provincial government made competition for office more expensive and more intense. The tribunate and plebeian assembly,
dormant as instruments of opposition, could be used by anyone willing to break with the Senate. And the mos maiorum, on which the
whole system depended, had no means of enforcement except the willingness of politicians to respect it. The next module shows what
happened when they stopped.

## The evidence {#evidence}

Our picture of the constitution rests on Polybius Book 6, on Cicero's speeches and treatises (On the Commonwealth, On the Laws) and on
Livy, together with inscribed laws and Senate decrees such as the decree on the Bacchanals. Much detail about procedure comes from
antiquarian writers such as Varro and Aulus Gellius. Historians reconstruct the system from sources written at different times,
often from the late Republic looking back; there is a constant risk of describing an idealised "classical" Republic that never
existed in quite that form.
