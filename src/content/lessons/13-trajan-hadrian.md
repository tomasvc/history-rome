## Historical context {#context}

After Domitian's murder the Senate chose one of its own, M. Cocceius Nerva, an elderly and childless lawyer-politician. His position
was weak: the Praetorian Guard, angry at Domitian's death, forced him to hand over the killers, and the armies were restless. In
October 97 Nerva adopted M. Ulpius Traianus, governor of Upper Germany, who had the support of the frontier legions. Trajan succeeded in
January 98. This transfer began the period that Gibbon called the happiest in human history, under five emperors from Nerva to Marcus
Aurelius.{{ref:gibbon-1776}}

## The ideology of adoption {#adoption}

Trajan was the first emperor from a provincial family: his ancestors were Italian settlers at Italica, near Seville in Baetica. His
accession through adoption was celebrated by Pliny the Younger in his *Panegyricus*, a speech of thanks for his consulship in 100,
which praised the choice of the best man rather than the accident of birth.{{anc:Plin. Pan. 7}} Tacitus, in the same years, had
Galba voice similar ideas in 69.{{anc:Tac. Hist. 1.16}}

:::check th-1
:::

Senatorial writers celebrated Trajan as everything Domitian had not been: accessible, modest, a military man who respected the Senate.
In 114 the Senate gave him the official title *Optimus*, "the best". This image was partly a construction of the senators who needed
it; but Trajan's long and largely harmonious relations with the Senate were real.

## Trajan's wars {#wars}

Trajan was the last great conquering emperor. The kingdom of Dacia, north of the Danube in modern Romania, under its king Decebalus, had
defeated Roman armies under Domitian and received subsidies. In two wars (101–102 and 105–106) Trajan defeated Decebalus, who killed
himself, destroyed his capital Sarmizegetusa Regia and made Dacia a province, settled by colonists "from the whole Roman world". The
Dacian gold that was captured paid for a great building programme in Rome, including Trajan's Forum and Markets and the column whose
spiral frieze narrates the wars in some 155 scenes.

:::claim interpretation
Trajan's Column is a carefully composed narrative, not a war reporter's record. Its scenes emphasise Roman discipline, building and
order and the emperor's presence, while showing Dacian bravery and despair. Historians use it for military equipment and practice,
but must allow for artistic convention and ideological intent.
:::

In 114 Trajan invaded Armenia and annexed it, then advanced down the Euphrates and Tigris, capturing the Parthian capital Ctesiphon and
reaching the Persian Gulf in 116. New provinces of Mesopotamia and Assyria were declared. Then the conquests unravelled: revolts broke
out across Mesopotamia, and in the same years the Jewish communities of Cyrene, Egypt and Cyprus rose in a bitter war against their
neighbours and the Roman authorities. Trajan fell ill and died at Selinus in Cilicia in August 117.

## The alimenta: welfare, demography or display? {#alimenta}

Trajan's most distinctive domestic programme was the *alimenta*. The emperor lent capital to landowners in the towns of Italy, secured on their estates; instead of repaying the loan, each landowner paid annual interest to his town, and the town used the income to give monthly allowances to local children. The two best-preserved records are large bronze tablets from Veleia, near Piacenza, and from the Ligures Baebiani, near Beneventum, which list the landowners, the value of their estates and the sums lent. Boys received more than girls, and legitimate children more than illegitimate ones. Coins advertised the scheme with the legend *ALIM(enta) ITAL(iae)*, and the arch at Beneventum, dedicated in 114, shows the emperor among Italian children.

Wealthy private citizens did the same on a smaller scale. Pliny transferred land worth more than 500,000 sesterces to his home town of Comum and took it back subject to a perpetual charge of 30,000 sesterces a year for the support of freeborn boys and girls; he explains in a letter why this arrangement was safer than simply giving the town cash.{{anc:Plin. Ep. 7.18}}

:::claim interpretation
Why did emperors fund the alimenta? Pliny's *Panegyricus* presents the children supported at Rome as future soldiers and citizens, which suggests concern with the manpower of Italy.{{anc:Plin. Pan. 28}} Older scholarship also saw the loans as cheap agricultural credit for farmers. Many historians now doubt both: the sums were small in relation to Italy's population, the loans were permanent charges rather than investment capital, and the scheme reached only some towns. It is perhaps best read as imperial generosity on the model of aristocratic patronage, a public demonstration of the emperor's care for Italy, which also helped real families.
:::

## Pliny in Bithynia {#pliny}

The tenth book of Pliny's letters preserves his correspondence with Trajan while governing Bithynia-Pontus around 109–111, a province
whose cities had been mismanaging their finances. It is the best evidence we have for what a governor did. Pliny writes about unfinished
aqueducts and theatres, the status of foundlings, the legal position of individuals, public baths, and the dangers of associations.

## The diaspora revolt, 115–117 {#diaspora}

While Trajan was in Mesopotamia, the Jewish communities of Cyrene, Egypt and Cyprus rose against their Greek neighbours and the Roman authorities. Our narrative sources are brief and hostile. Cassius Dio, preserved in an abridgement, gives lurid tales of atrocities and casualty figures in the hundreds of thousands that cannot be trusted; Eusebius names the rebel leader in Cyrene as Lukuas, whom he calls a king.{{anc:Euseb. Hist. eccl. 4.2}} Their causes are unclear: tensions with Greek neighbours, the aftermath of the destruction of the Temple and the special tax imposed on Jews after 70, and perhaps messianic expectations.

Documents and inscriptions give a more immediate picture. At Cyrene, later inscriptions record the rebuilding of roads, baths and temples "destroyed in the Jewish uprising". In Egypt, private letters on papyrus show a Greek official, Apollonios, called up to fight, and his anxious mother and wife praying for his safety. Nearly a century later the town of Oxyrhynchus still held an annual festival to celebrate the victory over the Jews.{{anc:P.Oxy. IV 705}} After 117 the once-great Jewish community of Alexandria, which had produced Philo, almost disappears from the evidence for generations. The revolt, followed fifteen years later by the war of Bar Kokhba, makes the "happiest age" look very different from the Jewish point of view; on Roman attitudes to Judaism more broadly, see [[lesson:roman-religion#tolerance|the limits of tolerance]].

## Hadrian, AD 117–138 {#hadrian}

P. Aelius Hadrianus, Trajan's cousin's son from Italica and his ward, was in Syria when Trajan died. His adoption was announced by
Trajan's widow Plotina and its authenticity was questioned at once. Early in his reign four distinguished ex-consuls were executed for
alleged conspiracy, without trial, damaging his relations with the Senate for good.

Hadrian immediately abandoned Mesopotamia, Assyria and Armenia beyond the Euphrates. He may also have considered giving up Dacia.

:::check th-4
:::

His reign was spent largely travelling. He visited almost every province, inspecting armies, founding cities, hearing petitions and
building. A speech he gave to the troops in North Africa in 128, commenting on their manoeuvres, survives on inscriptions from Lambaesis.
In Britain he ordered the construction of the wall that bears his name; in Germany the frontier was marked by a continuous timber
palisade. He was passionately Greek in his culture, wore a beard (the first emperor to do so), completed the temple of Olympian Zeus at
Athens, and founded the Panhellenion, a league of Greek cities. His young companion Antinous drowned in the Nile in 130; Hadrian founded
the city of Antinoopolis and promoted his cult across the Greek world.

:::people
trajan hadrian antoninus-pius pliny-younger
:::

In Rome he rebuilt the Pantheon, inscribing it with the name of its original builder Agrippa, and built the temple of Venus and Rome.
At Tibur (Tivoli) he built a vast villa evoking places he had seen. The praetor's edict, which had developed year by year, was codified
by the jurist Salvius Julianus, fixing a central body of Roman civil law.

The foundation of a Roman colony, Aelia Capitolina, on the site of Jerusalem, and perhaps a ban on circumcision, provoked the revolt of
Simon bar Kosiba (Bar Kokhba) in 132. Documents from caves near the Dead Sea, including letters from Bar Kokhba himself, show the
rebels' administration. The revolt was crushed by 135 with enormous loss of life; Jews were banned from Jerusalem and the province was
renamed Syria Palaestina.

## Hadrian's Wall {#wall}

Hadrian's Wall ran 80 Roman miles (about 117 km) from the Tyne to the Solway. It combined a stone (in the west originally turf) wall,
a ditch in front, small fortlets (milecastles) every Roman mile with turrets between them, and, after a change of plan, full forts on
the line of the wall itself. Behind it ran a great earthwork, the *vallum*, marking a military zone. Within twenty years Antoninus Pius
advanced the frontier to the Forth–Clyde line with the Antonine Wall, abandoned again in the 160s.

What was the wall for? It could not stop a determined army; its garrison was not large enough to man it in strength. Its purposes were
probably to control movement and trade, to provide a base for patrols and surveillance, and to make a statement of Roman power. Its
gates suggest regulation rather than exclusion. Hadrian's biography in the *Historia Augusta*, a late and unreliable source, says it was
built "to separate the barbarians from the Romans".{{anc:HA Hadr. 11.2}}

## Antoninus Pius {#antoninus}

Hadrian's first chosen heir, L. Aelius Caesar, died in 138. Hadrian then adopted T. Aurelius Antoninus, a senator of Gallic family,
requiring him in turn to adopt the young M. Annius Verus (Marcus Aurelius) and Lucius Verus, son of the dead Aelius. Antoninus' long
reign (138–161) was remembered as peaceful: he never left Italy, governed through correspondence, and left a large surplus. His
honorific *Pius* may refer to his insistence on deifying Hadrian against the Senate's resistance.

## Consequences {#consequences}

The age of Trajan and Hadrian set the territorial limits of the empire for the next century and established the pattern of the
emperor as a mobile ruler who governed by answering petitions and visiting armies. The integration of provincial elites reached the
throne itself. But the period also saw some of the bloodiest suppressions of revolt in imperial history, in Judaea and the Jewish
diaspora; the "happiest age" looked different from Cyrene or Jerusalem.

## Historiography {#historiography}

We lack a continuous narrative for this period: Tacitus' *Annals* stop at 66, and Cassius Dio's books for these reigns survive only in
Byzantine abridgements. We depend on Pliny's letters and speech, the late and partly fictional *Historia Augusta*, inscriptions, coins,
papyri and monuments. Anthony Birley's biography of Hadrian assembles this evidence around his travels.{{ref:birley-1997}} Fergus
Millar's *The Emperor in the Roman World* used the same kinds of evidence to show that the emperor's essential role was reactive: to
respond to embassies, petitions and letters.{{ref:millar-1977}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside The Historia Augusta
The *Historia Augusta* is a collection of imperial biographies from Hadrian to the late third century, presented as the work of six
authors writing under Diocletian and Constantine. In 1889 Hermann Dessau argued that it was written by a single author at the end of
the fourth century, and this is now generally accepted. The early lives contain good information; the later ones include invented
documents and people. Use it only with independent confirmation.
:::
