## The problem {#context}

Augustus created a monarchy without saying so. That decision shaped the whole history of the Roman empire. Because the emperor's position was
not defined as a hereditary office, his legitimacy had to be constantly produced: through law, through relationships with the groups that
mattered, through victory, religion, images and ceremony. And because there was no legal way to remove a bad emperor or to choose a new one,
the same groups that conferred legitimacy could also withdraw it, by murder, mutiny or usurpation. This lesson looks across the centuries at
how that worked.

## Who mattered? {#groups}

:::diagram legitimacy
:::

Historians often identify three groups whose acceptance an early emperor needed:

- **The army**, above all the Praetorian Guard and the frontier legions, whose loyalty was secured by pay, donatives, oaths and the emperor's
  image as their commander (*imperator*).
- **The Senate**, the aristocracy of office-holders whose cooperation was needed to govern and whose writers created the emperor's lasting
  reputation.
- **The people of Rome**, who expected grain, games and access, and whose acclamations in the circus and theatre were a public measure of
  popularity.

The German historian Egon Flaig described the principate as an "acceptance system" in which these groups' continuing consent was the
emperor's real constitution; usurpation was not a breakdown of the system but the way it removed rulers who had lost support.

:::check pl-3
:::

In late antiquity and Byzantium the list changed: the court, the bishops and the people of Constantinople became essential, and the idea that
God chose the emperor gave a new language of legitimacy. But the people's acclamation and the army's support remained necessary, and
Byzantine emperors could still be overthrown by popular revolt, as the Nika riot nearly showed.{{ref:kaldellis-2015}}

## Instruments of legitimacy {#instruments}

**Law.** Augustus' powers were voted by the Senate and people and held for fixed terms, renewed by law. Later emperors received a package of
powers conferred at once, as the law for Vespasian shows.

:::source lex-de-imperio
:::

**Dynasty and adoption.** Most emperors sought to found or join a dynasty. Adoption was a normal Roman practice, and emperors used it freely:
Augustus adopted Tiberius, Nerva adopted Trajan. Septimius Severus went further, declaring himself the son of the long-dead Marcus Aurelius and
naming his son Marcus Aurelius Antoninus. Constantine claimed descent from Claudius II. Names were powerful: "Caesar" became a title, "Augustus"
the title of the senior emperor, "Antoninus" and "Constantine" dynastic labels.{{ref:hekster-2015}}

**Victory.** Military success was the most traditional Roman source of prestige. Emperors celebrated triumphs, took victory titles
(*Germanicus*, *Parthicus*), and advertised conquests on coins.

:::source aegypto-capta
:::

**Religion.** Emperors were priests (pontifex maximus) and, in the provinces and after death, gods. The imperial cult offered subjects a way to
express loyalty. From Constantine onwards, emperors were Christians chosen by God, protectors of the Church, presiding over councils, while
Byzantine emperors were crowned in Hagia Sophia by the Patriarch.

**Image and ceremony.** Coins, statues and monuments carried the emperor's image everywhere. Ceremony evolved from the accessible
"citizen-emperor" (*civilis princeps*) whom Augustus and Trajan claimed to be, to the remote, sacred ruler of late antiquity, approached with
prostration and surrounded by silence.{{ref:wallace-hadrill-1982}}

## The succession problem {#succession}

:::source res-gestae
:::

Augustus' solution, to designate an heir by adoption and to confer powers on him in advance, worked when an emperor lived long enough and had
a suitable heir. When he did not, the result was murder or civil war: in 41, 68–69, 193, 235 and repeatedly in the third century, in which most
emperors died violently. Even when there was an heir, every other male relative was a potential rival, and dynastic murders (of Britannicus,
Geta, Crispus, Constantine's nephews in 337) recur throughout imperial history.

:::check pl-2
:::

Byzantine emperors addressed the problem by crowning sons as co-emperors in their own lifetimes; the idea of being "born in the purple"
(*porphyrogennetos*), to a reigning emperor, gave special prestige. Yet usurpation remained common: of the emperors who ruled from
Constantinople, a significant proportion were deposed, blinded or killed.

## Three accessions {#accessions}

Watching how emperors came to power shows the system working, and failing.

**AD 14: a prepared succession.** When Augustus died at Nola in August AD 14, Tiberius already held the tribunician power and proconsular imperium, so legally nothing needed to change. Yet the transition was carefully staged. Tiberius took control of the Guard and wrote to the armies as commander; the consuls, Senate, equestrians and people swore loyalty; and in the Senate Tiberius performed reluctance while senators urged him to accept. Tacitus mocks this as hypocrisy, but the scene shows that even a prepared succession needed public expressions of consent from every group.{{anc:Tac. Ann. 1.7–13}}

**AD 193: an open market.** After Pertinax's murder, the Guard accepted the senator who offered them most, Didius Julianus; the people of Rome jeered him in the circus and called on the frontier armies. The armies of the Danube and of Syria proclaimed their own commanders, Septimius Severus and Pescennius Niger, and the governor of Britain, Clodius Albinus, was bought off for the moment with the title of Caesar. Legitimacy was decided by civil war, and the winner, Septimius Severus, then claimed descent from Marcus Aurelius to give his victory the appearance of continuity.

**AD 518: a Byzantine acclamation.** When the emperor Anastasius died without a designated heir, officials, guards and factions argued in the palace and the Hippodrome over candidates. The commander of the palace guard, Justin, an elderly soldier from the Balkans, was chosen, raised on a shield and acclaimed by the people in the Hippodrome, then crowned. A detailed account of this accession, drawn from a sixth-century official, was preserved in the tenth-century *Book of Ceremonies*. Acclamation by army, Senate and people remained essential five centuries after Augustus.

## The emperor in everyone's pocket {#coins}

Coins were the medium through which most inhabitants of the empire saw the emperor. Every issue carried his portrait and titles on one side and, on the other, an image and legend chosen from a large repertoire: personifications such as *Pax*, *Concordia*, *Fides exercitus* (the loyalty of the army), *Liberalitas* (generosity); buildings; victories; members of the imperial family. Carlos Noreña's quantitative study of imperial coin types showed which virtues and benefits were advertised most often, and argued that they defined a shared language of imperial ideals across the western provinces.{{ref:norena-2011}}

Portraits also changed meaningfully. Augustus remained youthful on his coins for forty years; Hadrian introduced the beard; tetrarchic portraits made the emperors look alike; Constantine's later coins show him gazing upward, which Eusebius interpreted as an attitude of prayer.{{anc:Euseb. Vit. Const. 4.15}}

:::claim interpretation
Who chose coin types, and how far users noticed them, is debated. Mint officials may have selected many designs, and most users probably looked at coins mainly as money. But the sheer number of coins and the consistency of their messages made them the most widely distributed statement of how emperors wished to be seen.
:::

## Women and the court {#court}

Because power was personal and dynastic, those close to the emperor had influence: wives and mothers, freedmen, eunuchs, favourites. Livia,
Agrippina the Younger, Julia Domna, Julia Mamaea, Galla Placidia, Theodora and Irene exercised power that ancient historians typically
presented as illegitimate. In the late empire and Byzantium, empresses (*Augustae*) had their own courts, coinage and ceremonial roles, and
several ruled.

## Reputation {#reputation}

An emperor's reputation was decided after his death, by his successor, the Senate and the historians. Good emperors were deified; bad ones
had their memory condemned, their statues destroyed and names erased. The surviving literary tradition, written largely by senators, divides
emperors into good and bad according to how they treated the Senate.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Older constitutional history, from Theodor Mommsen onwards, sought to define the emperor's legal powers precisely. Fergus Millar shifted the
focus to what emperors did: receive embassies, answer petitions, judge cases.{{ref:millar-1977}} Later scholars have studied imperial image and
communication (Zanker, Noreña), ceremony (Sabine MacCormack, Michael McCormick), and the ways emperors were constrained by tradition (Hekster).
Mary Beard's *Emperor of Rome* asks what it was like to be, and to live under, an emperor.{{ref:beard-2023}}
