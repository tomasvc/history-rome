## Historical context {#context}

The young Republic was a city-state of perhaps a few tens of thousands of people, ruled by annually elected magistrates and a
council of elders, surrounded by rivals: Latin cities with which it shared language and religion, the Etruscan city of Veii only
15 km away, and hill peoples (Aequi, Volsci, Sabines) who raided the plain. Over the next two and a half centuries, two processes
transformed it. Internally, the struggle that historians call the [[term:conflict-orders|Conflict of the Orders]] reshaped who could
hold power. Externally, Rome conquered Italy south of the Po and bound its communities into a unique system of alliances.

The two processes were connected. Rome's wars depended on citizen soldiers, and those soldiers, many of them plebeians, had leverage.
The plebeians' most effective weapon in the tradition was the *secession*: withdrawing from the city and refusing military service.
Conquest in turn provided land, booty and colonies that could ease internal tensions.

approximate. The sequence of laws, however, is broadly accepted, because it fits the clear outcome visible by the third century.

## Patricians and plebeians {#people}

:::people
appius-claudius-caecus pyrrhus
:::

Who were the patricians? In the historical period they were a closed group of families (gentes) such as the Fabii, Cornelii,
Valerii and Claudii, with exclusive rights to certain priesthoods and the office of interrex. How they became closed is unclear:
the tradition makes them descendants of Romulus' first senators, but some historians argue that patrician status crystallised only
in the early Republic, as a group of families monopolised power after the end of the monarchy.{{ref:cornell-1995}}

The [[term:plebeians|plebs]] was everyone else, and it was not a homogeneous class. It included poor farmers burdened by debt,
craftsmen and traders in the city, and wealthy families who could afford to serve as cavalry but were excluded from office. Their
interests overlapped but were not identical, and this is the key to understanding the conflict. Poor plebeians wanted protection
from arbitrary punishment, relief from debt, and land. Rich plebeians wanted access to the magistracies and priesthoods that
brought prestige and power.

## Institutions born of the conflict {#institutions}

The plebeians created a state within the state. They elected their own officers, the [[term:tribune-plebs|tribunes of the plebs]],
whose persons were declared sacrosanct by a collective oath: anyone who harmed a tribune could be killed with impunity. A tribune's
power began as the right to bring aid (*auxilium*) to a plebeian threatened by a magistrate, and grew into a general right of veto
([[term:intercessio]]) over any act of a magistrate, the Senate or an assembly. The plebeians met in their own assembly, the
[[term:concilium-plebis|concilium plebis]], organised by tribes, which passed resolutions ([[term:plebiscitum|plebiscita]]).

The Twelve Tables answered a demand for written law. Their fragments show a society of households under the power of a
paterfamilias, of debtors who could be seized and bound, of patrons and clients whose relationship was protected by religious
sanction, and of rules separating the dead from the city.

:::source twelve-tables
The Tables were written down to make law knowable and less dependent on patrician interpreters. They also show how harsh that
law could be for debtors.
:::

The decisive political change came with the Licinio-Sextian laws of 367 BC, which (according to the tradition) established that one
of the two consuls could be a plebeian. Plebeian consuls appear regularly from the 360s and by 342 one consulship was effectively
reserved for plebeians. In 326 the lex Poetelia abolished *nexum*, the bondage of debtors. In 300 the lex Ogulnia opened the major
priestly colleges. In 287, after a final secession, the lex Hortensia made plebiscites binding on all Romans.

:::check er-1
:::

The result was not democracy but a new elite. Families who reached the consulship, patrician or plebeian, formed the
[[term:nobilitas|nobility]], and their descendants dominated the highest office for the rest of the Republic. Offices were unpaid,
campaigning was expensive, and the centuriate assembly that elected consuls gave greater weight to the votes of the wealthy.

:::claim interpretation
The Conflict of the Orders ended with the incorporation of wealthy plebeians into a patricio-plebeian nobility. The institutions
the plebs created, above all the tribunate and the plebeian assembly, were largely tamed and used by that nobility, but they
remained available to reformers, as the Gracchi would show in 133 BC.
:::

## Conquering and organising Italy {#causation}

Rome's expansion in Italy began with near neighbours. The capture of Veii in 396 BC, after a long siege, doubled Roman territory;
the tradition says soldiers were paid for the first time during the siege. A few years later a Gallic war band routed the Roman army
at the river Allia and occupied Rome. The trauma was remembered for centuries, but the recovery was fast. Rome built a new wall of
cut stone (the so-called Servian Wall, whose surviving stretches date to the 370s), and by the 340s it was strong enough to fight the
Latins and the Samnites.

The settlement of 338 BC after the Latin War was the foundation of Roman Italy. Rome dissolved the Latin League and made separate
arrangements with each community:

- Some Latin towns received full Roman citizenship and became self-governing **municipia**.
- Others, including Campanian cities such as Capua, received **citizenship without the vote** (*civitas sine suffragio*), sharing the
  burdens of citizens without political rights in Rome.
- Others remained **Latin** allies with rights of marriage and commerce with Romans.
- Rome planted **colonies** of citizens or Latins on confiscated land at strategic points.

Over the next seventy years, through the Samnite Wars and the war with Pyrrhus, this system was extended to almost all of
peninsular Italy. The defeated became **allies** ([[term:socii]]) bound by treaty. They kept their own government and paid no
tribute. Their one essential obligation was to supply soldiers for Rome's wars.

:::check er-2
:::

Why did the system work? Historians emphasise several features. It created a hierarchy of statuses that allowed communities to rise,
giving them a stake in the system. It prevented combinations against Rome. Above all, because allies contributed men rather than
money, Rome had to keep fighting to make the alliance profitable for all: successful wars brought booty and land, shared with allied
soldiers. Some historians see in this a structural engine of Roman expansion.{{ref:cornell-1995}}

:::claim contested
Whether the Roman alliance system "required" constant war to hold together, making expansion almost automatic, is debated. It is
an attractive structural explanation, but it can underplay the choices made by Roman leaders and the aggressiveness of Rome's
neighbours.
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

By 264 BC Rome controlled Italy south of the Po valley, through a network of perhaps 150 allied states, Latin colonies and citizen
communities. Three consequences shaped what followed.

First, Rome had access to military manpower far beyond any rival in the western Mediterranean. Polybius would later list the men
available in 225 BC as more than 700,000 infantry, an extraordinary figure even if it is a theoretical total.{{anc:Polyb. 2.24}}

Second, the political settlement at Rome was stable for over a century. The nobility competed fiercely for office and glory, but
within rules accepted by all, and its competition was channelled into war.

Third, the Romans built infrastructure to hold Italy together: the Via Appia from 312 BC, other roads and colonies at strategic
points. Appius Claudius' censorship shows how political ambition, public works and military needs went together.

:::

## Historians disagree {#historiography}

The narrative of the early Republic in Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus is full of speeches, secessions and heroic tribunes,
which look suspiciously like the political conflicts of the late Republic projected backwards. Annalists writing after the Gracchi
had every reason to imagine early tribunes as Gracchan heroes or villains. Historians therefore treat the narrative details with
great scepticism, while accepting the broad sequence of institutional change, which is confirmed by the visible outcome.

:::debate early-rome-sources
:::

Harriet Flower has argued that we should speak not of one Republic from 509 to 49 BC but of a series of republics; the
patricio-plebeian republic that emerged between 367 and 300 BC was in important respects a new political order.{{ref:flower-2010}}

:::check er-3
:::

## The evidence {#evidence}

For this period we depend on Livy Books 2–10, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, and scattered later references, together with the
fragments of the Twelve Tables, archaeology of Rome and the sites of Latium, and the lists of magistrates. Archaeology confirms the
scale of Roman building in the fourth century (the walls, temples and roads), the destruction of some sites, and the foundation of
colonies on regular plans. It cannot tell us about secessions or speeches. The Gallic sack, so vivid in literature, has left no clear
destruction layer in Rome.

The most useful habit here is to ask, of every statement in Livy, what kind of record could have preserved it: a law (likely
preserved), a magistrate's name (possibly), a temple dedication (probably), a speech (almost never).
