## Historical context {#context}

Marcus Aurelius, adopted by Antoninus Pius in 138 at Hadrian's direction, had been groomed for decades. A devoted student of Stoic
philosophy, he succeeded in 161 and immediately made his adoptive brother Lucius Verus co-emperor with equal powers, the first time two
emperors ruled together. His reign, remembered as that of the philosopher-king, was dominated by crises: war in the East, a pandemic and
a long, hard war on the Danube.

## War in the East {#parthia}

In 161 the Parthian king Vologaeses IV invaded Armenia and defeated a Roman army. Lucius Verus went east to command, though the fighting
was done by experienced generals, above all C. Avidius Cassius, who took and sacked Seleucia on the Tigris and the Parthian capital
Ctesiphon in 165. Roman control was extended to the middle Euphrates; Dura-Europos became a Roman garrison town.

## The Antonine plague {#plague}

The returning armies brought a disease that spread across the empire from 165–166 and recurred for decades. The physician Galen, who
left Rome when it arrived and was then summoned back by the emperors, describes fever, diarrhoea and a black pustular rash. Most
historians identify it, tentatively, as smallpox.

:::claim contested
The mortality of the Antonine plague is one of the most debated questions in Roman history. Some historians, drawing on Egyptian
papyri showing depopulated villages and collapsing rents, a gap in military diplomas, interruptions in building and mining, and
literary reports, estimate that 10–20 per cent or more of the empire's population died. Others argue that the evidence is too scattered
and ambiguous to support figures of that kind. No ancient pathogen DNA has yet identified the disease.
:::

:::

The plague came at the worst possible time, during the most dangerous northern war in two centuries. The Historia Augusta claims that
Marcus enlisted slaves, gladiators and bandits and sold palace treasures to pay for the war; whether or not this is accurate, the
pressure on manpower and money is evident.

## Galen in Rome {#galen}

The physician Galen of Pergamum is one of the richest sources for the age of Marcus, because he wrote copiously and loved to talk about himself. Trained in Pergamum, Smyrna and Alexandria, and for a time physician to the gladiators of his home city, he came to Rome in 162 and made his name with public anatomical demonstrations on animals: by cutting the nerves of the larynx in a living pig, he showed that they controlled its voice. He left Rome in 166, as the plague arrived, but was summoned by the emperors to their headquarters at Aquileia, and later attended Commodus.

His career shows how a Greek intellectual could win patronage at the highest level of Roman society. His writings preserve case histories of senators, slaves and athletes, descriptions of the plague, and his views on diet, drugs and the soul. The evidence is still growing. In 2005 a treatise thought lost, *On the Avoidance of Grief*, was identified in a manuscript in the Vlatadon monastery in Thessaloniki. In it Galen describes losing his books, drugs and instruments when the great fire of 192 burned the storehouses on the Sacred Way in Rome, and reflects on how he had trained himself to bear such losses calmly, having lived through the fear and cruelty of Commodus' reign.

## The Marcomannic Wars {#marcomannic}

From around 167 groups from beyond the upper and middle Danube, Marcomanni, Quadi, Iazyges and others, crossed into the empire. Around
170 a force broke through into Italy and besieged Aquileia, the first invasion of Italy since the Cimbri. Marcus spent most of the rest
of his life at the front, at Carnuntum and Sirmium. After hard campaigning he imposed treaties but in 175 had to break off to face the
revolt of Avidius Cassius in Syria, provoked by a false report of Marcus' death. Cassius was killed by his own officers.

War resumed in 177/178. Marcus apparently intended to annex new territory north of the Danube; he died in March 180, probably at
Vindobona (Vienna) or Sirmium. His column in Rome, modelled on Trajan's, shows a war with more scenes of brutality against civilians
and the "Rain Miracle", in which a sudden storm saved a thirsty Roman army; pagans credited it to an Egyptian magician or the gods,
Christians to the prayers of Christian soldiers.

Marcus also settled defeated groups inside the empire as farmers, a practice that would expand greatly in later centuries.

:::claim interpretation
Historians debate whether these wars reflect new pressures from beyond the frontier, caused by movements of peoples further north and
east, or the reorganisation of societies on Rome's borders into larger, more formidable confederations, itself partly a result of
centuries of contact with Rome. The two explanations are not exclusive.
:::

## The philosopher-emperor {#meditations}

During his campaigns Marcus wrote, in Greek, a collection of notes to himself that we call the *Meditations*. They are not a treatise
or a memoir, but exercises in Stoic self-discipline: reminders of the shortness of life, of duty, of the need to accept what happens
and to act justly towards others.

His reign also saw the persecution of Christians in some cities. In 177 at Lugdunum a mob and the governor tortured and executed
Christians, including the slave woman Blandina; a letter from the survivors is preserved by Eusebius.{{anc:Euseb. Hist. eccl. 5.1}}
These persecutions were local, but Marcus' reply to the governor's question confirmed the execution of those who refused to recant.

## Was adoption a principle? {#adoption-myth}

The so-called adoptive emperors are often said to have chosen "the best man" rather than a son. The record suggests a more practical explanation. Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian and Antoninus Pius had no surviving sons, and every one of their choices kept power within a network of kinship and marriage: Hadrian was Trajan's relative and married his great-niece Sabina; Antoninus' wife was Sabina's niece; Marcus was the nephew of Antoninus' wife and married Antoninus' daughter. Vespasian, the last emperor before Nerva with adult sons, had passed power to them; Marcus, the first emperor since Vespasian with a son who survived to adulthood, did the same.

Seen this way, [[lesson:trajan-hadrian#adoption|the ideology of adoption]] praised by Pliny made a virtue of necessity. It nonetheless mattered: it gave the Senate a language in which to approve an heir, and it set an expectation against which Commodus could be judged.

:::claim interpretation
Most historians now agree that no emperor between Nerva and Marcus passed over a biological son in favour of an adopted heir. Whether Marcus could have done so is another question: a living son of the emperor would always have been a focus for ambition and a danger to any other successor.
:::

## Commodus, AD 180–192 {#commodus}

Marcus broke with the pattern of his predecessors, who had chosen adult heirs by adoption: he had a surviving son, Commodus, made him
co-emperor in 177 and left the empire to him at eighteen. Commodus made peace with the northern peoples, against the advice of his
father's generals, and returned to Rome.

His reign was punctuated by conspiracies, including one in 182 involving his sister Lucilla, which turned him against the Senate. Power
passed to favourites: the praetorian prefect Perennis (killed 185) and the freedman chamberlain Cleander, who sold offices and was
handed to a rioting crowd in 190. In his later years Commodus identified himself with Hercules, renamed the months after his own titles,
renamed Rome *Colonia Commodiana* and fought as a gladiator in the arena. Dio, a senator present in the Colosseum, describes having to
chew laurel leaves to keep from laughing as the emperor waved the severed head of an ostrich at the senators.{{anc:Cass. Dio 73.21}}

On 31 December 192 he was strangled in his bath by a wrestler, Narcissus, in a plot involving his mistress Marcia, his chamberlain and
the praetorian prefect. His death began a year of civil war like that of 69.

## Consequences and historiography {#historiography}

Edward Gibbon began *The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* with the Antonine age, describing the period from Domitian's death to
Commodus' accession as the time when the human race was most happy and prosperous, and Commodus' reign as the start of the decline.{{ref:gibbon-1776}}
Cassius Dio had already marked the transition sharply. Modern historians see more continuity: the strains of plague, war and finance
under Marcus foreshadowed those of the third century, and the problems of succession had only been postponed. Kyle Harper has made
the plague, and the end of a favourable climate phase, central to a new environmental interpretation of Roman history; critics
argue that he overstates the evidence.{{ref:harper-2017}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Reading the papyri
Some of the strongest evidence for the plague's impact comes from Egypt, where tax registers and land leases on papyrus survive from
villages in the Fayum and the Nile delta. In some villages the number of taxpayers falls sharply around 166–170. The problem is
interpretation: did people die, flee from taxes, or move? Demographic historians debate how far such local evidence can be generalised.
:::
