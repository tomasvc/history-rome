## Historical context {#context}

Augustus had created a monarchy that pretended not to be one. His successors inherited the pretence, the powers and the problems:
the dependence on the army and the Praetorian Guard, the need to cooperate with a Senate that resented its subordination, a court of
relatives, freedmen and favourites with no formal place in the constitution, and the absence of a rule of succession. The four
emperors who followed him, all descendants of Augustus or Livia, are known together as the Julio-Claudians.

## Tiberius, AD 14–37 {#tiberius}

Tiberius was fifty-five, an experienced general and administrator, and by temperament reserved. At the Senate meeting after Augustus'
death he appeared reluctant to accept the full position; Tacitus presents this as hypocrisy, though it may also have reflected genuine
uncertainty about how a successor should behave.{{anc:Tac. Ann. 1.11–13}} He transferred elections from the people to the Senate,
consulted the Senate on many matters and refused extravagant honours.

The first crisis came from the army: the legions in Pannonia and Germany mutinied over pay and length of service. The second came
from the family. Germanicus, Tiberius' adopted son and heir, popular and charismatic, was sent to the East and died at Antioch in AD
19, convinced that the governor of Syria, Cn. Piso, had poisoned him. Piso was tried before the Senate and killed himself.

:::aside A document against the narrative
In the late 1980s copies of the Senate's decree concerning Piso (*senatus consultum de Cn. Pisone patre*), dated December AD 20, were
found in the province of Baetica in southern Spain. The long text condemns Piso, praises the restraint of the imperial family and
was ordered to be displayed in the main city of every province and in legionary camps. It broadly confirms Tacitus' account but shows
the official version of events in the regime's own words, and how far that version was meant to travel.
:::

:::check jc-3
:::

Tiberius increasingly relied on L. Aelius Sejanus, prefect of the Praetorian Guard, who concentrated the Guard's cohorts in a single
camp on the edge of Rome. After Tiberius' son Drusus died in 23 (Sejanus was later accused of poisoning him), Sejanus' influence grew,
and in 26 Tiberius left Rome for good, eventually settling on Capri. Agrippina the Elder, Germanicus' widow, and two of her sons were
arrested and died. In 31, apparently convinced that Sejanus was plotting, Tiberius sent a long letter to the Senate that led to his
immediate arrest and execution. Treason trials (for *maiestas*, "diminishing the majesty" of the Roman people and emperor) multiplied,
encouraged by informers who received a share of the convicted man's property.

## Gaius, AD 37–41 {#gaius}

Tiberius' successor was Germanicus' surviving son Gaius, nicknamed Caligula ("Little Boot") by the soldiers among whom he grew up. His
accession was greeted with enthusiasm. Within a year a serious illness was followed by the execution of his co-heir and, according to
the sources, increasingly autocratic and erratic behaviour. Suetonius and Dio tell stories of incest, of his horse being made consul,
of demands to be worshipped as a god. Philo of Alexandria, who led a Jewish embassy to him in 40, gives a contemporary portrait of an
emperor who mocked the envoys and ordered his statue placed in the Temple at Jerusalem.

:::claim contested
How much of the Caligula tradition is true is debated. Some stories, such as the horse made consul, derive from jokes or threats
reported as fact (Suetonius says only that he was said to have planned it). Some historians interpret his behaviour as a deliberate
assertion of open autocracy that humiliated the Senate, rather than madness.
:::

In January 41 officers of the Guard murdered him, with his wife and daughter. While the Senate debated restoring the Republic, soldiers
of the Guard found his uncle Claudius in the palace and carried him to their camp, where he promised them a donative and was
acclaimed. The Senate had no choice but to confirm him.

## Claudius, AD 41–54 {#claudius}

Claudius, aged fifty, had been kept out of public life because of a disability that affected his speech and movement. He was a
learned man who had written histories of Carthage and of the Etruscans. His accession through the Guard left him insecure with the
Senate, and some 35 senators and several hundred equestrians were executed during his reign according to Suetonius.{{anc:Suet. Claud.
29}}

He made major contributions to the administration. Freedmen of the imperial household, such as Narcissus (correspondence) and Pallas
(finance), ran secretariats that handled petitions and accounts; senators were outraged that former slaves held such influence and
wealth. He built a new harbour at Ostia and two aqueducts, the Aqua Claudia and the Anio Novus. In 43 he invaded Britain, visiting for
sixteen days to receive the surrender of the Catuvellaunian capital at Camulodunum. As censor in 47–48 he admitted leading men from
Gallia Comata to the Senate, defending the decision with a speech that survives both in Tacitus' version and on a bronze tablet at
Lyon.

The court around him became the scene of deadly rivalries. His wife Messalina was executed in 48 after apparently going through a
marriage ceremony with a senator; Claudius then married his niece Agrippina the Younger, who secured the adoption of her son Nero
over Claudius' own son Britannicus. Claudius died in 54, probably poisoned with mushrooms, according to most ancient writers.

## Nero, AD 54–68 {#nero}

Nero became emperor at sixteen. The first years, guided by the philosopher Seneca and the prefect Burrus, were later remembered as a
good period. Britannicus died at a dinner in 55, again probably poisoned. In 59 Nero had his mother killed, after an attempt to drown
her in a collapsible boat failed. In Britain the revolt of Boudica, queen of the Iceni, destroyed Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium
in 60 or 61 before it was crushed.

In July 64 a fire destroyed much of Rome. Nero opened public buildings and his gardens to the homeless and organised relief, but
rumours that he had started the fire, or sung of the fall of Troy while the city burned, spread. To deflect blame he punished
Christians, the earliest pagan reference to the movement in Rome.{{anc:Tac. Ann. 15.44}} On the ruins he built the Golden House, a
vast palace park in the heart of the city.

Nero's passion for performance, chariot racing and Greek culture offended senators; he sang in public and, in 66–67, toured Greece,
competing in the games and declaring the province free. Rebuilding Rome and his projects strained finances. The discovery of the
Pisonian conspiracy in 65 led to the deaths of Seneca, the poet Lucan, and others; leading generals, including Corbulo, were forced to
suicide.

In 68 C. Julius Vindex, governor in Gaul, revolted and called on Ser. Sulpicius Galba, governor of Hispania Tarraconensis, to take power.
Vindex was defeated by the Rhine legions, but Nero lost his nerve. The praetorian prefect Nymphidius Sabinus promised the Guard a huge
donative in Galba's name; the Senate declared Nero a public enemy. He fled and killed himself, reportedly saying "what an artist dies
with me".{{anc:Suet. Ner. 49}} With him the line of Augustus ended.

## Causation: a system or a set of personalities? {#causation}

The sources explain the Julio-Claudians through character: Tiberius' hypocrisy, Gaius' madness, Claudius' weakness under the control
of wives and freedmen, Nero's vanity and cruelty. Modern historians look for structures behind the personalities.

- **The Senate's ambiguous role.** Emperors needed senators as governors and generals, but any senator of distinguished birth was a
  potential rival. Emperors who emphasised cooperation were remembered well; those who exposed the reality of autocracy were
  remembered as tyrants.
- **The court.** Power lay with those who had access to the emperor, which made wives, mothers, freedmen and prefects central figures.
  The hostility of the sources to them reflects senatorial resentment of power outside the traditional hierarchy.
- **Succession.** Without rules, every adult male relative was a threat, which drove the cycle of executions within the family.
- **The army.** The Guard made Claudius emperor and abandoned Nero; in 68–69 the frontier legions would make their own emperors.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Our main narratives come from senators or writers in the senatorial tradition: Tacitus (writing c. AD 110–120), Suetonius (c. 120) and
Cassius Dio (early third century). Their judgements reflect the values of a class whose dignity the emperors had compromised. Some
modern historians, notably Edward Champlin, have tried to recover how Nero presented himself and why he remained popular with many,
including the imperial pretenders who claimed to be Nero returned from the dead.{{ref:champlin-2003}} Others, like Miriam Griffin,
reconstruct the political dynamics of his fall.{{ref:griffin-1984}} Mary Beard's *Emperor of Rome* asks what all emperors did, rather
than what made some monsters.{{ref:beard-2023}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Erasure and survival
After Nero's death his name was chiselled off some inscriptions, and many portraits were recut as other emperors. But Nero's coins
circulated for decades, and graffiti at Pompeii praise him and his wife Poppaea. The physical record preserves a more complicated
reputation than the narratives.
:::
