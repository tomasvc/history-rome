## Continuity and revival {#context}

Rome's legacy reached later ages through two kinds of channel. Some institutions and practices continued without break: the Latin language
evolving into the Romance languages; the Church, which preserved Latin, Roman law and much of the late Roman administrative geography; the
eastern Roman state itself until 1453. Others were deliberately revived: Roman law in the medieval West, classical literature and architecture in
the Renaissance, republican ideals in the revolutions of the eighteenth century. Distinguishing between the two matters: revival is always
selective, and every age has created the Rome it needed.

## Law {#law}

Justinian's codification preserved Roman law; its rediscovery in eleventh-century Italy made it the foundation of legal education in Europe and of
the civil law systems of continental Europe, Latin America and many other countries. Concepts such as contract, ownership and legal personality,
and Latin terms used by lawyers worldwide, derive from Roman law.

## How Roman law reached the modern world {#law-path}

The path from [[lesson:justinian#law|Justinian's Digest]] to a modern civil code was long and indirect; for how the jurists themselves worked, see [[lesson:roman-law#method|thinking like a Roman jurist]].

1. **Rediscovery.** In the late eleventh century the Digest, almost unknown in the West for five hundred years, began to be studied again in northern Italy. Its text depended above all on a single sixth-century manuscript, kept at Pisa and later at Florence.
2. **The glossators.** At Bologna, from the early twelfth century, teachers such as Irnerius explained the text line by line in marginal notes (glosses). Around 1230 Accursius gathered these into a standard commentary, the *Glossa ordinaria*. Students from all over Europe came to Bologna and carried Roman law home.
3. **The *ius commune*.** By the late Middle Ages, Roman law as interpreted by these scholars, together with Church law, formed a "common law" of continental Europe, used to fill the gaps in local customs and to train lawyers and officials.
4. **Codification.** In the early modern period jurists reorganised this material systematically. French scholars such as Domat and Pothier shaped the *Code civil* of 1804, issued under Napoleon; in Germany, Friedrich Carl von Savigny's historical school and the "Pandectists" who studied the Digest prepared the way for the German Civil Code (BGB) of 1900. Through conquest, colonisation and imitation these codes influenced law from Latin America and Quebec to Japan and Turkey.

England followed a different path: its common law developed through royal courts and precedent, with much less Roman influence, though Scotland and South Africa (through Roman-Dutch law) remain "mixed" systems in which Roman rules are still cited.

:::claim interpretation
Modern lawyers who distinguish ownership from possession, speak of usufruct or servitudes, or classify contracts by how they are formed are using categories worked out by Roman jurists. But the Roman law of modern codes is Roman law as reinterpreted by medieval and modern scholars for their own societies, not a direct survival of the law of the Severan jurists.
:::

## Language and letters {#language}

Spoken Latin evolved into Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, Catalan and other languages; Latin remained the language of the
Western Church, scholarship and science into the modern age, and continues in botanical and zoological names. A large share of English vocabulary
derives from Latin, directly or through French. Classical literature formed the core of European education for centuries.

## Politics {#politics}

Roman political vocabulary is everywhere: senate, republic, consul, dictator, veto, tribune, capitol, citizen, empire, emperor. Medieval rulers
claimed the title of Roman emperor: Charlemagne in 800, the Holy Roman Emperors until 1806, the Russian tsars (from Caesar), the Ottoman sultans
as *Kayser-i Rum*. Renaissance humanists and Machiavelli turned to the Roman Republic as a model of civic life. The American founders adopted
Roman symbols and ideals, and the French revolutionaries and then Napoleon drew on both the Republic and the empire.

## Three appropriations of Rome {#appropriations}

### The American republic

The founders of the United States read Roman history in school and in the political literature of the eighteenth century. They took the Roman Republic as a model of civic virtue and mixed government and as a warning of how republics decay into tyranny. The authors of the *Federalist* papers published under the name "Publius", after Publius Valerius Publicola, a legendary founder of the Republic. George Washington was praised as a modern Cincinnatus, the Roman who was said to have left his plough to save the state and returned to it afterwards; officers of the Revolutionary army founded the Society of the Cincinnati. The new republic built a Capitol and called its upper house the Senate. The founders took little interest in Roman slavery, although many of them were slaveholders, nor in the violence of Republican politics.

### Fascist Italy

Mussolini's regime made the cult of *romanità* central to its propaganda. A new avenue, the Via dell'Impero (1932), was driven through the Imperial Fora, demolishing medieval and Renaissance neighbourhoods to provide a parade route from the Colosseum to the Piazza Venezia. The bimillennium of Augustus' birth in 1937–38 was marked by a great exhibition, the Mostra Augustea della Romanità, the clearing of the area around the Mausoleum of Augustus, and the reassembly of the Ara Pacis in a new pavilion. The fasces, the bundle of rods carried before Roman magistrates, gave the movement its name. Archaeology served the state, and the Rome it presented was an imperial, militaristic and Italian Rome.{{ref:arthurs-2012}}

### The Orthodox world

Through Byzantium, the Roman empire's legacy passed to the Orthodox Christian peoples of eastern Europe. Bulgarian and Serbian rulers took the title *tsar*, from Caesar. After 1453, Ivan III of Moscow married Sophia Palaiologina, niece of the last emperor, and adopted the double-headed eagle; in the early sixteenth century a monk named Philotheos wrote to the grand prince that two Romes had fallen and Moscow was the third, and there would be no fourth. The idea of Moscow as the "Third Rome" has been revived at various times since, including in modern Russian politics.

:::claim interpretation
Each appropriation selected a different Rome: the virtuous Republic, the military empire, the Christian empire of Constantinople. Each tells us as much about the society that invoked Rome as about Rome itself.
:::

## Religion {#religion}

The Christian Church grew within the Roman empire and inherited much from it: the use of Latin and Greek, an organisation of bishops in cities
grouped in provinces and dioceses, the title *pontifex*, and the idea of a universal community. The Orthodox churches of eastern Europe
inherited the Christianity of Constantinople.

## Architecture and the city {#architecture}

Roman architecture, studied from ruins and Vitruvius by Renaissance architects, shaped European and later global building: domes, columns,
triumphal arches, basilicas. Neoclassical government buildings, banks and museums across the world communicate authority and permanence through
Roman forms. Roman city plans survive in the street grids of many European towns.

## Calendar and time {#calendar}

The Julian calendar, introduced by Caesar in 46 BC, was used in Europe until Pope Gregory XIII's reform of 1582 (adopted in Britain in 1752 and in
Russia in 1918), and is still used by some Orthodox churches. July and August are named after Julius Caesar and Augustus.

## Rome in the imagination {#imagination}

Rome has been continually reimagined: in Shakespeare's Roman plays, in Gibbon's history, in painting, opera and novels, and in films from *Ben-Hur*
to *Gladiator*. The "fall of Rome" has served as a warning for every age's anxieties: decadence, immigration, overextension, religion, bureaucracy,
inequality. Comparative history, such as Walter Scheidel's work on Rome and Han China, asks what was distinctive about Rome among world empires.{{ref:scheidel-2009}}

## Historiography {#historiography}

Historians increasingly study "reception": how later societies understood and used the ancient world. Reception studies show that the Rome we
know is shaped by the questions asked of it, from Gibbon writing in the age of the British Empire, to Mommsen in the age of German unification, to
Syme in the age of dictators. Understanding the legacy of Rome includes understanding our own position as interpreters.{{ref:bondanella-1987}}

:::aside A legacy of violence
Roman legacy includes conquest, enslavement and a model of empire that later colonial powers invoked to justify their own rule. Celebrating
Rome's achievements without acknowledging the costs reproduces the perspective of the conquerors. A historian's task is neither to admire nor to
condemn, but to understand the whole.
:::
