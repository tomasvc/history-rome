## The problem of evidence {#context}

Everything we know about Rome comes from what has survived, by chance and by choice, from a world that ended long ago. Only a fraction of ancient
literature survives; inscriptions, papyri, coins and archaeological remains survive unevenly, concentrated in certain places and periods. The
historian's craft is to ask what each piece of evidence can tell us, how it came to exist and survive, and how it fits with other evidence. This
lesson, which you may want to read first, sets out the tools used throughout the course.

## Types of evidence {#evidence}

- **Literary texts**: histories, biographies, speeches, letters, poetry, philosophy, technical treatises. They provide narratives and
  interpretations, but were written mostly by and for elite men, with their own purposes and conventions.
- **Inscriptions**: texts on stone or bronze, such as laws, decrees, dedications, epitaphs, building inscriptions, and the Res Gestae. Hundreds of
  thousands survive, collected in the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* (founded by Mommsen) and searchable online.
- **Papyri and tablets**: documents on papyrus, wax or wood, mostly from Egypt's dry sands and from sites like Vindolanda, recording everyday
  life: letters, contracts, petitions, accounts, census returns.
- **Coins**: official images and legends, datable and widely circulated, which show what rulers wanted to communicate and, through hoards and
  finds, economic activity.
- **Archaeology**: buildings, objects, landscapes, skeletons and environmental remains, including evidence that no text records.
- **Scientific evidence**: ice cores, tree rings, ancient DNA and isotopes, increasingly important for environment, disease and mobility.

## Ancient historians {#ancient}

Roman historiography began around 200 BC with Fabius Pictor, writing in Greek. Ancient historians shared conventions very different from those of
modern historians: they wrote literature for educated readers, aiming to instruct and to please; they rarely cited sources; they composed speeches
for their characters; and they explained events through character and morality.

- **Polybius** analysed Rome's rise with a statesman's experience and a claim to rigour.
- **Livy** wrote a moral, patriotic history of Rome from its foundation.

:::source livy-preface
:::

- **Tacitus** analysed the principate with brilliant, dark irony.

:::source tacitus-annals-1
:::

- **Suetonius** wrote biographies organised by topic, rich in anecdote.
- **Cassius Dio** wrote a vast Roman history in Greek, partly from his own experience as a senator under the Severans.
- **Ammianus Marcellinus**, a Greek-speaking former officer, wrote the last great Latin history of Rome in the 380s–390s.
- **Procopius** wrote the wars of Justinian in classicising Greek, and a secret invective against him.

## One event, several sources: the fire of AD 64 {#fire}

Comparing accounts of a single event shows how historians work. The fire that destroyed much of Rome in July 64 is described by three ancient writers.

- **Tacitus** (*Annals* 15.38–44, written c. 115–120) says it is uncertain whether the fire was an accident or the emperor's crime, since writers give both versions. He describes Nero's relief measures, then reports the rumour that Nero sang of the fall of Troy, and his new building regulations; he says Nero blamed the Christians, whom he describes with contempt while noting that their punishments aroused pity.
- **Suetonius** (*Nero* 38, c. 120) states flatly that Nero started the fire and watched it from the tower of Maecenas, singing of the sack of Troy.
- **Cassius Dio** (62.16–18, early third century) also blames Nero and gives a dramatic picture of looters and confusion.

The differences reveal the historians' methods. Tacitus reports uncertainty and multiple versions, yet arranges his material so that suspicion falls on Nero. Suetonius, writing biography organised by the emperor's vices, selects the most damning version. Dio, writing a century later, follows the hostile tradition. Archaeologists have found burn layers in several areas of the city, confirming the scale of destruction but not its cause. A modern historian might conclude that the cause is unknown, that Nero's relief efforts were real, that the rumours spread because of his unpopularity and his use of the burned land for his palace, and that the punishment of Christians is well attested.

## Exposing a forgery: the Donation of Constantine {#donation}

For centuries popes and their supporters cited a document in which Constantine, cured of leprosy and baptised by Pope Sylvester, supposedly gave the pope authority over the city of Rome and the western provinces of the empire. The *Donation of Constantine* was probably composed in the eighth century, when the papacy was establishing its independence from Constantinople.

In 1440 the Italian humanist Lorenzo Valla, then in the service of the king of Naples, who was in conflict with the pope, demonstrated that it was a forgery. His arguments remain a model of source criticism:

- **Language.** The Latin contains words and usages that did not exist in the fourth century, such as titles and phrases from later centuries.
- **Historical impossibility.** It refers to Constantinople as already existing and to institutions that did not yet exist at the supposed date.
- **Silence of the sources.** No contemporary historian, law or coin mentions such a gift, which would have been one of the most important acts of Constantine's reign.
- **Motive.** The document's purpose, to support papal claims, explained why it had been created.

:::claim established
Valla's treatise, printed in 1517 and taken up by Protestant reformers, showed that philology could be used to test historical claims against the evidence of language and context. The same techniques, refined over five centuries, underlie the detection of the fictional documents in the [[lesson:trajan-hadrian#evidence|Historia Augusta]] and the editing of every ancient text in this course.
:::

## Reading an inscription {#inscriptions}

Inscriptions require their own skills. They use abbreviations (COS for consul, TRIB POT for tribunician power, D M for "to the spirits of the dead"), conventional formulae and standard structures. Epigraphers restore missing letters in square brackets and mark uncertain readings. A building inscription names the donor and the occasion; an epitaph names the dead, their age, their family and sometimes their career. Dated inscriptions, for example those that give an emperor's titles and years of tribunician power, are among the most precise chronological evidence available.

## A checklist for any source {#checklist}

Whatever the source, the same questions apply.

1. **What is it?** A history, a law, a letter, a coin, a building, a skeleton?
2. **Who made it, when and where?** How close were they to the events?
3. **For whom, and why?** What did the maker want the audience to think or do?
4. **How has it reached us?** Is it an original, a copy, a translation, an excerpt, a restoration?
5. **What does it assume?** What values and categories does it take for granted?
6. **What does it leave out?** Whose voices are missing?
7. **What else confirms or contradicts it?**

Every primary source in this course is presented with notes on these questions.

## Modern historiography {#modern}

**The Renaissance and Enlightenment.** Humanists recovered ancient texts and studied inscriptions and coins; Machiavelli wrote *Discourses on Livy*.
Edward Gibbon's *History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776–1789) combined narrative brilliance with Enlightenment scepticism
about religion.{{ref:gibbon-1776}}

**Nineteenth-century source criticism.** Barthold Niebuhr subjected the traditions about early Rome to systematic criticism. Theodor Mommsen
wrote a celebrated *Roman History* (1854–1856), for which he won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1902, and organised the systematic collection of
inscriptions and the study of Roman public law.{{ref:mommsen-1854}}

**Twentieth-century approaches.** Michael Rostovtzeff wrote a social and economic history of the empire; Ronald Syme's *The Roman Revolution*
(1939) analysed Augustus' rise through the networks of the elite; Arnaldo Momigliano studied historiography itself; Moses Finley brought social
science to ancient economy and slavery; Peter Brown created the field of late antiquity. Since then, social, gender, cultural, environmental and
post-colonial history have transformed the subject.

## Digital tools {#digital}

Digital resources have transformed the field: gazetteers of ancient places (Pleiades), databases of inscriptions (the Epigraphik-Datenbank
Clauss-Slaby) and papyri (papyri.info), coin catalogues, network models of travel (ORBIS), and digitised texts. This course's atlas and
references draw on several of them, and the library lists them so you can explore for yourself.

## How to use this course {#use}

Throughout the course you will see labels: **established** for points on which the evidence and scholarly consensus are strong;
**interpretation** for reasonable readings that historians could dispute; **contested** for questions where serious scholars disagree; and
**speculative** for hypotheses with weak evidence. Primary sources are presented with their context, author, audience and limitations; modern
works are listed in the library with full references. Treat every claim, including those in this course, as something to test.

:::aside Absence of evidence
In ancient history, absence of evidence is rarely evidence of absence. If no source mentions something, it may not have happened, or it may not
have been recorded, or the record may not have survived. Before arguing from silence, ask whether we would expect a source to mention it.
:::
