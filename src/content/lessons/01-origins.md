## Historical context {#context}

Rome began as one community among many in Latium, the plain south of the Tiber, at a time when the most powerful peoples of
Italy were the Etruscans to the north, the Greek colonies of the south, and the Phoenicians whose trading posts ringed the
western Mediterranean. Its advantages were geographical. The site lay at the lowest point where the Tiber could be easily
crossed, about 25 km from the sea, where the river route inland met the north–south route along the coast. Nearby salt beds at
the river mouth fed a trade along what became the Via Salaria. A cluster of defensible hills (the Palatine and Capitol above all)
stood beside a marshy valley that could be drained.{{ref:cornell-1995}}

Archaeology shows people living on the Palatine and neighbouring hills from at least the tenth century BC, burying their dead in
cemeteries in the valley that later became the Forum and on the Esquiline. Post-holes for oval huts on the Palatine date to the
eighth century: the Romans themselves preserved a hut they called the "House of Romulus" there into imperial times. These were
villages of farmers and herders whose material culture archaeologists call Latial, shared with communities across Latium.

:::claim established
By the eighth century BC there were substantial settlements on the site of Rome, with cemeteries, huts and evidence of exchange with
Etruria and the Greek world. The Roman tradition of a single act of foundation on 21 April 753 BC is not supported by this evidence,
which points to gradual growth over several centuries.
:::

The seventh century transformed Italy. Contact with Phoenician and Greek traders brought the alphabet, new luxuries and new ways
of displaying wealth. Rich "orientalising" tombs in Latium and Etruria show a warrior aristocracy competing through feasting,
gifts and grave goods. Rome shared in this world. During the seventh century the low ground between the hills was drained and
given a beaten-earth surface, the first paving of what became the Forum: an act that required a community capable of collective
decisions and labour.{{note:Archaeologists debate whether to call this the moment of "urbanisation"; a common public space is one
of several criteria, together with monumental temples, defensive walls and a sense of the community as a single political unit.}}

## Two chronologies {#chronology}

It helps to keep two chronologies side by side: the one the Romans told, and the one the evidence supports.

:::timeline
c. 1000–800 BC | Hut villages and cemeteries on and around the Palatine; Latial Iron Age culture | early-settlement
753 BC | Traditional foundation by Romulus (a date calculated in the first century BC) | foundation-rome
753–509 BC | Seven kings in the tradition: Romulus, Numa, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius, Tarquinius Superbus
c. 650–600 BC | First paving of the Forum; early buildings on the site of the later Regia | forum-paved
c. 570–550 BC | The Lapis Niger inscription, mentioning a *rex*, is set up in the Comitium
509 BC | Traditional expulsion of the Tarquins; dedication of the Capitoline temple; first consuls | expulsion-kings
c. 500 BC | The Lapis Satricanus records the "companions of Publius Valerius"
509/508 BC | First treaty between Rome and Carthage, according to Polybius
c. 200 BC | Quintus Fabius Pictor writes the first Roman history, in Greek
:::

The traditional reign lengths give seven kings an average of 35 years each, an implausibly long span for a succession of rulers
who, according to the same tradition, were mostly murdered or killed. The number seven, the neat allocation of institutions to
particular kings, and the parallels with Greek stories all suggest systematic reconstruction by later writers.{{ref:forsythe-2005}}
Yet the sixth century, the age of the last three kings, is much better attested by archaeology than the earlier period, and it is
here that tradition and evidence come closest together.

## The kings and the stories told about them {#people}

:::people
romulus tarquinius-superbus livy
:::

Roman tradition fused two foundation stories. One made Aeneas, a Trojan prince and son of Venus, the ancestor of the Romans; Greek
writers were connecting Aeneas with Rome by the fifth century BC. The other made Romulus, son of Mars and a Vestal Virgin, the
founder. Because the fall of Troy was dated around 1184 BC, later writers filled the gap with a line of kings at Alba Longa
descended from Aeneas, ending with the twins Romulus and Remus.

Each king became the "inventor" of an institution. Romulus founded the Senate and divided the people into curiae; Numa, a peaceful
Sabine, created the priesthoods and the calendar; Tullus Hostilius was the warrior who destroyed Alba Longa; Ancus Marcius
extended Roman territory to the sea at Ostia. The last three kings form a group associated with Etruscan culture. Tarquinius
Priscus, said to be the son of a Corinthian exile settled at Tarquinii, built great public works; Servius Tullius, of disputed
origin, organised the people by wealth for military service and voting; Tarquinius Superbus, the tyrant, completed the great
temple on the Capitol and was expelled.

These are stories with purposes. The asylum Romulus opened to refugees, the rape of the Sabine women, and the absorption of Sabines
and Albans into the citizen body express a lasting Roman idea that Rome grew by incorporating outsiders, a theme the emperor
Claudius would use in AD 48 when arguing that Gauls should sit in the Senate.{{anc:Tac. Ann. 11.24}} The murder of Remus by his
brother made fratricide the original sin of the city, a theme that resonated painfully with writers of the civil-war era.

:::aside A Roman tradition from outside Rome
In his speech of AD 48, preserved on the Lyon Tablet, Claudius drew on Etruscan sources to identify Servius Tullius with an Etruscan
adventurer called Mastarna. Paintings in the François Tomb at Vulci (fourth century BC) show a "Macstrna" freeing a captive and
fighting men including a "Cneve Tarchunies Rumach", Gnaeus Tarquinius of Rome. These suggest that Etruscan cities told their own,
quite different stories about sixth-century Rome, as a city fought over by Etruscan warlords.
:::

## Institutions of the regal period {#institutions}

Some institutions plainly go back to the monarchy, because later Romans preserved them as fossils. A priest called the *rex
sacrorum* ("king of the sacred rites") continued to perform the king's religious duties throughout the Republic, but was barred
from political office. The *interrex*, who held power for five days when there were no consuls, preserves a procedure for choosing
a new king. A building in the Forum called the Regia ("royal house") served the pontifex maximus. The thirty curiae, ancient
divisions of the people, survived as a formal assembly (the comitia curiata) that conferred imperium on magistrates.

The most important surviving document is the Lapis Niger, an inscribed stone found in 1899 beneath a black marble pavement in the
Comitium, the meeting place beside the Senate house. Written in a very early form of Latin, its lines run alternately left-to-right
and right-to-left, and the stone is badly damaged. It seems to record a sacred law, and it includes the word RECEI, an early dative
form of *rex*.

:::claim established
The Lapis Niger, dated to the sixth century BC, shows that Rome had a king (*rex*) and that public rules were inscribed in the
political centre of the city. It confirms the existence of kingship, though not the specific kings of the literary tradition.
:::

The great temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitol, whose foundations survive, was among the largest temples in the
Mediterranean in its day, built in an Etruscan style with terracotta decoration by craftsmen from Veii. A city capable of
such a project was wealthy and populous. Polybius quotes a treaty between Rome and Carthage which he dates to the first year of the
Republic; in it Carthage undertakes not to harm Latin coastal towns "subject to the Romans".{{anc:Polyb. 3.22}} If the date is
right, as many historians accept, late regal Rome was already the dominant power on the Latin coast.

## Why did Rome grow? {#causation}

No single cause explains why this community, rather than its neighbours, became the leading city of Latium. Historians stress a
combination:

- **Location.** Control of the Tiber crossing and the salt routes made Rome a natural meeting place of Latins, Etruscans and Sabines,
  and a market.
- **Openness.** The traditions of the asylum, the Sabine merger and immigrant kings may exaggerate, but Rome does seem to have
  absorbed outsiders readily, including aristocratic clans that moved with their followers. The Claudii were said to have arrived as
  Sabine immigrants around 504 BC.
- **Etruscan influence.** Rome adopted Etruscan religious and architectural forms and was ruled in the sixth century by kings with
  Etruscan connections. It was not "conquered" by the Etruscans so much as part of a shared Tyrrhenian world.
- **Organisation for war.** The reforms attributed to Servius Tullius, which organised citizens by wealth into classes that supplied
  heavy infantry, may reflect a real sixth-century shift to hoplite-style warfare that required broad participation of property
  owners.

:::claim interpretation
The "Servian" reforms almost certainly did not create, in one act, the elaborate centuriate system described by Livy and Dionysius,
which reflects later conditions. But a sixth-century reorganisation of the citizen body for military purposes, by wealth rather than
by birth, is plausible and fits developments elsewhere in central Italy.
:::

## The end of kingship and what it left {#consequences}

The tradition dates the expulsion of the Tarquins to 509 BC. The story of Lucretia, raped by the king's son and choosing death
over dishonour, made the revolution a moral drama about the abuse of power and the chastity of Roman women. Modern historians are
more interested in what changed: kingship was replaced by annually elected magistrates (later called consuls), the aristocracy
(the patricians) consolidated its control over priesthoods and the Senate, and Rome seems to have lost ground for a generation,
fighting the Latins and Etruscan forces under Lars Porsenna of Clusium, who may even have captured the city.

The memory of kingship shaped Roman political culture for centuries. To call a man a would-be king (*rex*) was the most damaging
political accusation; Tiberius Gracchus, Caesar and others were attacked in these terms. At the same time, the religious functions
of kingship survived in the rex sacrorum, and the powers of the king were divided rather than abolished: the consuls inherited his
imperium, his insignia (the fasces carried by lictors) and his command in war.

## How much can we know? {#historiography}

:::debate early-rome-sources
:::

Modern scepticism about early Rome began in the nineteenth century with Barthold Niebuhr, who suggested that the stories derived
from lost aristocratic banquet songs, and was sharpened by later scholars who argued that most of the narrative was invented by
annalists of the second and first centuries BC. Since the 1970s, excavation in the Forum, on the Palatine and across Latium has
transformed the picture. T. J. Cornell argued that while the narrative details are largely fiction, a core of structural
information (about institutions, social organisation and the scale of the city) was transmitted and is broadly confirmed by
archaeology.{{ref:cornell-1995}} Gary Forsythe and others are more sceptical.{{ref:forsythe-2005}} At the other extreme, the
archaeologist Andrea Carandini has argued that excavations of an eighth-century wall on the Palatine confirm a deliberate act of
foundation close to the traditional date; most historians regard this as reading the legend into ambiguous evidence.{{ref:carandini-2011}}

:::

## The evidence {#evidence}

The first Roman to write history, the senator Quintus Fabius Pictor, wrote in Greek around 200 BC, three centuries after the end of
the monarchy. What could he have known? There were lists of magistrates (the *fasti*), whose earliest parts may have been
reconstructed; the commentaries of the pontiffs, which recorded prodigies, famines and events year by year; treaties inscribed in
temples, like the one Polybius saw; laws such as the Twelve Tables; family traditions and funeral eulogies (which Livy and Cicero
both warn exaggerated their ancestors' deeds); and Greek writers who had noticed Rome. Livy, writing under Augustus, is frank about
the problem.

:::source livy-preface
Livy states plainly that the foundation stories are "more fit for poetry" than history, then narrates them anyway. Read the full
source to see the reasons he gives.
:::

:::check or-1
:::

Documents from the period itself are few and precious. Besides the Lapis Niger, the Lapis Satricanus, found at Satricum in 1977 and
dated around 500 BC, records a dedication to Mars by "the companions (*suodales*) of Publius Valerius". It shows aristocratic leaders
with armed followings operating across community boundaries, exactly the kind of figure (Valerius Publicola was one of the first
consuls in Livy) that the tradition remembers, though identifying the two is speculative.

:::claim speculative
Some historians identify the Publius Valerius of the Satricum inscription with P. Valerius Publicola, consul in the first years of the
Republic. The name was common in the Valerian clan, and the identification cannot be proved.
:::

The lesson for the student is methodological. For early Rome, every statement needs a label: confirmed by contemporary material
evidence, plausible structural inference, or later story. The habit of asking "how could anyone have known this?" is the most
important skill this course aims to build.
