## Historical context {#context}

After 121 BC the Senate's leaders appeared to have won. The Gracchi were dead, the land commission was wound down, and the nobility
monopolised the consulship. Within a generation that position collapsed. A war in North Africa exposed senatorial corruption and
incompetence; a migration of Germanic peoples destroyed Roman armies; a "new man" from the country town of Arpinum was elected consul
seven times; Rome's allies fought a ferocious war to become Romans; and a patrician consul led his army against the city. This lesson
traces those forty years.

## Jugurtha and the new man {#jugurtha}

Jugurtha, an adopted grandson of Masinissa, seized the kingdom of Numidia by killing his cousins and, in 112, massacred Italian
traders in the town of Cirta. Sallust's monograph on the war, written in the 40s BC, makes it a story of a corrupt nobility: Jugurtha
bribed envoys and commanders and, summoned to Rome, is said to have called it "a city for sale and doomed to perish quickly if it
finds a buyer".{{anc:Sall. Iug. 35}} The phrase may be Sallust's, but the war certainly went badly and embarrassed the Senate.

C. Marius, the son of a well-to-do family from Arpinum, had served at Numantia, risen through the offices with the support of the
Metelli, and married into the patrician Julii (he was the uncle by marriage of Julius Caesar). As legate under Q. Caecilius Metellus
in Numidia he campaigned for the consulship, attacking the nobility's arrogance, and was elected for 107 on a wave of popular anger.
An assembly vote transferred the Numidian command from Metellus to him, overriding the Senate's allocation.

To fill his army Marius enrolled volunteers, many of them without enough property to be registered in the classes (the *capite
censi*, "counted by head").

The war was ended in 105 when Marius' quaestor, L. Cornelius Sulla, persuaded Jugurtha's father-in-law, Bocchus of Mauretania, to
betray him. Marius triumphed; Sulla wore a signet ring showing the surrender. Their rivalry began here.

## The northern invasions and Marius' consulships {#cimbri}

The Cimbri and Teutones, peoples from northern Europe on the move with their families, had defeated a Roman army in 113 and others
afterwards. In 105, at Arausio (Orange) on the Rhône, two Roman armies whose commanders refused to cooperate were destroyed; ancient
sources give enormous casualty figures. Italy appeared open to invasion. Marius was elected consul for 104 in his absence, and then
again every year until 100, in violation of the rules on repeated office. He reorganised and trained his army, and in 102 destroyed
the Teutones at Aquae Sextiae (Aix-en-Provence); in 101, with Q. Lutatius Catulus, he destroyed the Cimbri at Vercellae in northern
Italy.

### The "Marian reforms"

Later writers and many modern textbooks credit Marius with transforming the Roman army: opening it to the poor, standardising
equipment, replacing the maniple with the cohort of about 480 men as the main tactical unit, making the eagle the standard of every
legion, and making soldiers carry their own baggage ("Marius' mules").{{anc:Plut. Mar. 13; Plin. HN 10.16}}

:::claim contested
How much of this was Marius' work is disputed. The minimum property qualification for service had been lowered several times during
the second century, so many poor men already served; cohorts appear in earlier campaigns; and change in equipment and recruitment was
probably gradual. Recent studies (for example by François Cadiou) argue that the Roman army of the first century was still raised
largely by conscription and that the notion of a sudden professionalisation is a modern construction.
:::

What is clearer is a political consequence. Veterans who had no land expected their general to secure it for them when they were
discharged. In 103 and 100 Marius worked with the tribune L. Appuleius Saturninus to pass laws granting land to his veterans. In 100
Saturninus' followers murdered a rival candidate; the Senate passed its ultimate decree, and Marius, as consul, had to suppress his own
ally. Saturninus and his supporters were lynched in the Senate house. Marius' political standing never recovered from that ambiguous
role.

## The Social War, 91–88 BC {#social-war}

Rome's Italian allies ([[term:socii]]) supplied at least half of the soldiers in its armies, yet they had no vote, no share in the
profits of empire comparable to Romans, and no protection against the arbitrary behaviour of Roman magistrates. Some had sought
citizenship since the Gracchan years. In 91 the tribune M. Livius Drusus, son of Gaius Gracchus' opponent, proposed a wide programme
that included citizenship for the allies; it failed and he was murdered.

The allies, led by the Marsi in central Italy and the Samnites in the south, revolted. They created a federal state called Italia with
a capital at Corfinium, a senate and consuls, and minted coins; some show the Italian bull goring the Roman wolf. The war was fought by
men trained in the same way and often by veterans who had served side by side. It was savage; ancient writers put the dead in the
hundreds of thousands, and the figures cannot be checked.

Rome won the war by conceding what the allies had demanded. The lex Julia of 90 offered citizenship to communities that had stayed
loyal or laid down their arms, and the lex Plautia Papiria of 89 extended it to individuals who registered; the Transpadane Gauls
received Latin rights. By the 80s nearly all free inhabitants of Italy south of the Po had become Roman citizens.

:::claim interpretation
Why did the allies want citizenship? Historians have stressed different motives: protection against Roman magistrates, access to the
benefits of empire (land, contracts, provincial profits), a voice in decisions about war, or equal status as such. Some, following
ancient hints, argue that a part of the rebels sought independence from Rome rather than inclusion. The answer probably varied among
communities and social groups.
:::

The integration of Italy had huge long-term consequences. Rome's citizen body tripled. Italian elites began to enter Roman politics,
and over the next century "Roman" increasingly meant "Italian". But in the short term the distribution of new citizens among the
voting tribes became a bitter political issue.

## Mithridates and the march on Rome, 88 BC {#march}

While Italy was at war, Mithridates VI of Pontus, the most capable king in the Hellenistic East, overran the province of Asia, where
Roman tax-farmers and moneylenders were hated. In 88 he ordered the killing of all Romans and Italians in Asian cities on a single
day; ancient writers give 80,000 or more victims, numbers that may be exaggerated. Athens and much of Greece went over to him.

Sulla, consul in 88, received the command against him. A tribune, P. Sulpicius Rufus, then passed laws distributing the new citizens
across all the tribes and transferring the Mithridatic command to the elderly Marius. Sulla went to his army at Nola in Campania and
told his soldiers that they would lose the war, and its plunder, to others. They urged him to lead them to Rome. Appian reports that
all his officers except one quaestor refused to follow.{{anc:App. B Civ. 1.57}} For the first time a Roman army entered the city in
arms against Romans. Sulpicius was killed; Marius escaped to Africa. Sulla passed laws and left for the East.

:::claim interpretation
The soldiers' choice matters as much as Sulla's. They followed a commander who promised them a profitable war, against an assembly
that had legally given it to someone else. Whether they did so because they were poor men dependent on their general, because they
were loyal to a consul whose command had been taken by violent means, or simply for booty, is debated.
:::

In 87 the consul L. Cornelius Cinna, opposing Sulla's arrangements, was driven from Rome; he raised an army, joined Marius, and took
the city. Marius' followers carried out a massacre of their opponents. Marius entered his seventh consulship in 86 and died within
days. Cinna dominated Rome until his own soldiers killed him in 84.

## Sulla's victory and dictatorship {#dictator}

In the East Sulla besieged and sacked Athens (86), defeated Mithridates' armies, and made a quick peace at Dardanus (85) that left the
king on his throne, so that he could return to Italy. He extracted 20,000 talents from the cities of Asia. In 83 he landed at
Brundisium. Young men raised armies to join him, among them the twenty-three-year-old Cn. Pompeius (Pompey) with three legions
recruited from his father's clients in Picenum, and M. Licinius Crassus. After a hard civil war, in which the Samnites fought on the
Marian side, Sulla won the battle of the Colline Gate outside Rome in November 82.

Sulla then published lists of those declared outlaws (the [[term:proscription|proscriptions]]): anyone could kill them for a reward,
their property was confiscated and sold, and their sons and grandsons were barred from office. Many were killed for their wealth;
Crassus laid the foundations of his fortune by buying confiscated property. Whole communities that had resisted had their land
confiscated for Sulla's veterans; at Pompeii a colony of veterans was imposed beside the existing population.

An assembly appointed Sulla dictator "for writing laws and setting the state in order", with no time limit. His programme aimed to
restore senatorial control:

- the Senate was doubled to about 600, with new members from the equites and Italian elites, and future quaestors (now 20 a year)
  entered it automatically, which ended the censors' control of membership;
- the extortion and other criminal courts were expanded into a system of standing courts, all with senatorial juries;
- tribunes were barred from further office, their veto was restricted and their legislation probably required prior senatorial
  approval, making the office a dead end;
- the cursus honorum was enforced with minimum ages and intervals; praetors increased to eight;
- a treason law forbade governors to leave their province or make war without authority.

In 79, or perhaps 80, Sulla laid down his dictatorship and in 78 died in Campania. His epitaph is said to have claimed that no friend
had surpassed him in kindness nor enemy in harm.{{anc:Plut. Sull. 38}}

## Consequences {#consequences}

Sulla's settlement collapsed quickly. In 77 Pompey, who had held no office, was given a command in Spain against the Marian Sertorius;
in 70, as consuls, Pompey and Crassus restored the tribunes' powers, and the juries were divided among senators, equites and the
*tribuni aerarii*. But what Sulla had done could not be undone: he had shown that a general could take Rome and then govern it by
proscription. Caesar, who as a young man escaped the proscriptions, is supposed to have said that Sulla did not know his political
ABCs when he gave up the dictatorship.{{anc:Suet. Iul. 77}}

## Historiography {#historiography}

Historians of the fall of the Republic often divide between those who emphasise structural causes, such as the army, the agrarian
problem and the allies, and those who emphasise political culture and choices. P. A. Brunt traced the role of poor soldiers and the
land question;{{ref:brunt-1988}} Harriet Flower identifies Sulla's dictatorship as the end of the "republic of the nobles" and the
creation of a new, short-lived republic with its own rules.{{ref:flower-2010}} The traditional picture of the Marian army as a body of
landless professionals bound to their generals is being revised by military historians, which in turn changes how we explain 88 and
49 BC.{{ref:cadiou-2018}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Coins as voices of the losers
The Italian rebels of 91–88 BC wrote no histories that survive, but their coins do: legends in Oscan and Latin naming *Italia* or
*Víteliú*, images of oath-taking warriors and of the Italian bull trampling the Roman wolf. Coins are some of the very few documents
produced by those who fought Rome rather than by Romans writing about them.
:::
