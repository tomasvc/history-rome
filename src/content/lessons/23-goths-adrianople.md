## Historical context {#context}

By the fourth century, Goths had lived along the lower Danube and north of the Black Sea for over a hundred years. They had raided the empire
in the third century, but also traded with it, served in its armies and, in some cases, converted to Christianity, translated into Gothic by
the bishop Ulfila in a non-Nicene form. In the 370s the Huns, nomadic horsemen from the steppe, attacked the Gothic groups north of the Black
Sea. In 376 large numbers of Goths, mainly the Tervingi under Fritigern and Alavivus, gathered on the Danube and asked to be admitted to the
empire.

## The crossing and Adrianople {#adrianople}

The emperor Valens, campaigning against Persia, saw the Goths as potential recruits and taxpayers. He admitted the Tervingi; others,
including the Greuthungi, were refused but crossed anyway. The Roman officials in charge mishandled the operation: food ran short and,
according to Ammianus, officials sold dogs to starving Goths in exchange for their children as slaves.{{anc:Amm. Marc. 31.4.11}} An attempt
to murder the Gothic leaders at a banquet at Marcianople led to open revolt in 377. The Goths, joined by other groups and by discontented
slaves and miners, ravaged Thrace.

Valens returned from the East and, on 9 August 378, attacked the Gothic wagon camp near Adrianople without waiting for his nephew Gratian,
the western emperor, who was marching to join him. The attack went wrong; Gothic cavalry returning from foraging fell on the Roman flank, and
the Roman infantry was surrounded and destroyed. Valens was killed, along with most of his senior officers and, according to Ammianus, two
thirds of the army.

## Ammianus Marcellinus: the soldier-historian {#ammianus}

Our account of Adrianople depends heavily on one man. Ammianus Marcellinus, from a well-off family of Antioch, served in the 350s as a staff officer (*protector domesticus*) under the general Ursicinus, escaped from the Persian siege of Amida in 359, and accompanied Julian on his Persian campaign in 363. Settling in Rome, he wrote a Latin history continuing Tacitus from 96 to 378; the surviving books, 14 to 31, cover 353 to 378. He ends with a famous self-description: he has written as "a former soldier and a Greek", and leaves later events to younger writers.{{anc:Amm. Marc. 31.16.9}}

His strengths are those of a participant: precise knowledge of military practice, vivid eyewitness passages, wide geographical and scientific digressions, and a willingness to criticise emperors, including Valens, whose greed and suspicion he blames for many disasters. His weaknesses are equally visible. He admired Julian to the point of hero-worship; his description of the Huns as barely human, eating raw meat warmed under their saddles, repeats the stereotypes Greek writers had applied to steppe nomads for centuries; and his narrative of Adrianople, written about a decade later, combines information from survivors with the rhetorical conventions of classical battle description.

:::claim established
Ammianus was not present at Adrianople, but he knew men who were and he had seen the region. Most historians accept his outline of the campaign and battle while treating his figures, speeches and explanations of motive with care.
:::

## Theodosius and the treaty of 382 {#treaty}

Gratian appointed Theodosius, a Spanish general, as emperor in the East in 379. After years of inconclusive fighting, in 382 the Goths were
settled in the Balkans. The terms are not preserved in a single source and are debated: it seems that they received land in return for
military service, under their own leaders, rather than being dispersed among Roman units, which would make them a new kind of autonomous
federate (*[[term:foederati|foederatus]]*) group within the empire.

:::claim contested
Historians disagree about how novel the treaty of 382 was. Some see it as a fundamental break, creating an autonomous barbarian people inside
the empire, the first step towards the barbarian kingdoms. Others argue that it was an arrangement in a long tradition of settling defeated
groups, made distinctive only by later events.
:::

Gothic contingents fought for Theodosius in his civil wars, including at the Frigidus in 394, where they suffered heavy losses. After his
death in January 395 the empire was divided between his sons: Arcadius, eighteen, in Constantinople, and Honorius, ten, in the West, under
the guardianship of the general Stilicho, himself the son of a Vandal officer and a Roman mother, and married to Theodosius' niece.

## Alaric and Stilicho {#alaric}

Alaric, a Gothic leader who had commanded at the Frigidus, led his followers in revolt in 395, seeking a Roman command and supplies for his
people. He played the rival courts of East and West against each other, ravaged Greece, and was at times appointed to Roman military office
in Illyricum. He invaded Italy in 401–402 and was defeated by Stilicho, who also destroyed the invading army of Radagaisus in 406. Honorius
moved his court to Ravenna, protected by marshes.

At the end of 406 (the traditional date is 31 December) a mass of Vandals, Alans and Sueves crossed the Rhine into Gaul. The army in Britain
proclaimed a usurper, Constantine III, who crossed to Gaul. The western empire lost control of Britain, which by 410 was left to defend
itself, and of much of Gaul and Spain.

In 408 a palace coup at Ravenna, exploiting suspicion of Stilicho's dealings with Alaric and his "barbarian" origin, led to his execution.
Roman soldiers then massacred the families of barbarian troops in Italian cities, and thousands of those troops joined Alaric.

## The East's Gothic crisis: Gainas, AD 400 {#gainas}

The western empire was not alone in depending on generals of barbarian origin. In 399–400 Gainas, a Gothic officer who had risen to be a senior commander (*magister militum*), used his army to force the eastern emperor Arcadius to hand over leading ministers and to give him control of the government. Some in Constantinople urged resistance. Synesius of Cyrene, then in the capital on an embassy, wrote a speech *On Kingship* urging the emperor to lead his armies in person, to recruit citizens rather than barbarians, and to expel Goths from positions of command.

In July 400, while Gainas was outside the city, the population of Constantinople rose against the Gothic soldiers within the walls and killed thousands of them, burning some alive in a church where they had taken refuge. Gainas tried to cross into Asia Minor and was defeated by the fleet of Fravitta, himself a Goth and a pagan, who had long served Rome loyally. Fleeing north of the Danube, Gainas was killed by the Hunnic king Uldin, who sent his head to Constantinople.

:::claim interpretation
The episode undermines any simple division between "Romans" and "barbarians": a Goth saved the eastern court from a Goth. It also suggests one reason the East fared better than the West: its capital could act against an overmighty general, and in the following decades its government relied more on generals who were integrated into the court and on native recruits, especially Isaurians from the mountains of Asia Minor. In the West, by contrast, power remained with the commanders of field armies, a contrast taken up in [[lesson:fall-of-the-west#causation|why the West fell]].
:::

## The sack of Rome {#sack}

Alaric besieged Rome three times between 408 and 410, seeking land, supplies and a high command from Honorius, who refused to negotiate
seriously from the safety of Ravenna. Alaric even set up a rival emperor, the senator Attalus. Finally, on 24 August 410, his Goths entered
Rome and sacked it for three days. They respected the churches of St Peter and St Paul as sanctuaries, but killed, raped, looted and took
captives, including the emperor's half-sister Galla Placidia.

Alaric moved south, intending to cross to Africa, but died in Calabria. His brother-in-law Athaulf led the Goths into Gaul and married Galla
Placidia. After further conflict, in 418 the Goths were settled in Aquitaine as federates; their kingdom, centred on Toulouse, became the
first of the barbarian kingdoms within the western empire.

The sack prompted pagans to blame the abandonment of the old gods. In reply, Augustine of Hippo wrote *The City of God* (413–426), arguing that
earthly cities, including Rome, rise and fall, and that the true home of Christians was the heavenly city.

## Who were the Goths? {#identity}

:::debate barbarian-identity
:::

Older historiography imagined the Goths as a nation that had migrated as a unit from Scandinavia, as the sixth-century writer Jordanes
claimed. Twentieth-century scholars of the "Vienna school" (Reinhard Wenskus, Herwig Wolfram, Walter Pohl) argued instead that barbarian
peoples were formed through "ethnogenesis": followings that coalesced around leaders and a core of traditions, absorbing people of diverse
origins.{{ref:wolfram-1988}} Walter Goffart and Guy Halsall went further, doubting large-scale migrations and stressing that groups like
Alaric's were Roman military forces as much as peoples.{{ref:halsall-2007}} Michael Kulikowski argued that the Goths as a people were
created by their relationship with Rome.{{ref:kulikowski-2007}} Peter Heather, by contrast, maintains that large groups, including women and
children, did migrate under Hunnic pressure, and that this pressure was a key external cause of the West's collapse.{{ref:heather-2005}}

:::check ga-3
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

Adrianople did not end the empire, and the eastern empire recovered from it. But it created a large, armed, autonomous group inside the
empire that Roman governments could neither destroy nor fully integrate. In the West, a weak court, rivalries between generals, the loss of
provinces after 406 and the dependence on barbarian soldiers combined into a crisis from which the western empire would not recover. The
sack of Rome in 410 marked the moment when contemporaries began to ask whether the empire could survive.

:::debate fall-of-west
:::

## Historiography and evidence {#historiography}

Ammianus Marcellinus, a Greek from Antioch who served as an officer and wrote his Latin history in Rome around 390, is our best source for
376–378. For the years after, we depend on fragments of lost Greek historians (Olympiodorus, Eunapius) preserved by Zosimus and Byzantine
excerptors, on church historians, chronicles and the poetry of Claudian, who wrote propaganda for Stilicho. Archaeology of the Balkans and of
the "Sântana de Mureș–Černjachov" culture north of the Black Sea, associated with the Goths, adds a material dimension, though linking
archaeological cultures to named peoples is itself controversial.

:::aside A word about "barbarian"
Romans used *barbarus* for peoples outside the Greco-Roman cultural world. Historians still use "barbarian" as a technical term for these
groups, but it carries Roman prejudice. Many "barbarians" of the fourth and fifth centuries were Christians, served as Roman officers, spoke
Latin or Greek, and saw themselves as part of the Roman world.
:::
