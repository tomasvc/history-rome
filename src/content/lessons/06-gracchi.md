## Historical context {#context}

In 133 BC Rome ruled most of the Mediterranean, but its politics were still those of a city-state run by a few hundred families. The
previous lesson described the strains that conquest had produced: great wealth concentrated in elite hands; large numbers of slaves;
a countryside where many citizens felt they had lost their land; an army that depended on property-owning citizens; allies who bore
much of the burden of war without political rights. Recruitment for the endless wars in Spain had met open resistance. In 137 a Roman
army under the consul Mancinus had surrendered at Numantia; the treaty, negotiated by his quaestor Tiberius Gracchus, was repudiated
by the Senate.

Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus came from the heart of the nobility. His father had been consul twice and censor; his mother, Cornelia,
was the daughter of Scipio Africanus; his sister was married to Scipio Aemilianus. He was elected tribune for 133.

## Tiberius Gracchus, 133 BC {#tiberius}

### The land law

Rome's conquests in Italy had created large areas of public land ([[term:ager-publicus]]). Much of it had been occupied by wealthy
Romans and Italians, who paid little or no rent, built villas and tombs on it and passed it on to heirs. An old law, perhaps of the
fourth century, limited holdings of public land to 500 iugera (about 125 hectares), but it had long been ignored.

Tiberius' law reaffirmed the limit, allowing an extra 250 iugera for each of up to two sons, and gave the excess back to the state.
Occupiers would receive secure title to what they kept. The reclaimed land would be distributed in small allotments (perhaps 30
iugera) to poor citizens; the allotments could not be sold, to stop the rich buying them back. A commission of three, initially
Tiberius, his brother Gaius and his father-in-law Appius Claudius, would carry out the work.

:::

The purpose, according to Appian, was to restore a class of small farmers who could serve in the legions, and so to solve the
manpower problem.{{anc:App. B Civ. 1.9–11}} Plutarch reports that Gaius later said his brother had been inspired by the sight of
Etruria worked by foreign slaves as he travelled to Numantia.{{anc:Plut. Ti. Gracch. 8}}

### Escalation

The law was not in itself revolutionary. It had distinguished senatorial supporters, including the consul P. Mucius Scaevola and the
leader of the Senate, Appius Claudius. But Tiberius took it directly to the plebeian assembly without first consulting the Senate,
which was legal but contrary to custom. When another tribune, M. Octavius, vetoed it, Tiberius made an unprecedented move: he had the
assembly vote Octavius out of office, arguing that a tribune who acted against the people's interest ceased to be a tribune. The law
then passed.

Next, when the Senate starved the commission of funds, news arrived that Attalus III of Pergamum had died and left his kingdom to the
Roman people. Tiberius proposed that the people, not the Senate, decide how to use the inheritance, and use it to equip the new
settlers. This intruded on the Senate's control of finance and foreign affairs. Finally he stood for re-election as tribune for 132,
which was not clearly illegal but without recent precedent. His enemies claimed he was aiming at kingship; one story told of a
Pergamene envoy bringing him a royal diadem and purple robe.

On election day the Senate met in the temple of Fides. When the consul Scaevola refused to use force against a citizen without trial,
the pontifex maximus P. Cornelius Scipio Nasica, Tiberius' own cousin, called on those who wished to save the state to follow him.
Senators and their attendants, armed with clubs and the legs of benches, attacked Tiberius' supporters on the Capitol. Tiberius and
some three hundred others were killed and their bodies thrown into the Tiber.{{anc:Plut. Ti. Gracch. 19–20; App. B Civ. 1.16}}

:::

:::claim interpretation
The killing of Tiberius was the first time in centuries that political conflict in Rome ended in bloodshed within the city. Ancient
writers already treated it as a beginning: Appian dates the start of the civil wars to this moment, and Velleius Paterculus wrote that
from then on right was overwhelmed by might.
:::

## Between the brothers, 133–123 BC {#interval}

The land commission went on working; the senators had killed the man, not the law. Several boundary stones survive, inscribed with
the names of the commissioners, from Campania, Lucania and elsewhere in southern Italy. The census figures recorded by Livy's
epitomator rise from about 319,000 citizens in 131/130 BC to about 395,000 in 125/124. Many historians connect the rise with the land
grants, which may have raised poor citizens into the registered property classes, though the figures are open to other
explanations.{{anc:Livy, Per. 59–60}}

The commission's work raised a new problem. Much public land was held by Italian allies, and their leaders complained of being
dispossessed. In 129 Scipio Aemilianus took up their cause and had the commission's judicial powers transferred to a consul, stalling
its work; soon afterwards he was found dead in his bed, and rumours of murder circulated. In 125 the consul M. Fulvius Flaccus, a
Gracchan ally, proposed giving citizenship to allies who wanted it, perhaps as compensation. The proposal failed, and the Latin colony
of Fregellae revolted and was destroyed. The question of the allies' status was now on the table, and would not go away until the
Social War of 91–88 BC.

## Gaius Gracchus, 123–121 BC {#gaius}

Gaius, nine years younger than Tiberius and a more formidable orator, was tribune in 123 and again in 122. His programme was much
wider than his brother's. Not all details are certain, but the main measures were:

- a **grain law** ([[term:frumentatio]]): the state bought grain and sold it to citizens in Rome at a fixed, subsidised price, with
  public granaries to store it;
- renewal of the **land law** and the foundation of **colonies**, including one on the site of Carthage, called Junonia;
- a law on the **extortion court** that replaced senatorial jurors with men of equestrian census, so that senators accused of
  plundering provinces would be judged by others;
- the auctioning of the taxes of the new province of **Asia** at Rome, which benefited the equestrian companies of publicani;
- **roads** and public works across Italy;
- laws confirming that no citizen could be executed without trial by the people, aimed retrospectively at those who had killed and
  persecuted his brother's supporters;
- in 122, a proposal to give **citizenship** to the Latins and Latin rights to other allies.

:::check gr-2
:::

The citizenship proposal was his undoing. The tribune M. Livius Drusus, backed by the Senate, outbid him with offers of more colonies
for poor Romans, and Gaius' urban supporters were not eager to share their privileges with Latins. In the elections for 121 he failed
to win a third tribunate. When his opponents moved to repeal the Carthage colony law, his supporters and those of Fulvius Flaccus
gathered, and a servant of the consul L. Opimius was killed in a scuffle.

The Senate responded by passing a decree that the consuls "should see to it that the state suffers no harm", the so-called
*senatus consultum ultimum* ([[term:sc-ultimum]]). Opimius took this as authority for armed action. Fulvius' followers occupied the
Aventine; they were attacked by an armed force including Cretan archers; Fulvius was killed, and Gaius had a slave kill him in a sacred
grove across the Tiber. Some 3,000 of their supporters were later executed without trial. Opimius then restored the temple of Concord.

:::claim contested
Whether the "ultimate decree" gave magistrates legal authority to kill citizens without trial was disputed at the time and has been
ever since. Opimius was prosecuted in 120 and acquitted; Cicero, who invoked the decree against the Catilinarians in 63, was exiled for
it in 58. The decree was a political weapon whose legitimacy depended on who won.
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The Gracchan years changed Roman politics in several lasting ways.

- **Violence.** Political disputes could now be settled by killing. Each episode, 133, 121, 100 and 88, set precedents for the next.
- **Optimates and populares.** Later writers distinguish [[term:optimates]], who upheld the authority of the Senate, from
  [[term:populares]], who worked through the people and the tribunate. These were not parties with members and programmes but styles of
  politics, and individuals could change sides. But the Gracchi made the popularis style available and gave it martyrs.
- **The equestrian order.** By giving equites the extortion courts and the Asian tax contracts, Gaius created a politically conscious
  second order with interests opposed to the Senate's, and the composition of juries became a recurring struggle.
- **The grain dole.** Subsidised grain for the citizens of Rome became a permanent feature; it was made free in 58 BC and continued under
  the emperors.
- **The allies.** The land question had raised the citizenship question, which led to the Social War.

The land programme itself had limited long-term effect. A law of 111 BC turned remaining allotments into private property that could
be sold. The recruitment problem it had been meant to solve was addressed in a different way within a generation, when Marius enrolled
volunteers without property.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Our two main narratives are by Greeks writing two and a half centuries later. Plutarch's paired *Lives* of the Gracchi (c. AD 100)
present them as noble idealists undone by ambition and opposition, drawing on sources sympathetic to them, perhaps including writings
of Gaius himself. Appian's *Civil Wars* (second century AD) sets the Gracchi at the start of a century of civil strife and places the
land question and the allies at its centre. Both draw on lost Republican writers; we also have fragments of Gaius' speeches and
Cicero's many comments, which vary with his audience: in speeches to the people he could praise the Gracchi; in his treatises he
condemns them.

Modern historians have read the Gracchi as social reformers responding to a real agrarian crisis, as aristocrats pursuing family
rivalries and *gloria*, or as men who exposed the latent democratic powers of the Roman constitution. Harriet Flower argues that 133 BC
marks the end of the "republic of the nobles", in which the elite settled disputes by consensus.{{ref:flower-2010}} Others stress the
structural problems the Gracchi tried to solve, which their deaths left unresolved.{{ref:brunt-1988}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside What do we really know?
Ask of every detail in the narratives: is this attested in a near-contemporary source (an inscription, Cicero, a fragment of Gaius'
speeches), or only in Plutarch and Appian? The boundary stones confirm the commission's work; the census figures can be read in more
than one way; the stories of diadems and purple robes may be hostile rumour preserved by later writers.
:::
