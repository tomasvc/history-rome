## Historical context {#context}

When Caesar was killed, his great-nephew C. Octavius was eighteen and studying at Apollonia across the Adriatic. Caesar's will adopted
him as son and heir. Against his family's advice he accepted, taking the name C. Julius Caesar; modern historians call him Octavian to
avoid confusion, although he never used that name himself. With the name came Caesar's money, clients and, above all, the loyalty of
Caesar's veterans. Fifty-seven years later he died in his bed as the first Roman emperor, having governed longer than any Roman before
him.

## From heir to triumvir, 44–36 BC {#triumvirate}

Octavian had no office, no army and no experience. In 44–43 he raised troops from Caesar's veterans on his own initiative, which was
illegal, and offered them to the Senate against Antony, who was trying to take Cisalpine Gaul by force. Cicero, believing he could
use and then discard the young man, praised him in the *Philippics*. After both consuls of 43 died in the fighting around Mutina,
Octavian marched on Rome with his army and forced his election as consul at nineteen. He then joined Antony and Lepidus. The lex Titia
of November 43 created a legal "board of three for setting the state in order" (*triumviri rei publicae constituendae*) with powers
for five years.

The triumvirs began, as Sulla had, with proscriptions, to eliminate enemies and to raise money for their armies. Cicero was among the
victims; his head and hands were displayed on the Rostra. In 42 Antony and Octavian defeated Brutus and Cassius at Philippi. Antony took
the East; Octavian took on the task of settling some tens of thousands of veterans in Italy, which meant confiscating land from
eighteen Italian cities. The dispossessed and Antony's brother Lucius rose against him; Octavian besieged them at Perusia (41–40).
Virgil's first *Eclogue*, with its shepherd driven from his land, reflects the human cost.

Octavian then fought Sextus Pompeius, the son of Pompey, who held Sicily and blockaded Italy's grain. M. Vipsanius Agrippa, Octavian's
friend from youth and his indispensable commander, built a new fleet and won at Naulochus in 36. Lepidus, attempting to claim Sicily,
was deserted by his troops and stripped of power, though allowed to keep his post as pontifex maximus until his death.

## Actium and Egypt, 36–30 BC {#actium}

Antony, based in the East, failed in a major invasion of Parthia (36) and relied increasingly on Cleopatra's resources. In 34, at the
"Donations of Alexandria", he distributed eastern territories to Cleopatra and her children, including Caesarion, presented as
Caesar's son. Octavian's propaganda portrayed Antony as a Roman enslaved by an eastern queen. When Antony divorced Octavia in 32,
Octavian illegally seized and published Antony's will, which asked to be buried in Alexandria. Italy and the western provinces swore
an oath of personal loyalty to him, and war was declared on Cleopatra, not Antony, making it a foreign war.

At Actium, on the west coast of Greece, on 2 September 31, Agrippa's fleet defeated Antony and Cleopatra, who broke out and fled to
Egypt. In 30 Octavian took Alexandria; Antony and Cleopatra killed themselves, and Caesarion was put to death. Egypt became a
possession governed by an equestrian prefect appointed by Octavian, not by a senator.

## The settlement of 27 BC {#settlement-27}

In January 27, Octavian, as consul for the seventh time, laid down his extraordinary powers and declared that he returned the state to
the Senate and people. The Senate responded by asking him to keep a large "province" consisting of Spain, Gaul and Syria, the regions
where most of the legions were stationed, for ten years, to be governed through legates of his choosing. The other provinces were
governed, as before, by proconsuls chosen by lot. He was given the name Augustus, a word associated with augury and sanctity; a laurel
tree was planted at his door and a golden shield, recording his virtues, set up in the Senate house.

:::source res-gestae
:::

His own description of this moment, in the account of his achievements he composed for display at his mausoleum, is the most famous
statement of the Augustan settlement: after it he excelled all in [[term:auctoritas]], but had no more power than his colleagues in
each magistracy.{{anc:RG 34}}

## The settlement of 23 BC and after {#settlement-23}

:::diagram augustus-powers
:::

By 23 Augustus had held the consulship every year since 31. It was a problem: two consulships a year were the summit of aristocratic
ambition, and he was occupying one of them. In 23, after a serious illness and a conspiracy, he resigned the consulship. In its place
he received the [[term:tribunicia-potestas|tribunician power]] for life, giving him the right to convene the Senate and the people, to
propose legislation and to veto, and the sacrosanctity of a tribune, without holding the office. He numbered the years of his rule by
it. His imperium as proconsul was made greater than that of other governors ([[term:imperium-maius]]) and allowed to operate inside
Rome. In 19 he probably received some further consular prerogatives; the details are debated.

Further honours accumulated: pontifex maximus on Lepidus' death in 12 BC, and in 2 BC the title "father of his country" (*pater
patriae*). He never took a title of kingship. He was *princeps*, "first citizen", an informal word used under the Republic for leading
statesmen.

## The real foundations {#foundations}

Formal powers alone do not explain Augustus' position. Historians emphasise several other foundations.

**The army.** Augustus turned the civil war armies into a standing professional force of 28 legions (25 after AD 9), with fixed terms
of service (eventually twenty years plus reserve) and a cash discharge bonus paid from a military treasury ([[term:aerarium-militare]])
founded in AD 6 and funded by new taxes on inheritances and sales. Soldiers swore their oath to him; their commanders were his legates.
A Praetorian Guard of nine cohorts protected him in Italy. He paid for much of this personally, as the Res Gestae records.

**Wealth and patronage.** Augustus was by far the richest man in the empire. He paid soldiers, subsidised the treasury, distributed
grain and cash to the Roman plebs, built and restored temples, and supported impoverished senators. Like any great patron, he expected
loyalty in return.

**The aristocracy.** He needed senators to govern provinces and command armies. He kept the Republican offices and their prestige,
reviewed the Senate's membership, created new careers for equestrians (prefects of Egypt, the Guard, the grain supply, the fire
brigade), and advanced men whose loyalty he trusted, including "new men" from Italy. Ronald Syme saw the new regime as the triumph of
a faction, a "Roman revolution" in which an Italian elite replaced the old nobility.{{ref:syme-1939}}

**Consensus and image.** Karl Galinsky and others emphasise that Augustus offered something many Romans wanted after twenty years of
civil war: peace, order and the restoration of traditional religion and morality, presented through a new visual language.{{ref:galinsky-1996}}
The next lesson examines that culture.

## Succession {#succession}

Because the Principate was not an office, it could not be inherited, yet Augustus clearly intended a successor from his family. He
had no son. His daughter Julia was married first to his nephew Marcellus (d. 23 BC), then to Agrippa, with whom she had three sons and
two daughters. Augustus adopted the two eldest boys, Gaius and Lucius Caesar, and gave them public prominence. Agrippa died in 12 BC;
Julia was then married to Livia's son Tiberius. In 2 BC she was exiled for adultery, perhaps also for political reasons. Lucius died
in AD 2 and Gaius in AD 4.

Augustus then adopted Tiberius, a proven general, together with his last surviving grandson, Agrippa Postumus (later exiled), and
required Tiberius to adopt his nephew Germanicus. Tiberius received tribunician power and imperium, so that when Augustus died at Nola
in August AD 14 the essential powers were already in his hands. The Senate formally confirmed his position; the Principate passed
without civil war.

:::claim interpretation
Augustus' succession arrangements reveal the central paradox of his regime. It claimed to be a restored Republic, but power was
transmitted within one family through adoption, marriage and the conferral of powers in advance. That combination of dynastic
expectation without a rule of succession would trouble the empire for centuries.
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The Augustan system lasted, in its essentials, for some two centuries. Its strengths were its flexibility and its ability to absorb the
aristocracy into the service of a monarch. Its weaknesses were the dependence of the emperor on the army, which would make and unmake
emperors in AD 69 and the third century, and the lack of clear rules of succession. The defeat of Varus in Germany in AD 9, in which
three legions were destroyed, also marked the practical limits of expansion; Augustus' advice to his successors, Tacitus reports, was
to keep the empire within its existing boundaries.{{anc:Tac. Ann. 1.11}}

## Historiography {#historiography}

The two most influential ancient analyses are Augustus' own Res Gestae and Tacitus' opening chapters of the *Annals*, written a
century later. Tacitus' dark view, that Augustus seduced everyone with gifts and peace and that liberty died unnoticed, shaped Syme's
*The Roman Revolution* (1939), written as fascist dictatorships rose in Europe. Later scholars have focused on Augustan culture and
consensus (Zanker, Galinsky), the workings of imperial government (Millar), and the ways in which Augustus' position was gradually
defined through negotiation with the Senate rather than by a single plan (Wallace-Hadrill).{{ref:wallace-hadrill-1982}}
