## Historical context {#context}

Nero's death in June 68 ended the dynasty of Augustus. There was no obvious successor, and no institution capable of choosing one.
The Senate could confer powers, but power lay with the armies. Over the next eighteen months four men were recognised as emperor in
Rome, and three of them died violently. Tacitus, opening his *Histories* with this year, identified what it had exposed.

:::source tacitus-histories-1-4
:::

## The year of the four emperors {#year-69}

**Galba** was seventy-one, from an ancient patrician family, and stern. He refused to pay the donative promised to the Guard in his
name, executed opponents and adopted as heir a young aristocrat, Piso Licinianus, rather than his ambitious supporter M. Salvius
**Otho**, former husband of Nero's wife Poppaea. On 15 January 69 Otho had the Guard murder Galba and Piso in the Forum.

Two weeks earlier, the legions of Upper and Lower Germany had refused to renew their oath to Galba and proclaimed their governor, A.
**Vitellius**. Their armies marched on Italy and defeated Otho's forces near Bedriacum, between Cremona and Mantua, in April. Otho,
though he still had troops, killed himself to end the bloodshed, a death that even hostile writers admired. Vitellius entered Rome,
replaced the Praetorian Guard with his own legionaries, and indulged, according to the hostile tradition, in banquets.

:::people
galba otho vitellius vespasian
:::

In July the prefect of Egypt, Tiberius Julius Alexander, had his troops swear allegiance to T. Flavius **Vespasianus**, the general
commanding the war in Judaea; the legions of Judaea and Syria (under its governor Mucianus) followed. Vespasian came from an
equestrian family of Reate in the Sabine country; his father had been a tax collector. He had a solid military record, two adult sons
and no grand ancestry. The legions of the Danube declared for him and, under Antonius Primus, invaded Italy before Vespasian or
Mucianus could arrive, defeating the Vitellians in a second battle near Bedriacum and sacking Cremona. In December, fighting in Rome
burned the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol, the symbolic centre of the state; Vitellius was dragged through the streets and killed.

## The legal basis of power {#lex}

The Senate immediately voted Vespasian all the powers his predecessors had held. Part of the law that ratified this vote survives on a
bronze tablet rediscovered in Rome in the fourteenth century; Cola di Rienzo used it in 1347 to argue for the authority of the Roman
people over their rulers.

:::source lex-de-imperio
:::

:::check yf-2
:::

Vespasian dated his reign not from the Senate's vote but from his proclamation by the troops in July, a revealing choice.

## The Jewish War {#jewish-war}

The revolt in Judaea had begun in 66, triggered by conflict between Jews and Greeks in Caesarea, the governor's seizure of money from the
Temple treasury, and tensions over Roman rule and taxation. Vespasian, appointed by Nero, had reduced most of the province by 68. In 70
his son Titus besieged Jerusalem, which was divided among rival Jewish factions; after a long siege the city was taken and the Temple
destroyed by fire. The last rebel stronghold, Masada, fell in 73 or 74.

Our main source, Flavius Josephus, was a Jewish aristocrat and rebel commander who surrendered to Vespasian, predicted his rise to
power, and became a client of the Flavians in Rome. His *Jewish War* is invaluable and deeply compromised: he wrote under Flavian
patronage, blamed the war on extremists among his own people, and insisted that Titus had tried to save the Temple.

:::claim contested
Josephus says that at Masada 960 defenders killed themselves rather than surrender. Excavations in the 1960s revealed the fortress,
siege works and remains, but whether the mass suicide happened as he describes is debated; the story's role in modern Israeli
national identity has made the question unusually charged.
:::

The Flavians made the war the centrepiece of their legitimacy. Vespasian and Titus celebrated a joint triumph in 71, displaying the
spoils of the Temple; coins proclaimed *Iudaea capta*, "Judaea captured"; the Temple of Peace was built to house the spoils; and a tax
formerly paid by Jews to the Temple was diverted to Jupiter Capitolinus (the *fiscus Iudaicus*).

:::

## Vespasian and Titus, AD 69–81 {#vespasian}

Vespasian faced an empire damaged by civil war and a treasury emptied by Nero and the armies. Suetonius reports his saying that 40,000
million sesterces were needed to restore the state.{{anc:Suet. Vesp. 16}} He raised taxes and was famously careful with money: when
Titus objected to a tax on urine collected from public latrines (used by fullers), Vespasian held a coin to his nose and said it did not
smell.{{anc:Suet. Vesp. 23}}

He restored order in the provinces, ending the revolt of the Batavian Julius Civilis on the Rhine, granted Latin rights to the whole of
Spain, and recruited new senators from Italy and the western provinces. He rebuilt the Capitol and began the great amphitheatre on the
site of Nero's private lake, giving back to the public what Nero had taken: the poet Martial praised this restitution. He was
remembered as a good emperor, unpretentious and humorous; on his deathbed he joked, "Oh dear, I think I am becoming a god".{{anc:Suet.
Vesp. 23}}

Titus succeeded in 79. His short reign saw the eruption of Vesuvius, which buried Pompeii, Herculaneum and other towns (August or
perhaps October 79), a fire in Rome, and the inauguration of the amphitheatre with a hundred days of games in 80. He died in 81,
remembered as "the darling of the human race".

## Domitian, AD 81–96 {#domitian}

Titus' younger brother Domitian ruled for fifteen years. He was a conscientious administrator who supervised provincial governors
closely, maintained the currency at a high standard, rebuilt much of Rome after the fire of 80 (including a vast palace on the
Palatine), and raised soldiers' pay by a third. He fought the Chatti in Germany and the Dacians on the Danube with mixed success.

He also made the autocracy explicit. He held the consulship repeatedly, made himself censor for life, and was said to have liked
being addressed as "master and god" (*dominus et deus*). After a revolt by the governor of Upper Germany, Saturninus, in 89, and
especially from 93, he executed senators and exiled philosophers. In September 96 he was murdered in a palace plot involving his
chamberlain and, probably, his wife. The Senate condemned his memory: his name was erased from inscriptions and his statues destroyed.
The elderly senator Nerva was proclaimed emperor.

:::claim contested
Domitian's reputation was formed after his death by senators who had served him and needed to distance themselves: Tacitus and Pliny
the Younger both prospered under him. Documentary evidence (inscriptions, papyri and his administrative record) suggests effective
government in the provinces. Historians still disagree about how far his "tyranny" was real repression of a dangerous aristocracy and
how far it was a retrospective construction.
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The civil war of 69 showed that emperors could be made by the armies and away from Rome, and that a man of modest origins could found
a dynasty. The Flavians demonstrated that the principate could survive the end of Augustus' family because it had become an
institution: its powers could be conferred as a package by law, and legitimacy could be built from military victory, public works and
the support of the army. But the Flavian dynasty ended as the Julio-Claudian had, with an emperor whom the Senate hated. The next
dynasty would try a different method of succession.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Tacitus' *Histories*, which covered 69–96 in perhaps fourteen books, survive only for 69–70. His account of the civil war, written
under Trajan, is a masterpiece of analysis of collective behaviour: armies, crowds and senators. Suetonius covers each emperor
biographically, and Josephus gives the Flavian view of the Jewish War. Modern work has emphasised the significance of 69 for the
nature of the principate (Wellesley), the solid achievement of Vespasian (Levick) and the role of damnatio memoriae in constructing
reputations (Flower).{{ref:flower-2006}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Pompeii and Herculaneum
The eruption of 79 preserved two towns as no other ancient places are preserved, with houses, shops, graffiti, election notices and
human remains. The letters of Pliny the Younger to Tacitus describe the eruption and the death of his uncle Pliny the Elder, who
commanded the fleet at Misenum. Recent research on charred fruit and an inscription has led some scholars to argue that the eruption
took place in October rather than on 24 August, the date in the manuscripts of Pliny.
:::
