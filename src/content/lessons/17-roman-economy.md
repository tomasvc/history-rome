## Historical context {#context}

The Roman empire united the Mediterranean and its hinterlands in a single political system for about four centuries. It had one
currency, a common legal framework for property and contracts among citizens, paved roads, harbours and a long period of internal peace.
Did these conditions produce a more integrated, productive and prosperous economy? And how can historians measure an economy for
which almost no statistics survive?

## The structure of the economy {#structure}

The Roman economy was overwhelmingly agrarian. Most people, perhaps 80 per cent or more, lived on the land and produced food. The
"Mediterranean triad" of grain, olives and wine dominated production, with livestock, fish and timber. Most farms were small and largely
produced for household consumption, but large estates, worked by slaves, tenants (*coloni*) and hired labour, produced for markets.
Wealth was measured in land, and elite fortunes were built on agricultural rents.

Cities were centres of consumption, administration and craft production. Rome itself, with perhaps a million inhabitants in the early
empire, required imports of some 200,000 tonnes or more of grain a year, mainly from Egypt, Sicily and North Africa, and large quantities
of oil and wine. The state organised part of this through the *annona*, the grain supply, which provided free grain to around 200,000
registered citizens and supervised the wider market.

Crafts and services ranged from potters and bakers to builders and shippers. Many businesses were run by slaves and freedmen, acting
for themselves or on behalf of elite owners who preferred not to be seen in trade.

## Trade and transport {#trade}

Transport by sea was far cheaper than by land. The Roman price data in Diocletian's Price Edict (301) suggest that shipping grain across
the whole Mediterranean cost less than carting it some 75–100 miles overland, which is why coastal and river cities could be supplied from
distant regions.{{ref:scheidel-morris-saller-2007}}

Archaeology provides proxies for the scale of trade:

- **Shipwrecks** dated to the period between about 100 BC and AD 200 are far more numerous than those of earlier or later centuries.
- **Amphorae**, the clay containers used to ship wine, oil and fish sauce, can be traced to their production regions by shape and clay,
  revealing patterns of trade across the empire.
- **Fine pottery** such as terra sigillata, mass-produced in Italy and then Gaul, was distributed in huge quantities across the West.

:::source monte-testaccio
:::

Long-distance trade extended beyond the empire. Ships sailed from the Red Sea ports of Myos Hormos and Berenike to India using the monsoon
winds; a papyrus records the cargo of a ship from Muziris on the Malabar coast, worth an enormous sum. Pliny the Elder complained that
India, China and Arabia drained the empire of 100 million sesterces a year.{{anc:Plin. HN 12.84}}

:::claim interpretation
Shipwreck counts are a powerful but imperfect proxy: they are affected by where divers have searched, how wrecks are dated, changes in
ship size and the use of containers that survive (amphorae) versus those that do not (barrels and sacks, which became more common later).
The peak in the early empire is real, but its precise height is uncertain.
:::

## Money {#money}

The Roman monetary system rested on the gold aureus, the silver denarius (25 denarii to an aureus) and bronze and brass coins, including
the sestertius (four to a denarius), used as the unit of account. Coins were minted mainly to pay the army and the state's expenses, and
spread through taxation, trade and payment. The degree of monetisation varied: highly monetised in Italy and the cities, less so in
remote rural areas, where much exchange was in kind.

## Credit in a Roman port: the Sulpicii archive {#sulpicii}

In 1959 road builders at Murecine, just outside Pompeii, uncovered a cache of wax-covered writing tablets. They are the business records of the Sulpicii, a family of freedmen who lent money and arranged deals at Puteoli, the great port on the Bay of Naples where grain ships from Alexandria docked. Dated between the 20s and 60s AD, they record loans, guarantees, sureties, auctions of pledged goods, and agreements to appear in court at Puteoli or Rome.

The most famous dossier concerns a grain dealer, Gaius Novius Eunus, who in AD 37 borrowed money and pledged as security thousands of measures of Alexandrian wheat and sacks of pulses stored in a warehouse at Puteoli. The goods stayed where they were; the creditor took over the right to them. Some of the acknowledgements are in Eunus' own hand, in vigorous and unconventional Latin spelling.

:::claim established
The Murecine tablets show sophisticated commercial practice in the early empire: written contracts, credit secured on goods in storage, the use of slaves and freedmen as agents, and reliance on courts. They support historians who argue that Roman institutions lowered the costs of trade, though they come from a single family business in a single, exceptionally commercial port.
:::

## The state and the economy {#state}

The Roman state had no economic policy in the modern sense, but it shaped the economy profoundly. Its taxes forced provincials to sell
produce; its spending on the army and on the city of Rome created large concentrations of demand; its roads, harbours and legal system
lowered transaction costs; and peace reduced risk.

## The great debate {#debate}

How should the Roman economy be characterised? For much of the twentieth century the debate was framed as "primitivist" versus
"modernist". Michael Rostovtzeff (1926) described an urban bourgeoisie and capitalist enterprise. Moses Finley's *The Ancient Economy*
(1973) argued that ancient economic behaviour was embedded in social relations: status, not profit, guided elites, who invested in land
and display; cities consumed rather than produced; there was no integrated market and no economic thinking.{{ref:finley-1973}}

:::check re-1
:::

Since the 1990s the debate has been transformed by new methods. The "new institutional economics" asks how institutions (property rights,
law, money, the state) reduced the costs of exchange; the *Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World* (2007) used it as a
framework.{{ref:scheidel-morris-saller-2007}} Peter Temin argued that Roman markets for grain, labour and credit functioned as integrated
markets.{{ref:temin-2013}} The Oxford Roman Economy Project gathered quantitative data on agriculture, urbanism, trade and mining.{{ref:bowman-wilson-2009}}

:::debate roman-economy
:::

Most historians now accept that the early empire saw real growth in aggregate output, driven by population growth, urbanisation,
specialisation and trade. Whether output *per person* grew, and by how much, is more contested; estimates suggest modest growth at best.

:::claim contested
Did ordinary people benefit? Skeletal evidence on stature and health, which some historians read as an indicator of nutrition, does not
show clear improvement in the Roman period and may show the opposite in urban populations, where disease was rife. Gains from growth were
probably concentrated among landowners, merchants and the state.
:::

## How rich were Romans? {#living-standards}

Aggregate growth does not tell us how ordinary people lived. Historians approach the question through wages and prices. [[lesson:diocletian#price-edict|The Price Edict of 301]] sets a farm labourer's maximum daily wage at 25 denarii with food, and the price of a measure of wheat at 100 denarii; papyri from Egypt record wages, rents and prices across three centuries. Walter Scheidel has used such data to argue that the real wages of unskilled workers in the Roman empire were generally close to subsistence, comparable to other pre-modern societies and well below those of the most advanced early modern economies such as the Netherlands and England.{{ref:scheidel-2010}}

A related approach estimates how income was distributed. Scheidel and Steven Friesen suggested that a tiny elite of perhaps 1.5 per cent of households received about a fifth of total income, that a "middling" group of perhaps 6 to 12 per cent lived comfortably above subsistence, and that the great majority lived at or near it.{{ref:scheidel-friesen-2009}}

:::claim contested
These figures are models built on many assumptions, not measurements, and their authors present them as such. Their value is to make arguments explicit and testable: for example, they suggest that a substantial middling group of traders, craftsmen and better-off farmers existed between the elite and the poor, a group largely invisible in elite literature but visible in tombstones such as [[src:eurysaces-tomb|that of the baker Eurysaces]].
:::

## Consequences {#consequences}

The early imperial economy was the most complex and productive in the western world before early modern times. Its integration depended
on the empire: when the political unity of the Mediterranean broke down in the fifth to seventh centuries, long-distance trade, fine
pottery production, coinage and urban populations contracted sharply in the West. The scale of that contraction is itself evidence for the
scale of what had existed before.

## Historiography and evidence {#historiography}

The Roman economy is an unusually interdisciplinary field: historians work with economists, archaeologists, numismatists and climate
scientists. Ice cores, tree rings, pollen, animal bones and isotopes now supplement texts and pottery. The danger is that proxies are
used beyond what they can bear; the benefit is that the Roman economy can now be studied with data unimaginable to Finley.

Imagine trying to write the economic history of your own country if all that survived were a few laws, a few famous speeches, the
