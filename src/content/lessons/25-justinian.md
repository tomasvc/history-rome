## Historical context {#context}

In the early sixth century the eastern Roman empire was stable and prosperous. The emperor Anastasius (491–518) had reformed the currency,
improved tax collection and left a large surplus. His successor Justin I (518–527), a peasant from the Balkans who had risen to command the
palace guard, relied on his nephew Justinian, who succeeded him in 527. Justinian, Latin-speaking, from the Balkans, a tireless worker, ruled
for thirty-eight years with ambitions that encompassed law, religion, building and the recovery of the lost western provinces.

## The law {#law}

Within weeks of becoming sole emperor, Justinian appointed a commission to collect the valid imperial constitutions into a new Code (529,
revised 534). In 530 he ordered his quaestor Tribonian to do something far more ambitious: to excerpt the vast literature of the classical
jurists, some three million lines, into a single authoritative work. The *Digest* (or *Pandects*), in fifty books, was completed in three
years and issued in December 533, together with the *Institutes*, a textbook for students. Justinian's later legislation, the *Novels*,
was largely in Greek, the language of most of his subjects.

:::source justinian-institutes
:::

:::check ju-1
:::

:::claim established
Justinian's codification preserved most of what survives of classical Roman jurisprudence. A single complete manuscript of the Digest,
written probably in the sixth century and now in Florence, underlies the text that was studied at Bologna from the late eleventh century,
and through it Roman law shaped the legal systems of continental Europe, Latin America and beyond.
:::

## The Nika riot and Hagia Sophia {#nika}

Constantinople's population was divided into supporters of the chariot-racing factions, the Blues and the Greens, organisations that also
played roles in public ceremonial and could mobilise crowds. In January 532, after the government refused to pardon two rioters, Blues and
Greens united against the emperor's ministers with the cry "Nika!" ("Conquer!"), burned much of the city centre, including the church of Hagia
Sophia, and proclaimed a rival emperor, Hypatius, a nephew of Anastasius. Justinian considered flight.

:::source procopius-nika
:::

:::check ju-2
:::

Belisarius and Mundus led troops into the Hippodrome, where the crowd had gathered, and massacred them; Procopius gives a figure of over
30,000 dead. Hypatius was executed. Within weeks Justinian began rebuilding Hagia Sophia on a scale never before attempted.

:::source procopius-buildings
:::

The new church, designed by Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, two mathematicians, was dedicated in December 537. Its dome, some 31
metres in diameter, seemed to float on a ring of windows above a vast open interior. It partially collapsed after earthquakes in 558 and was
rebuilt higher. For almost a thousand years it was the largest church in Christendom and the setting of imperial ceremony.

## The wars {#wars}

Justinian inherited a war with Persia, and in 532 bought an "Endless Peace" with a large payment in gold so that he could turn west. In 533 an
expedition of perhaps 15,000 men under Belisarius destroyed the Vandal kingdom in Africa in a few months. In 535 he invaded Italy, ruled by
the Ostrogoths since Theoderic. Belisarius took Sicily, Naples and Rome, endured a year-long Gothic siege of Rome (537–538), and entered
Ravenna in 540.

The war in Italy then dragged on for fourteen more years. Persia broke the peace in 540; Khosrow I sacked Antioch and deported its
population. The Gothic king Totila recovered most of Italy in the 540s. Only in 552 did a large army under the eunuch general Narses defeat
him. The final Gothic resistance ended in 554, and in the same year Justinian issued a "Pragmatic Sanction" restoring the Italian order. A
foothold was also taken in southern Spain (552).

:::claim interpretation
The Gothic War devastated Italy more than the fifth-century invasions had: Rome was besieged several times and its population dramatically
reduced. Many Italians were unenthusiastic about their "liberation", and the empire's control proved fragile: from 568 the Lombards
conquered much of the peninsula, leaving imperial Italy as a set of enclaves around Ravenna, Rome, Naples and the south.
:::

## Plague and climate {#plague}

In 541 a pandemic appeared at Pelusium in Egypt and reached Constantinople in 542, where Procopius describes it at its height killing
thousands a day.{{anc:Procop. Wars 2.22–23}} It recurred in waves across the Mediterranean and Europe for two centuries. Ancient DNA from
sixth-century burials has confirmed that the agent was *Yersinia pestis*, the bacterium of bubonic plague.

:::check ju-3
:::

The plague followed a period of climatic disruption. Tree rings and ice cores show volcanic eruptions in 536 and 539/540 that caused severe
cooling across the northern hemisphere, the beginning of what some scientists have called the Late Antique Little Ice Age.{{ref:buntgen-2016}}
Contemporaries noted the sun shining dimly for a year.

:::claim contested
The scale of the plague's mortality and its long-term effects are debated. Some historians argue that it killed a large share of the
population and shattered the empire's fiscal and military capacity, preparing the crises of the seventh century. Others, notably Lee
Mordechai and Merle Eisenberg, argue that the evidence for catastrophic impact is thin and that "maximalist" accounts have relied on
assumptions; they point to continuities in administration, building and coinage.
:::

:::

## Living through the plague {#plague-life}

Contemporaries described the pandemic of 541–542 in detail. Procopius says that at its height in Constantinople the officials could not keep up with burials: when the graves were full, they took the roofs off the towers of the fortifications across the Golden Horn at Sykai, filled them with corpses and roofed them over again.{{anc:Procop. Wars 2.23}} The emperor himself fell ill and recovered. John of Ephesus, a church leader who travelled through the affected regions, recorded that people in the capital went out wearing tags with their names, so that they could be identified if they died in the street.

The government responded with law. In 544 Justinian issued a law complaining that, after the "chastisement" sent by God, traders, craftsmen and farm workers were demanding double or triple their former prices and wages, and ordering them to return to customary rates on pain of fines.

:::claim interpretation
The wage law resembles the English Statute of Labourers passed after the Black Death eight centuries later, and points to a similar effect: labour had become scarce, and workers could demand more. Compare the evidence for the [[lesson:marcus-aurelius#plague|Antonine plague]] four centuries earlier. For historians who see the plague as catastrophic, this is evidence of heavy mortality; for sceptics, a single law shows a short-term disruption in the capital rather than a permanent change.
:::

## Religion {#religion}

Justinian saw himself as responsible for the orthodoxy of his subjects. He persecuted pagans, heretics and Samaritans; in 529 he forbade
pagans to teach, which is often connected with the end of the Platonic school at Athens. His greatest religious problem was the division
over the Council of Chalcedon. Much of Egypt and Syria rejected Chalcedon's definition of two natures in Christ. Theodora protected
anti-Chalcedonian leaders, while Justinian sought formulas to reconcile them, condemning writings offensive to them at the Fifth Council
(553). The attempt failed, and alienated the Pope and western bishops as well.

## Silk and the wider world {#silk}

Silk was a luxury of enormous political value at Justinian's court, used for imperial robes and diplomatic gifts, and it came from China through Persian intermediaries who controlled the trade and its prices. Procopius tells how, around 552, some monks who had lived in a land beyond India brought silkworm eggs to Constantinople at Justinian's request, so that silk could be produced within the empire.{{anc:Procop. Wars 8.17}} Silk production became an imperial monopoly and a mainstay of Byzantine prestige for centuries.

The episode belongs to a wider sixth-century picture. Merchants sailed from Egypt and the Red Sea to India and Sri Lanka; a retired merchant known as Cosmas Indicopleustes described these voyages in his *Christian Topography*. In the 560s Justin II sent an embassy to the Turkic khagan in Central Asia, seeking both silk and an ally against Persia. The eastern empire was part of a connected Eurasian world, which also explains how a pandemic could travel so quickly to its shores.

## Procopius and the emperor's image {#procopius}

Procopius of Caesarea, Belisarius' secretary, wrote three works that contradict each other: the *Wars*, a classicising history of the
campaigns; the *Buildings*, a panegyric of Justinian's construction; and the *Secret History*, an invective, unpublished in his lifetime,
that portrays Justinian as a demon and Theodora as a former actress and prostitute of insatiable lust. Historians debate how to reconcile
them. Averil Cameron read Procopius as a conventional man of his age;{{ref:cameron-1985}} Anthony Kaldellis as a subtle classicist whose
hostility to autocracy runs through all three works.{{ref:kaldellis-2004}}

## Consequences {#consequences}

Justinian left an empire larger than he had inherited, a legal legacy of global importance, and the most famous building of the Middle Ages.
He also left an exhausted treasury, a devastated Italy, an unresolved religious division and frontiers threatened on every side. Within
forty years of his death the Lombards held most of Italy, Slavs and Avars were crossing the Danube, and Phocas' murder of the emperor
Maurice in 602 opened a war with Persia that would bring the empire to the brink of destruction.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Justinian has been seen as the last Roman emperor and as the first Byzantine one; as a restorer and as a destroyer. Some historians locate
his significance in his ambition to restore a Roman universal empire, others in his creation of a Christian, Greek-speaking imperial
autocracy. The new environmental history has added plague and climate to the story, provoking one of the liveliest current debates in the
field.{{ref:sarris-2023}}
