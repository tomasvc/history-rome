## Historical context {#context}

In 600 the Roman empire still ruled from Italy to the Euphrates, and its main rival was the Sasanian Persian empire. The two great powers had
fought intermittently for centuries, but neither had sought to destroy the other. Within forty years, both had been transformed beyond
recognition: Persia had been conquered entirely by the Arabs, and the Roman empire had lost Syria, Palestine, Egypt and soon North Africa,
its richest provinces. The empire that survived, centred on Constantinople, Anatolia and the Aegean, was the medieval state historians call
Byzantium.

## The last great war of antiquity, 602–628 {#persian-war}

In 602 the army on the Danube mutinied against the emperor Maurice and proclaimed a centurion, Phocas, who had Maurice and his sons killed.
Khosrow II, whom Maurice had helped to regain his throne, declared war to avenge his benefactor. The Persian armies broke through the eastern
frontier, and after Heraclius, son of the exarch of Africa, overthrew Phocas in 610, matters grew worse. The Persians took Antioch (613),
Jerusalem (614), where they carried off the relic of the True Cross, and Egypt (619), the empire's granary. Avars and Slavs overran the
Balkans.

:::people
heraclius khosrow-ii
:::

Heraclius rebuilt an army, financed partly by melting down church treasures, and from 622 campaigned in Armenia and the Caucasus, allying with
the Turkic Khazars. In 626, while he was away, the Avars and a Persian army besieged Constantinople from both sides of the Bosporus; the
city's walls and fleet held, and its defenders attributed the victory to the Virgin Mary. In December 627 Heraclius defeated a Persian army
near the ruins of Nineveh and advanced on Ctesiphon. Khosrow was overthrown and killed by his own son, who made peace. In 630 Heraclius
restored the True Cross to Jerusalem in triumph.

## The Arab conquests {#conquests}

At the same time, in western Arabia, the Prophet Muhammad had united the tribes of the peninsula under the new religion of Islam. After his
death in 632 his successors, the caliphs, directed Arab armies against both empires. Roman armies were defeated in Palestine and, in 636,
decisively on the Yarmuk river. Damascus, Jerusalem and Antioch surrendered; Egypt fell in 640–642. The Persian empire collapsed after the
battles of al-Qadisiyya and Nihavand; its last king died in 651.

:::

:::claim contested
The early Islamic narrative sources were written down from the eighth and ninth centuries onwards and preserve later traditions shaped by
religious and political concerns; contemporary Christian sources are fragmentary. Historians debate the chronology of the conquests, the
size of armies, the role of religion as a motive, and how far local Christian communities, divided over Chalcedon or alienated by taxation,
welcomed or resisted the conquerors. Robert Hoyland's synthesis emphasises the conquerors' use of existing structures and the gradual
creation of a new empire.
:::

Heraclius, who had saved the empire from Persia, lived to see the loss of the provinces he had recovered; he died in 641. His attempts to
reconcile Chalcedonian and anti-Chalcedonian Christians by a compromise formula (Monotheletism, that Christ had one will) divided the
Church further and were condemned in 680–681.

## Under new rulers: continuity after the conquests {#continuity}

For the people of Syria and Egypt, the Arab conquest did not at first change daily administration very much. The new rulers were a small military elite who settled in garrison cities such as Fustat in Egypt and Kufa and Basra in Iraq, and they relied on the existing tax system and the Greek-speaking officials who ran it.

Papyri from Egypt make this visible. One of the earliest dated documents of Islamic rule, a receipt written at Herakleopolis on 25 April 643, records the delivery of sixty-five sheep to Abdallah son of Jabir and his men, to feed the crews of the ships, the cavalry and the heavy infantry. It is written in both Greek and Arabic, begins with a Christian sign of the cross and a Greek invocation, and is dated both by the Egyptian tax year and by year 22 of the new Islamic calendar. Greek remained an administrative language in Egypt into the early eighth century.

Coinage tells the same story. For decades the new rulers struck copper coins imitating Byzantine designs, sometimes with the emperor's image, a cross modified, and Arabic words added. Only in 696–697 did the caliph Abd al-Malik introduce a new gold coinage bearing only Arabic religious inscriptions, a deliberate break with Roman models. His Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (completed 691/692) was decorated with mosaics in the late Roman tradition, and its inscriptions address Christians directly about the nature of Jesus.

:::claim interpretation
The early caliphate was in many ways a successor state of Rome and Persia, built on their administrative and artistic traditions, which it transformed gradually. This continuity is one reason modern historians increasingly treat the seventh century as a transition within a shared late antique world rather than an abrupt break.{{ref:hoyland-2015}}
:::

## Survival {#survival}

The Arab armies built fleets, raided Cyprus, Rhodes and Asia Minor, and threatened Constantinople. In the 670s Arab forces attacked the city;
Theophanes describes a seven-year siege repulsed by a new weapon.

:::

In 717–718 an enormous Umayyad army and fleet under Maslama besieged Constantinople by land and sea. The new emperor Leo III, Greek fire, the
Theodosian walls, a harsh winter and Bulgar attacks on the besiegers combined to defeat it. It was the last time an Arab army would threaten
the city.

Why did the empire survive when Persia did not? Historians point to the walls and position of Constantinople; the barrier of the Taurus
mountains, which made Anatolia defensible; the fleet; and the reorganisation of the state.

## Emperor, pope and theology: the trial of Martin I {#martin}

The religious politics of the seventh century show how far the emperor's authority still reached in the West. Heraclius' Monothelete compromise, and the decree of his grandson Constans II in 648 forbidding further discussion, were rejected by the bishops of Rome and by the monk Maximus the Confessor. In 649 Pope Martin I held a council at the Lateran in Rome that condemned Monotheletism.

Constans responded with force. In 653 the exarch of Ravenna arrested Martin in Rome and sent him to Constantinople, where he was tried for treason, publicly humiliated, and exiled to Cherson in the Crimea, where he died in 655. Maximus, tried in his turn, had his tongue and right hand cut off in 662 so that he could neither speak nor write against the emperor's doctrine, and died in exile. Constans himself moved his court to Sicily in 663, visited Rome, where he removed bronze from its monuments, and was murdered in his bath at Syracuse in 668.

:::claim interpretation
These events show that in the mid-seventh century the empire still regarded Rome and its bishop as subjects. They also show the price of using imperial power to settle doctrine: both Martin and Maximus became saints, and the Sixth Ecumenical Council of 680–681 condemned the very position Constans had enforced.
:::

## Transformation {#transformation}

:::check hi-1
:::

The empire of 700 was very different from that of 600. It had lost perhaps two thirds of its territory and an even larger share of its
revenues. Its cities, except Constantinople, shrank into fortified hilltop centres (*kastra*). Coin use declined outside the capital. Greek
had replaced Latin entirely in government and the army. The surviving field armies withdrew into Anatolia and were settled in large districts
that came to be called **themes** (*themata*), commanded by generals (*strategoi*) who also took over civil authority.

:::check hi-4
:::

The empire's religious culture also changed: the cult of icons and relics grew, the Virgin became the protector of Constantinople, and
emperors presented themselves as defenders of a Christian people, the Romans (*Rhomaioi*), against unbelievers.

:::claim interpretation
John Haldon described the seventh century as the period in which "Byzantine" culture took shape out of the late Roman world. Recent work by
Anthony Kaldellis and others stresses that its inhabitants continued to call themselves Romans and their state the Roman polity, and that
the transformation was one of a Roman state adapting to survive, not a new civilisation replacing an old one.
:::

## Historiography and evidence {#historiography}

The seventh century is one of the most poorly documented periods of Roman history. Narrative histories are few and late: the chronicle of
Theophanes (early ninth century), the short history of the Patriarch Nikephoros, and Syriac, Armenian and Coptic chronicles. Papyri from
Egypt, seals of officials, coins and archaeology (including the decline of cities and changes in pottery) are crucial. The contrast between
the brilliance of Justinian's age and the darkness of the seventh century is partly an effect of the sources themselves.{{ref:haldon-1997}}

:::aside Seals as evidence
Thousands of lead seals used by Byzantine officials to close documents survive, bearing names and titles. For the seventh and eighth
centuries, when narrative sources are thin, they are among the best evidence for how the administration was organised, including the
emergence of the themes.
:::
