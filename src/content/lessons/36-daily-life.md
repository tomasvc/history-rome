## Introduction {#context}

Most of what ancient writers tell us about daily life concerns the elite: their villas, dinners and leisure. Archaeology, inscriptions and
documents allow historians to reconstruct the lives of the majority. This lesson visits the Roman city, especially Rome, Pompeii, Herculaneum and
Ostia, and the frontier at Vindolanda.

## Houses {#housing}

:::diagram domus
:::

A wealthy family lived in a *domus*: a house built around an atrium, a hall open to the sky with a basin to catch rainwater, often with a garden
surrounded by columns (peristyle) beyond. The house was a public space as well as a private one: the master received clients in the morning,
and wall paintings, mosaics and the arrangement of rooms displayed status. Andrew Wallace-Hadrill showed how Pompeian houses were designed to
manage visitors' movement and to signal social distinctions.{{ref:wallace-hadrill-1994}} Households included slaves, freedpeople and often
lodgers; many houses had shops facing the street.

Most inhabitants of Rome lived in apartment blocks (*insulae*) of several storeys, with shops (*tabernae*) at ground level and progressively
smaller, cheaper rooms higher up. Fires and collapses were common; Augustus limited building height to 70 feet. Ostia preserves many such
buildings of the second century AD.

## Food {#food}

The basic diet was grain, eaten as bread or porridge, supplemented by olive oil, wine, vegetables, legumes, cheese and, for those who could
afford it, meat and fish. Fish sauce (*garum*) was a universal seasoning. Many urban poor lacked kitchens and bought hot food at street counters;
Pompeii has over 150 such bars. Citizens registered in Rome received free grain from the state. The elite dined reclining in the triclinium in
elaborate dinner parties, which moralists criticised for luxury.

:::source eurysaces-tomb
A baker's monument displays the bread-making business that fed the city.
:::

## Water and baths {#baths}

Aqueducts brought large quantities of water to Rome and other cities, feeding public fountains, baths and the houses of the wealthy. Bathing was a
daily social ritual for all classes: public baths, for a small fee or free, offered hot, warm and cold rooms, exercise yards and sometimes
libraries and gardens. The baths of Caracalla and Diocletian in Rome could serve thousands at a time. Garrett Fagan showed how baths functioned
as social centres where hierarchies were both displayed and relaxed.{{ref:fagan-1999}}

## Time and work {#time}

Romans divided daylight into twelve hours, longer in summer and shorter in winter; the elite ideal was business in the morning and leisure
after the main meal. Most people worked: shopkeepers, craftsmen, porters, builders, sellers. Work was organised by households and small
workshops, often with slaves and freedpeople. Associations (*collegia*) of trades provided sociability and funerals for their members.

## Spectacle {#spectacle}

The Circus Maximus, the great chariot-racing stadium, held on one estimate some 150,000 spectators; fans followed the racing factions with
passion. Gladiatorial combats (*munera*), animal hunts and public executions took place in amphitheatres, most famously the Flavian Amphitheatre
(Colosseum), opened in 80. Theatre included tragedies, comedies and above all mime and pantomime.

:::check dl-3
:::

Gladiators were mostly slaves, prisoners of war or condemned criminals, though some free men volunteered. Not every fight ended in death; skilled
gladiators were valuable. Excavations of a gladiators' cemetery at Ephesus have revealed their injuries and diet.

## Voices from the walls {#graffiti}

Thousands of painted notices and scratched graffiti survive at Pompeii: endorsements of candidates for local office by neighbours and trade groups,
notices of games, rental advertisements, greetings, insults, love messages and lines of poetry.

## Life at the frontier {#frontier}

## A day in Rome {#day}

The poet Martial, writing in the 80s and 90s AD, sketched the rhythm of a day in the capital for a reader who wanted to know when to send him a book of poems. The first two hours wore out clients at the morning greeting; the third set the lawyers shouting in the courts; business went on until the fifth; the sixth brought rest and the seventh an end to work; the eighth and ninth were for exercise and the baths; at the ninth hour one lay down to dinner.{{anc:Mart. 4.8}} It is an elite day, and a joke, but it captures the structure of urban life: business in the morning, leisure in the afternoon, dinner as the social climax.

Three lives illustrate how differently that day could be lived.

**A senator.** Before dawn his house fills with clients who have come for the *salutatio*, the morning greeting at which they pay respects, ask favours and receive small gifts. He then goes down to the Forum escorted by them, the size of his following a public measure of his standing. He may speak in the Senate, appear in court for a friend or meet officials. By the early afternoon he exercises and bathes; dinner, reclining with guests placed carefully by rank, may last for hours.

**A shopkeeper.** She lives with her family in a room behind or above a shop on the ground floor of an apartment block, selling bread, cloth or hot food from a counter open to the street. Her day follows the light and the market; her rent is paid to a landlord who may be a senator, and many shopkeepers were freed slaves running businesses for, or with money from, their former owners. Inscriptions record women as shopkeepers, innkeepers and craftworkers.

**A household slave.** He rises before the household to light fires, fetch water and prepare for the morning visitors. His work is defined by his place in a large household with dozens or hundreds of specialised slaves: door-keeper, accountant, hairdresser, litter-bearer. He may hope to be freed, and may save in his *peculium* to buy his freedom; he may also be beaten, sold or separated from his partner at his owner's will.

## Rents, collapses and fires {#poor}

For most of the city's inhabitants the main daily concerns were rent, food, fire and disease. Juvenal's third satire, written in the early second century, is a catalogue of urban misery: buildings propped up by their landlords and liable to collapse, fires that spread from the cheap upper floors, noise that makes sleep impossible, and the dangers of walking home at night.{{anc:Juv. 3}} Satire exaggerates, but the problems were real. Even Cicero, writing to his friend Atticus in 44 BC, mentions that two of the shops he owned had collapsed and the rest were cracking, and jokes that the tenants, and even the mice, had moved out.{{anc:Cic. Att. 14.9}}

Augustus' fire brigade of freedmen (*vigiles*) and limits on building height, and Nero's regulations after the fire of 64 requiring wider streets and fire-resistant materials, show that the state recognised these dangers, but the dense, tall, flammable city persisted.

## Prices and wages {#prices}

What did things cost? Evidence is scattered: graffiti, papyri, legal texts and, above all, Diocletian's Price Edict of 301, which set maximum prices and wages in its own inflated currency. It allows comparisons rather than absolute values. A farm labourer could be paid 25 denarii a day with his food; a carpenter 50; a teacher of reading 50 denarii a month for each boy; a teacher of grammar 200 a month per pupil. Such figures suggest a world in which skilled work earned modestly more than unskilled, and in which education beyond the elementary level was expensive.

:::claim interpretation
Wages in the Price Edict were maximums set by a government trying to stop inflation, not observed market rates, and they come from the very end of our period. Historians use them for relative values, for example the gap between unskilled and skilled labour, rather than as a picture of earnings in the early empire.
:::

## Health and death {#health}

Ancient cities were unhealthy: crowded, with contaminated water, poor sanitation in many areas, and endemic diseases such as malaria in Rome.
Doctors ranged from the learned Galen, physician to Marcus Aurelius, to untrained practitioners. Life expectancy was low and death familiar:
tombs lined the roads outside every town, and burial clubs ensured decent funerals for their members.

## Historiography {#historiography}

Daily life was long treated as "background" to political history. Social historians and archaeologists now place it at the centre, using houses,
skeletons, graffiti, papyri and everyday objects. Jerry Toner and Robert Knapp have emphasised the culture and experience of ordinary
Romans,{{ref:knapp-2011}} and Mary Beard's study of Pompeii shows how much of what we "know" rests on interpretation of fragmentary
evidence.{{ref:beard-2008}}

:::aside The "Pompeii premise"
Archaeologists warn against the "Pompeii premise", the assumption that a site is a snapshot of normal life frozen in time. Pompeii had been damaged
by an earthquake in the 60s and was still being repaired; many inhabitants fled with valuables before the end; and early excavators removed
objects without recording them. Even Pompeii must be interpreted.
:::
