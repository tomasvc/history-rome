## Historical context {#context}

The previous lesson treated Augustus' power through law, army and patronage. Romans also experienced the new order through what they
saw, heard and did: statues and temples, coins, festivals, poems, new laws on marriage, altars at street corners. Augustus himself
claimed to have found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble.{{anc:Suet. Aug. 28}} This lesson explores that cultural
programme and asks how far it was designed from the centre.

## A new visual language {#images}

Paul Zanker's *The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus* argued that the transition from Republic to Principate brought a
transformation in visual culture: the competitive, eclectic display of late Republican nobles gave way to a coherent set of images
centred on the emperor, drawn from classical Greek art and expressing a programme of religious renewal, moral restoration and
peace.{{ref:zanker-1988}}

The **Ara Pacis Augustae**, the Altar of Augustan Peace, was voted by the Senate in 13 BC on Augustus' return from Spain and Gaul and
dedicated in 9 BC. Its outer walls show a procession of the imperial family, priests and senators, including children, an unusual
feature that stresses dynasty and fertility. Panels show mythical scenes (Aeneas sacrificing; the twins with the wolf) and a female
figure surrounded by children, animals and plants, variously identified as Tellus (Earth), Italia, Pax or Venus. The lower walls are
covered with luxuriant acanthus scrolls full of small creatures: peace as abundance.

:::claim interpretation
Identifications of figures on the Ara Pacis are frequently debated, and the altar we see today was reassembled from fragments
excavated between the sixteenth and twentieth centuries, with substantial restoration. It was reconstructed in 1938 under Mussolini,
who exploited Augustus as a model; its modern setting is itself a historical document.
:::

The **Forum of Augustus**, dedicated in 2 BC, centred on the temple of Mars Ultor, Mars the Avenger, vowed at Philippi. Its porticoes
held statues of Aeneas and the kings of Alba Longa on one side and Romulus and the "great men" (*summi viri*) of the Republic on the
other, each with an inscription of his achievements. Augustus' own statue in a chariot stood at the centre. The message was that Roman
history culminated in him, and that he, too, should be measured by the standard of the great men. Even Republican heroes who had
fought against his family were included, as long as they had served Rome.

Coins carried these images across the empire, and portraits of Augustus, all following a few standard types and showing him
eternally youthful, were set up in cities everywhere. Over 200 portrait heads survive.

## Religion and the imperial cult {#religion}

Augustus presented himself as the restorer of Roman religion. He rebuilt temples, revived priesthoods and rituals that had fallen into
disuse, became a member of all the major colleges and, in 12 BC, pontifex maximus. In 17 BC he staged the Secular Games, a festival
marking the start of a new age (*saeculum*), for which Horace wrote a hymn sung by choirs of girls and boys.

He was also, increasingly, an object of worship. Julius Caesar was officially deified in 42 BC, making Octavian "son of a god" (*divi
filius*). In the Greek East, where cities had long honoured kings as gods, Augustus allowed provincial assemblies to build temples to
Roma and Augustus from 29 BC; Pergamum's was the first. In the West, an altar of Rome and Augustus was founded at Lugdunum in 12 BC as
a focus for the Gallic provinces. In Rome itself he was not worshipped as a living god, but in 7 BC the cult of the *Lares Augusti* and
the [[term:genius]] (guardian spirit) of Augustus was established in the city's 265 neighbourhoods, administered by local officials who
were mostly freedmen. When he died the Senate voted him a god, *divus Augustus*.{{ref:price-1984}}

:::claim interpretation
Historians no longer treat the imperial cult as mere flattery or political manipulation. Simon Price argued that for Greek cities it
was a way of making sense of a new kind of power in the religious language available to them, and of placing the emperor in a
relationship with their gods and their own community.
:::

## Morality, marriage and the family {#morality}

Augustus believed, or claimed, that the civil wars were divine punishment for moral decline, and that the upper classes were failing to
marry and raise children. In 18 BC the lex Julia on the marriage of the orders required men aged 25–60 and women aged 20–50 of
propertied rank to marry, penalised the unmarried and childless in inheritance, and gave privileges to parents of three or more
children (the *ius trium liberorum*). The lex Julia on adultery made adultery by a wife a public crime with a special court, and
required a husband who discovered it to divorce her or face prosecution himself. The lex Papia Poppaea of AD 9 adjusted the system;
Cassius Dio says equestrians had demonstrated against the laws, and both consuls who proposed it were unmarried.{{anc:Cass. Dio
56.1–10}}

:::check ar-2
:::

The laws struck at the heart of the family, where Roman fathers had traditionally ruled ([[term:patria-potestas]]). Their effect on
birth rates was probably small, but they made private life a matter of state concern. In 2 BC Augustus' own daughter Julia was exiled
for adultery; in AD 8 his granddaughter was exiled for the same offence. The poet Ovid, whose *Art of Love* mocked the moral
programme, was banished in the same year for what he called "a poem and a mistake".{{anc:Ov. Tr. 2.207}}

Women of the imperial house had a new public prominence. Livia and Octavia received statues, the right to manage their own property
without a guardian and, in Livia's case, the inviolability of a tribune. Livia's influence, presented by Tacitus as sinister, was
recognised in her lifetime and after: in his will Augustus adopted her into the Julian family as Julia Augusta.

## Literature and patronage {#literature}

The Augustan age was the high point of Latin literature. Virgil's *Aeneid* tells how Aeneas, fleeing Troy, came to Italy to found the
race that would produce Rome and the Julii; it prophesies Augustus' rule and shows Actium on Aeneas' shield. Horace's Odes celebrate
Augustus and the peace, as well as wine, friendship and the brevity of life. Livy's history of Rome from the foundation, 142 books
written over forty years, presented Roman history as a moral lesson. Propertius and Tibullus wrote love elegies; Ovid wrote love
poetry, the *Metamorphoses* and the *Fasti*, a poem on the Roman calendar.

Many of these poets belonged to the circle of Maecenas, a wealthy equestrian friend of Augustus, who gave Horace a farm and supported
Virgil. They wrote in a world of patronage, not of state commissions, and their work is far from uniformly celebratory: the *Aeneid*
dwells on loss and the human cost of Rome's destiny, and ends not with triumph but with Aeneas killing a suppliant enemy in fury.

## The city {#city}

Augustus and Agrippa transformed the physical city. Agrippa, as aedile in 33 BC, repaired the sewers and aqueducts and later built the
Aqua Virgo, the first public baths of Rome, and the original Pantheon. Augustus completed Caesar's Forum and the Senate house, built
the Mausoleum and the Forum of Augustus, and encouraged his associates to build. Administration followed: fourteen regions and 265
neighbourhoods (*vici*); a fire brigade (*vigiles*) of seven cohorts of freedmen; equestrian prefects for the grain supply and the
city; boards of senators for aqueducts and the Tiber banks. These changes created the institutions that would run the city of perhaps
a million people for centuries.

## Consequences and historiography {#historiography}

The Augustan cultural programme set the pattern for later emperors: monuments, images, cults and the patronage of letters became
standard instruments of rule. It also set a standard for later ages, from the Carolingians to Mussolini, who saw in Augustus a model
of order and renewal.

Modern scholarship has moved from treating Augustan culture as propaganda to seeing it as a dialogue. Zanker emphasised how images
were picked up and reproduced by many agents, not only by the regime.{{ref:zanker-1988}} Galinsky described an "Augustan culture"
of shared values that allowed for diversity and tension.{{ref:galinsky-1996}} Andrew Wallace-Hadrill described a "cultural revolution"
in which knowledge and authority, once dispersed among the aristocracy, became concentrated around the emperor.{{ref:wallace-hadrill-2008}}

## Working with the evidence {#evidence}

:::aside Reading an image as a historian
For any Roman image, ask: who paid for it and who made it? Where was it displayed and who could see it? What models does it echo? What
words accompanied it? Is the object we see today the original, a copy, or a reconstruction? The Prima Porta statue, for instance, was
found in a villa belonging to Livia and may be a marble copy of a bronze original, made after Augustus' death.
:::
